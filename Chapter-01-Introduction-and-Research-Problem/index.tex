\chapter{Introduction and Research Problem}
\label{ch:introduction}

Decentralized Finance (DeFi) has transformed how capital is allocated on public blockchains: lending, borrowing, and leveraged trading can now run without traditional intermediaries \cend{1}. Most credit-like transactions are still heavily collateralized, though, because protocols cannot draw on off-chain identity frameworks or existing credit data \cend{2}. Under pseudonymity, the central issue is how to allocate capital efficiently among unknown parties.

This work poses a specific research question about EndorseRank, an on-chain reputation metric derived from ERC-20 \texttt{approve}/\texttt{permit} events. On Arbitrum One, can EndorseRank be computed more cheaply than transfer-based Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP), does it capture endorsements more accurately, and does it depart from AWP in a way that can be verified? The core evaluation sets EndorseRank against AWP on the same wallets. Validation uses matching transfer and approval statistics plus a holdout period of future approvals, and follows the rank-correlation testing method described with AWP\cend{3}. In this paper, an on-chain reputation metric is a value derived from on-chain activity and interactions without any off-chain identity information \cend{4}, and EndorseRank is that value computed from approval edges.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:background]{Background: DeFi, collateralization, and pseudonymity}

Public blockchains make programmable settlement possible without a central authority \cend{5}. A smart contract can encode the terms of a deal \cend{6}, and a DeFi platform that uses such contracts clears trades on the blockchain instead of routing them through a custodian \cend{7}. Protocols then stack these contracts into decentralized markets for lending, automated market making and perpetual futures \cend{8}. On Arbitrum One and other L2 networks, activity is high enough for transfers, approvals, and protocol events to be inspected wallet by wallet.

Even with this visibility, DeFi credit markets remain inherently over-collateralized. Most protocols make a borrower or trader post more collateral than the position is worth, because the counterparty is a pseudonymous account whose owner has not been verified \cend{9,10}. Pseudonymity is a basic property of public ledgers. Anyone can create addresses at almost no cost, and one entity can own more than one wallet \cend{11}. Conventional credit ratings depend on personal histories that translate poorly to this setting \cend{2}, so protocols control risk through collateral reserves, liquidation mechanisms, and protocol rules instead of personalized reputation.

Collateralization protects protocols, but it uses capital inefficiently. Traders and borrowers have to tie up funds they could otherwise invest, and the platforms offer none of the under-collateralized products that traditional finance extends to people with a credit record. Researchers have therefore turned to on-chain signals as a proxy for off-chain identity: transfer-graph centrality \cend{4}, network risk propagation \cend{12}, prospect theory and blockchain-based credit scoring \cend{13}, and machine learning pipelines over lending protocols \cend{14}. Each of these lines of work assumes that public ledger data contains information usable for risk assessment without deanonymizing users.

Graph ranking algorithms appear well suited to this problem. PageRank and its variants rank nodes by recursive endorsement, so that a node's score depends on the endorsements it receives from other nodes \cend{15}. Earlier web trust algorithms such as EigenTrust \cend{16} and TrustRank \cend{17} apply similar ideas to peer-to-peer trust and to web spam. Do and Do\cend{4} use both PageRank and HodgeRank on a graph of transfers to judge how trustworthy Ethereum users are, and call the result a form of social credit. Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP), proposed by Do, Do, and Nguyen\cend{3}, is another transfer-graph ranking algorithm. It weights edges by transfer value and applies logistic time decay; its authors validate it by showing that it correlates with inbound transfer count and value, taken as proxies for economic importance. These studies establish transfer-graph reputation as a credible baseline, but they leave out explicit spending authorization, a separate on-chain action recorded as ERC-20 allowances \cend{18,19}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:motivation]{Motivation for on-chain reputation}

Four main factors drive the need for on-chain reputation research in leveraged DeFi.
\begin{itemize}
\item Capital efficiency: protocols are seeking ordinal ranking signals that could support tiered services (more frequent limit increases, lower fees, or higher priority in monitoring, for example), and perhaps even differentiated collateral requirements in future controlled experiments, while keeping settlement on chain \cend{1}.
\item Auditability: off-chain credit scores are opaque, but graph-based scores computed from public event data can be recomputed and audited, which would help to ease some of the concerns about black-box decentralized credit scoring \cend{2}.
\item Operational cost: a reputation system that must be updated often (before each leverage adjustment, for instance) cannot afford to aggregate historical transfers on every run.
\item Standardization: other markets use screening ratios to tell counterparties apart, but pseudonymous DeFi has no standard ordinal language for doing so at the wallet level. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} explores possible industry uses in which EndorseRank and AWP can provide infrastructure that lets protocols offer differentiated fees, utilization, and monitoring efficiency without replacing collateral.
\end{itemize}

Transfer-based AWP satisfies the first two criteria but is expensive on the third. Building $G_{\text{transfer}}$ means aggregating the user's transfer history, applying logistic time decay to those edges, and then running PageRank over the resulting dense edge set \cend{3}. Allowance graphs are a structurally different alternative. Whenever a user approves an ERC-20 token, the contract records how much of that token a spender may transfer \cend{18,19}. Keeping only the most recent allowance for each owner--spender pair yields a snapshot of current spending authorization, and because each new approval supersedes the older ones, no temporal decay is needed. Approvals are also rarer than transfers. On the same wallets, the endorsement graph $G_{\text{approve}}$ therefore has an order of magnitude fewer edges than the transfer graph $G_{\text{transfer}}$, and PageRank, at $O(|E|)$ per iteration, finishes proportionally faster (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}). The same solver runs in both cases, so the speedup is an artifact of the sparser graph that latest-allowance snapshots produce, not an algorithmic improvement. Claim~C1 treats it as an operational property of the representation.

Efficiency aside, allowances also mean something different. A transfer might reflect routing, market making, or a passive receipt, whereas an allowance is an active grant of permission to spend funds. Treating allowances as endorsement edges maps PageRank's citation analogy \cend{15} onto a trust relationship that protocols already depend on in token interactions. Whether this semantic difference produces different rankings, and if so whether those rankings correlate differently with authorization and transfer checks, remains open. We examine both questions empirically on Arbitrum One, using a matched sample of wallets with non-zero endorsement- and transfer-graph connectivity ($n=5{,}521$).\footnote{The matched sample consists of the wallets with non-zero connectivity in the endorsement and transfer subgraphs during the observation period. For the sensitivity of runtime to node count, we use an expanded sample of $N=99{,}080$ wallets.}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:research-problem]{Research problem}

Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) methods infer trust from transfer history through weighted sums and logistic time-decay \cend{3}. Their economic interpretation is rich, yet AWP (i) depends on costly aggregation of historical transfers, (ii) models a high-value wallet as one that receives many inbound transfers, and (iii) assigns no role to spending authorization. Our proposal, EndorseRank, takes the latest allowance of an owner--spender pair as the edge strength of the directed endorsement graph. The resulting graph is much sparser and needs no temporal decay, and the signal it encodes is different.

We pose the following research question: on an identical sample of Arbitrum One wallets, do EndorseRank's wallet rankings differ from AWP's in construct validity, in correlation with allowance and transfer checks, in predicting future endorsements over a time holdout, and in computational cost? EndorseRank versus AWP is the main comparison. A second question follows from the first. Because the two methods run random walks on two edge types across the same set of addresses, we ask whether the allowance and transfer edges can be mixed node by node with a fixed weight $\lambda$ to define a Coupled PageRank (C-PR), and whether any interior point of this family ranks wallets better than either end member.

We define the main terms here to prevent any misreading:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{DeFi (Decentralized Finance):} Financial services such as lending and borrowing, implemented as smart contracts on public blockchains and generally run without a central operator in charge of custody or settlement \cend{1}. Because DeFi protocols are composed through open interfaces, token movements can be observed on chain.
\item \emph{Smart contract:} Self-executing program code that runs on a blockchain and carries out an agreement without a trusted intermediary \cend{6}. In this paper, the allowance and transfer logs are emitted by smart contracts.
\item \emph{Wallet / Address:} A blockchain account that holds funds and interacts with smart contracts; it is the object whose reputation we want to rank \cend{7}. Each wallet is identified by its address, and no off-chain identity is assumed or recovered.
\item \emph{On-chain reputation score:} A number computed from on-chain data that expresses a wallet's relative position for financial activity under pseudonymity \cend{4}. Scores are ordinal and meant for ranking; their absolute scale has no meaning for credit limits.
\item \emph{PageRank:} A graph ranking algorithm that spreads influence through a directed graph with a damping factor \cend{15}. EndorseRank is PageRank on the latest-allowance endorsement graph, where each owner--spender pair's edge weight equals its current allowance value and no temporal decay is applied. Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) is PageRank on transfer graphs, with each edge weighted by transfer value times a logistic time-decay \cend{3}.
\item \emph{Coupled PageRank (C-PR):} One random walk that moves over both the allowance and the transfer layer. On leaving a wallet, the walk mixes the two row vectors with weights $\lambda$ and $1-\lambda$ and then renormalizes over the layers that wallet actually occupies. On a common node set, $\lambda=1$ recovers EndorseRank and $\lambda=0$ recovers AWP. Endorsement-seeded PageRank (S-PR) is the ablation: it keeps the transfer walk but restarts from the EndorseRank distribution, much as TrustRank \cend{17} restarts from a seed set.
\item \emph{ERC-20 allowance / Approve / Permit:} Contract methods that record how many of the owner's tokens a spender may spend on the owner's behalf \cend{18,19}. \texttt{Approve} is an ordinary on-chain call; EIP-2612 \texttt{permit} makes the same state change through a signature. For each owner--spender pair, EndorseRank uses the latest non-zero allowance snapshot.
\item \emph{Endorsement graph:} A directed graph whose edges denote an explicit delegation of spending authority, as opposed to an actual transfer of assets \cend{18}. In EndorseRank an edge $u \to v$ means that wallet $u$ has approved wallet or contract $v$ to spend tokens belonging to $u$; AWP on the transfer graph is the contrasting baseline \cend{3}.
\item \emph{Domain alignment:} The degree to which a reputation ranking agrees with an observable proxy ranking, measured with Spearman's $\rho$ \cend{20} and Kendall's $\tau$ \cend{21} and interpreted as construct validity in the sense of Cronbach and Meehl\cend{22}. Alignment is specific to a construct. A method could align closely with its own construct graph yet weakly with a different, orthogonal check.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:supplementary-extension]{Temporal holdout}

PageRank on $G_{\text{approve}}$ should agree with spender-side approval degree, because the score is a global smoothing of that degree. To test whether the score conveys information beyond the same-window graph, the scores are frozen at 28~February~2026 and compared with two labels that only become available in March--May~2026: new owner--spender approval pairs (the endorsement construct) and new transfer senders (the flow construct). Inbound spenders at $t_1$ make up the holdout cohort ($n=1{,}335$). Each PageRank is also compared with the raw degree it smooths at $t_1$, so the gain of the propagated score over the local count can be seen directly.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:research-objectives]{Research objectives}

The research has five objectives, one for each of RQ1--RQ5. Each is stated with the measurement used to operationalise it and with the criterion that Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} applies when judging whether the objective was met.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[O1.] Establish whether EndorseRank and AWP order the wallets of the same Arbitrum One cohort differently (RQ1). Measurement: Kendall $\tau$ between the cohort's EndorseRank and AWP scores ($n=5{,}521$), with a bootstrapped 95\% confidence interval. Criterion: the confidence interval contains neither $0$ nor $1$; $0$ would indicate uncorrelated orderings, and $1$ perfect agreement.
\item[O2.] Assess, on the same cohort, how well EndorseRank and AWP each measure their own construct (RQ2). Measurement: family-level Kendall $\tau$ with 95\% confidence intervals for every construct and method, plus paired contrasts $\Delta\tau$ between the two methods on a given construct. Criterion: on its own construct, each method's score must show a positive $\Delta\tau$ with a confidence interval that excludes $0$.
\item[O3.] Compare PageRank computation times for EndorseRank and AWP at the same number of vertices, and test the effect of node count on the larger vertex set (RQ3). Measurement: wall-clock solve time (mean and standard deviation of five timed runs after one warm-up run), memory use, iteration count, and $|V|$ and $|E|$ for each of the four configurations, all under a single protocol. Criterion: the solve-time ratio is reported only together with the ratio of $|E|$, so that any improvement can be traced to the sparser graph structure.
\item[O4.] Test whether scores obtained at $t_1$ can predict approvals at $t_2$ among the cohort's spenders (RQ4). Measurement: Kendall $\tau$, with a 95\% confidence interval, between the scores computed on 28~February~2026 and the counts of new approvals from March to May~2026, over the $n=1{,}335$ spenders who received funds within that window. The results are compared with those for AWP and with a paired contrast between EndorseRank and the $t_1$ in-degree. Criterion: EndorseRank's confidence interval excludes $0$, and the paired difference between EndorseRank and AWP is positive; the paired difference between EndorseRank and the $t_1$ in-degree is reported with its confidence interval and judged on that interval.
\item[O5.] Test whether a single PageRank step across both edge types does better than either single-layer method (RQ5). Measurement: C-PR at $\lambda=0.5$, with $\lambda\in\{0.25,0.75\}$ as a sensitivity test, and S-PR; both are evaluated on the same cohort and compared with EndorseRank and AWP through paired confidence intervals on $\Delta\tau$, for the holdout labels and for the same-window labels. Criterion, fixed before the experiment: on the holdout, C-PR performs as well as EndorseRank on new approval edges and better than AWP on new transfer edges; in the same window, it lies within the 95\% confidence interval of the best single-layer method for the transfer and allowance labels and beats both on Sybil stability.
\end{enumerate}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:research-questions]{Research questions}

The research addresses three main questions. H1: on the same wallet set, EndorseRank and AWP produce different wallet orderings. H2: EndorseRank and AWP fare differently when measured against specific construct checks (the allowance check versus the transfer check). H3: at the same value of $n$, EndorseRank is faster to compute than AWP. We address these hypotheses through the research questions below, which Table~\ref{tab:hrq-claim-map} summarises.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[RQ1.] On the same Arbitrum wallet set, do EndorseRank and AWP produce different wallet orderings?
\item[RQ2.] Is EndorseRank more strongly correlated than AWP with current allowance checks and with current transfer checks (using Spearman's $\rho$ and Kendall's $\tau$)?
\item[RQ3.] Is EndorseRank faster to compute than AWP on the same wallet set, as measured by time, memory, and iterations?
\item[RQ4.] Given scores computed at time $t_1$, can EndorseRank predict $t_2$ new owner--spender approvals to inbound spenders better than AWP can, and better than the allowance degree from the same time window?
\item[RQ5.] Does a coupled PageRank running on both the allowance and the transfer layer rank wallets better than any single-layer approach, out of sample as well as in sample?
\end{enumerate}

RQ1 asks whether the allowance graph and the transfer graph induce different centrality measures, using Kendall's $\tau$ between the score vectors of the methods. Under RQ2 we evaluate how well the different approaches correlate with our intended constructs (local transfer statistics and local allowance statistics). Since every own-construct proxy is computed from the same events that were used to build the graph, RQ2 should be regarded as an observation about construct validity; it does not amount to external validation. For RQ3, PageRank computation time, memory use, and iteration count are measured on the same wallet sets, together with node-count sensitivity as the wallet pool grows. Endorsements remain the construct in RQ4, a time-split test that predicts future new approvals, not trading profits. RQ4 replaces the trading-success comparison outlined in the original research proposal; that comparison was withdrawn because the outcome-native reference it used as a proxy was circular with our proposed proxies (see Section~\ref{sub:gf-pr}). With RQ5 the question moves from testing two alternative edge swaps to one of methodology. The coupled operator defined in Section~\ref{sub:coupled-graph} contains both single-layer methods as boundary conditions, and the rule for judging an intermediate point a success was fixed before any scores were computed (Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol}).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:literature-summary]{Summary of the literature review}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} reviews four main areas of literature. Below I summarise what each area has contributed to our approach and where it falls short. In graph ranking, PageRank \cend{15} ranks nodes by recursively propagated endorsements and is computed by power iteration, with a per-iteration cost linear in the number of edges \cend{23}. EigenTrust \cend{16} and TrustRank \cend{17} adapt the approach to trust in peer-to-peer systems and to web spam, respectively, adding normalization and seed sets that prevent inflation. This paper takes its solver and complexity arguments from these methods, although their edges are limited to links, ratings, or seeds. On-chain reputation is the second area. Do and Do\cend{4} apply PageRank and HodgeRank to Ethereum transaction graphs as a social credit metric, while Do, Do, and Nguyen\cend{3} introduce AWP, which weights transfer edges by value, applies a logistic decay over age, and validates the ranking against inbound transfer count and value. Lin et al.\cend{12} propagate account risk along the edges of the transaction network. Wu et al.\cend{24} show that for phishing detection, graph embeddings of transaction data outperform per-account features, and surveys of blockchain reputation systems \cend{25,26} find explicit ratings and transfer volume to be the most common trust indicators. None of these papers takes the ERC-20 allowance, the on-chain action that logs a deliberate delegation of spending rights \cend{18}, as its graph edge, and the AWP validation checks scores against proxies computed from the same transfer information used to build the graph. In DeFi risk and measurement, surveys of DeFi \cend{8} and of DeFi attacks \cend{27} treat behavioral counterparty risk as one level alongside smart contract, oracle, market, and governance risk. Wash trading \cend{28,29}, extractable value \cend{30}, scam tokens \cend{31}, and airdrop farming \cend{32} all show that much transfer activity is artificial, which motivates the search for a signal other than payment flow. The fourth area is measurement theory and inference. Construct validity \cend{22,33} separates what a score measures from external criteria, and bootstrap resampling \cend{34,35} yields distribution-free intervals for rank statistics. The resulting gap (Section~\ref{sec:research-gap}) is a same-cohort comparison of an allowance-edge ranking with the transfer-edge baseline, backed by confidence intervals, paired difference tests, a fixed timing protocol that reports edge counts, and a temporal holdout that separates same-window agreement from prediction.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:methodology-summary]{Summary of the research methodology}

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} applies a quantitative, comparative study design to public ledger data. Arbitrum One ERC-20 \texttt{Approval} and \texttt{Transfer} event logs from 1~December~2025--31~May~2026 are extracted from Google BigQuery. The matched wallet cohort ($n=5{,}521$) consists of the addresses with non-zero connectivity in both the most recent allowance graph $G_{\text{approve}}$ and the time-decayed transfer graph $G_{\text{transfer}}$. One power-iteration PageRank implementation scores both graphs ($d=0.85$, $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$, max 300 iterations). EndorseRank takes the sum of latest allowances as its edge weights, while AWP takes value-weighted, logistically decayed transfers as defined in the baseline paper\cend{3}. The coupled operator C-PR runs on the same solver, with the two edge lists interleaved per node ($\lambda=0.5$ primary). Rank concordance is measured with Spearman's $\rho$ and Kendall's $\tau$ against three contemporary proxy groups (transfer, allowance, Sybil-adjusted stability). For RQ4 and RQ5 it is also measured against new owner--spender approvals and new transfer senders in a three-month holdout period after the scores are frozen, alongside the raw $t_1$ degree baselines. Each coefficient is reported with a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval from 400 paired wallet resamples (seed 42), and pre-specified method comparisons are presented as paired mean intervals. Runtime, peak memory, iteration count, and the sizes of $V$ and $E$ are recorded under one measurement regime: an unrecorded initialization pass, then five recorded passes, with memoization across runs so that identical graphs return identical numbers in every table where they appear. Further sensitivity experiments vary the damping, restrict the data to the top tokens, and adopt an alternative definition of a wallet. All of the pipeline code, configuration files, and machine-readable summaries behind every table are version-controlled in a public GitHub repository (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}). The experiment uses only public data and involves no human subjects (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:contributions]{Contributions}

The paper makes four contributions to the study of on-chain reputation and DeFi risk. Both EndorseRank and AWP apply classic PageRank \cend{15} unchanged, each to a single edge type; the coupled operator is the only place in the pipeline where the random walk itself is modified. What is new, then, is an edge type, a same-cohort contemporaneous comparison, a temporal holdout experiment with degree baselines, and a pre-registered test of whether the two edge types interact.
\begin{itemize}
\item Latest-allowance edge construction (EndorseRank): an endorsement graph in which an edge $u \to v$ stands for the current ERC-20 \texttt{approve}/\texttt{permit} allowance that the spender $v$ holds from the owner $u$. Newer approvals override older ones, so the latest-allowance edge needs no temporal decay; it captures an explicit delegation of spending rights, not a history of payments. None of the reputation methods examined uses this edge (Section~\ref{sec:research-gap}).
\item Same-cohort contemporaneous comparison with statistical inference: EndorseRank and AWP are computed on one matched set of wallets, and their outputs are compared on identical transfer, allowance, and Sybil-stability proxies under the AWP validation protocol \cend{3}, with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals for every coefficient and paired approximate $t$-tests for the contrast estimators \cend{34}. Part of the agreement between the two methods is tautological. Because Claim~C1 follows directly from sparsity, runtime is reported alongside raw edge counts.
\item Temporal holdout experiment with degree baselines: for the spender cohort, scores obtained at time $t_1$ are tested against new owner--spender approvals and new transfer senders that occur at time $t_2$, alongside the raw count of $t_1$-degree edges over which PageRank smoothing averages. The design separates in-sample agreement from out-of-sample prediction, and the propagated PageRank score from its local degree. Together with the fully open, versioned evaluation pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}; Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}), this experiment provides the evidence behind Claims~C1--C5.
\item Coupled operator and its pre-registered evaluation: C-PR blends the allowance and transfer layers node by node with a fixed mixing weight, so that EndorseRank and AWP are its lower and upper bounds, respectively; S-PR is a seeded ablation. The performance metric, the choice of $\lambda$, and the holdout labels were all fixed before any experiment was run, and the result is reported as obtained (Claim~C5). On this dataset and cohort the intermediate values do not exceed the extremes. This outcome implies a bound on what any convex combination of the two edge types could achieve, and it shows that the allowance edge owes its advantage to how it propagates, not to its ability to seed rankings.
\end{itemize}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} presents the primary numerical results, including cross-method timing, within-family correlations, and runtime (Tables~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime} and~\ref{tab:alignment-family-ci}). They illustrate an alignment-efficiency trade-off, in that neither reputation method achieves the best score on both edge types.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:scope-limitations]{Scope and limitations}

The study covers Arbitrum One from 2025-12-01 to 2026-05-31. Its main sample is $n=5{,}521$ wallet addresses with non-zero endorsement- and transfer-graph connectivity, and Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime} reports the number of edges in each graph. Runtime sensitivity to node count is examined on a larger set of wallets ($N=99{,}080$), and I also report sensitivity analyses for damping, for top-token subgraphs, and for the wallet sample. Default labels in unsecured lending are outside the scope of this study. Constructs and ethics are defined in Chapters~\ref{ch:introduction} and~\ref{ch:methodology}. For the holdout claim I use the spender cohort ($n=1{,}335$). Extended baselines are left out and archived for future comparison (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). I make no claim that the results generalize to networks other than Arbitrum One or beyond my six-month study window. Edge weights can be influenced by token-decimal normalization, price oracles, and permit-versus-approve coverage. Adversarial manipulation of allowances or transfers could pose a threat, which I discuss in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}. My Sybil-adjusted proxies help reduce the risk of sybil attacks but do not eliminate it completely \cend{11}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:roadmap]{Research roadmap}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} surveys graph-based on-chain reputation metrics and ERC-20 allowance semantics. In the same chapter I develop the theoretical framework that informs the primary EndorseRank vs.\ AWP comparison, drawing on hyperlink propagation, domain alignment, and computational complexity.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} covers data sources and sampling, graph construction for EndorseRank and AWP, the contemporaneous construct checks and temporal holdout, computational benchmarks and robustness checks, and reproducibility standards and ethics. There I also define the matched wallet cohort, the spender holdout cohort, the expanded wallet pool I use for scaling, and the operational procedures used to produce the Chapter~\ref{ch:results} tables.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:results} I report the empirical results: cohort characteristics, runtime and scaling benchmarks (Claim~C1), robustness checks, allowance and transfer alignment (Claims~C2--C3), rank divergence (H1/RQ1), and the same-window placement of the coupled operator together with the temporal holdout (RQ4, RQ5).
