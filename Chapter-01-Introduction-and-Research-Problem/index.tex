\chapter{Introduction and Research Problem}
\label{ch:introduction}

Decentralized Finance (DeFi) has transformed how capital is allocated on public blockchains: lending, borrowing, and leveraged trading can now run without traditional intermediaries \cend{1}. Most credit-like transactions are still heavily collateralized, though, because protocols cannot draw on off-chain identity frameworks or existing credit data \cend{2}. Under pseudonymity, the central issue is how to allocate capital efficiently among unknown parties.

This thesis asks whether bringing ERC-20 allowance edges into PageRank improves on-chain wallet reputation over a transfer-only baseline. The allowance edges come from the \texttt{Approval} events that \texttt{approve} and \texttt{permit} emit, and EndorseRank is PageRank computed on them. The baseline is the transfer-based Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) of Do, Do and Nguyen\cend{3}, run on the same Arbitrum One wallets. We split ``improves'' into four questions: whether the score measures something the baseline misses, whether it is cheaper to compute, whether it anticipates later behavior better, and whether one walk over both edge types does better than the transfer walk alone. Validation follows the rank-correlation method used with AWP\cend{3}, with paired contrasts, raw-degree baselines and temporal holdouts added. In this thesis, an on-chain reputation metric is a value derived from on-chain activity and interactions without any off-chain identity information \cend{4}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:background]{Background: DeFi, collateralization, and pseudonymity}

Public blockchains make programmable settlement possible without a central authority \cend{5}. A smart contract can encode the terms of a deal \cend{6}, and a DeFi platform that uses such contracts clears trades on the blockchain instead of routing them through a custodian \cend{7}. Protocols then stack these contracts into decentralized markets for lending, automated market making and perpetual futures \cend{8}. On Arbitrum One and other L2 networks, fees are low enough that ordinary users transact often, and every transfer, approval and protocol event can be inspected wallet by wallet.

Even with this visibility, DeFi credit markets remain inherently over-collateralized. Most protocols make a borrower or trader post more collateral than the position is worth, because the counterparty is a pseudonymous account whose owner has not been verified \cend{9,10}. Pseudonymity is a basic property of public ledgers. Anyone can create addresses at almost no cost, and one entity can own more than one wallet \cend{11}. Conventional credit ratings depend on personal histories that translate poorly to this setting \cend{2}, so protocols control risk through collateral reserves, liquidation mechanisms, and protocol rules instead of personalized reputation.

Collateralization protects protocols, but it uses capital inefficiently. Traders and borrowers have to tie up funds they could otherwise invest, and the platforms offer none of the under-collateralized products that traditional finance extends to people with a credit record. Researchers have therefore turned to on-chain signals as a substitute for off-chain credit histories: transfer-graph centrality \cend{4}, network risk propagation \cend{12}, prospect theory and blockchain-based credit scoring \cend{13}, and machine learning pipelines over lending protocols \cend{14}. Each of these lines of work assumes that public ledger data contains information usable for risk assessment without deanonymizing users.

Graph ranking algorithms appear well suited to this problem. PageRank and its variants rank nodes by recursive endorsement, so that a node's score depends on the endorsements it receives from other nodes \cend{15}. Earlier web trust algorithms such as EigenTrust \cend{16} and TrustRank \cend{17} apply similar ideas to peer-to-peer trust and to web spam. Do and Do\cend{4} use both PageRank and HodgeRank on a graph of transfers to judge how trustworthy Ethereum users are, and call the result a form of social credit. Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP), proposed by Do, Do, and Nguyen\cend{3}, is another transfer-graph ranking algorithm. It weights edges by transfer value and applies logistic time decay; its authors validate it by showing that it correlates with inbound transfer count and value, taken as proxies for economic importance. These studies establish transfer-graph reputation as a credible baseline, but they leave out explicit spending authorization, a separate on-chain action recorded as ERC-20 allowances \cend{18,19}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:motivation]{Motivation for on-chain reputation}

Four main factors drive the need for on-chain reputation research in leveraged DeFi.
\begin{itemize}
\item Capital efficiency: protocols are seeking ordinal ranking signals that could support tiered services (more frequent limit increases, lower fees, or higher priority in monitoring, for example), and perhaps even differentiated collateral requirements in future controlled experiments, while keeping settlement on chain \cend{1}.
\item Auditability: off-chain credit scores are opaque, but graph-based scores computed from public event data can be recomputed and audited, which would help to ease some of the concerns about black-box decentralized credit scoring \cend{2}.
\item Operational cost: a score that is refreshed often, before each leverage adjustment for instance, should be cheap to recompute.
\item Standardization: other markets use screening ratios to tell counterparties apart, but pseudonymous DeFi has no standard ordinal language for doing so at the wallet level. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses where the scores studied here could serve that role and where they cannot.
\end{itemize}

Transfer-based AWP is public and auditable, but its graph grows with every payment. Building $G_{\text{transfer}}$ means aggregating the transfer history, applying logistic time decay to those edges, and running PageRank over the resulting edge set \cend{3}. Allowance graphs are a structurally different alternative. Whenever a user approves an ERC-20 token, the contract records how much of that token a spender may transfer \cend{18,19}. Keeping only the most recent approval of each owner--spender pair in the observation window gives one edge per pair, and because each new approval supersedes the older ones, no temporal decay is needed. Approvals are also rarer than transfers. On the same wallets, the endorsement graph $G_{\text{approve}}$ therefore has an order of magnitude fewer edges than the transfer graph $G_{\text{transfer}}$, and the PageRank solve, whose cost grows linearly with $|E|$, finishes proportionally faster (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}). The same solver runs in both cases, so the speedup comes from the sparser graph and is not an algorithmic improvement. Claim~C1 treats it as an operational property of the representation; at this scale both solves take seconds.

Efficiency aside, allowances also mean something different. A transfer might reflect routing, market making, or a passive receipt, whereas an allowance is an active grant of permission to spend funds. Treating allowances as endorsement edges maps PageRank's citation analogy \cend{15} onto a trust relationship that protocols already depend on in token interactions. Whether this semantic difference produces different rankings, and if so whether those rankings correlate differently with authorization and transfer checks, remains open. We examine both questions empirically on Arbitrum One, using a matched sample of $n=5{,}521$ GMX~V2 trading wallets.\footnote{The sample is matched in the sense that every method scores the same wallets. It consists of the GMX~V2 wallets on Arbitrum One with at least three closes between 1~December~2025 and 31~May~2026; a close is one GMX \texttt{PositionDecrease} event, so partial closes and liquidations count. The list is fixed from GMX data before any approval or transfer is extracted, and each method's graph keeps every edge with at least one endpoint in the sample. Runtime is also measured on subsamples drawn from a pool of $N=99{,}080$ addresses; the pool adds no edges, so these subsamples are subgraphs of the cohort graph.}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:research-problem]{Research problem}

Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) methods infer trust from transfer history through weighted sums and logistic time-decay \cend{3}. Their economic interpretation is rich, yet AWP (i) depends on costly aggregation of historical transfers, (ii) models a high-value wallet as one that receives many inbound transfers, and (iii) assigns no role to spending authorization. Our proposal, EndorseRank, takes the latest allowance of an owner--spender pair as the edge strength of the directed endorsement graph. The resulting graph is much sparser and needs no temporal decay, and the signal it encodes is different. It also ranks a different side of each relationship. An allowance points from the owner to the spender, so EndorseRank scores the spenders that receive approvals, such as routers and vaults, and gives every owner that receives none the same value.

The research question follows. On an identical sample of Arbitrum One wallets, do allowance edges give a PageRank reputation score that differs from AWP in what it measures, in its correlation with allowance and transfer checks, in how well it predicts future endorsements over a time holdout, and in computational cost; and does a walk that mixes the two edge types improve on the transfer walk? EndorseRank against AWP is the first comparison. The second follows from it. Each method runs the same walk on its own edge type over the same addresses, so the allowance and transfer edges can be mixed node by node with a fixed weight $\lambda$, which defines a Coupled PageRank (C-PR). We ask whether that mixture improves on AWP, the transfer-only end member, and whether any interior point of the family ranks wallets better than both end members. The mixture is worth testing because of the unit of analysis: most traders are owners that EndorseRank cannot tell apart, and a single walk over both layers could keep the transfer layer's coverage of them while carrying the allowance signal for spenders.

We define the main terms here to prevent any misreading:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{DeFi (Decentralized Finance):} Financial services such as lending and borrowing, implemented as smart contracts on public blockchains and generally run without a central operator in charge of custody or settlement \cend{1}. Because DeFi protocols are composed through open interfaces, token movements can be observed on chain.
\item \emph{Smart contract:} Self-executing program code that runs on a blockchain and carries out an agreement without a trusted intermediary \cend{6}. In this thesis, the allowance and transfer logs are emitted by smart contracts.
\item \emph{Wallet / Address:} A blockchain account that holds funds and interacts with smart contracts; it is the object whose reputation we want to rank \cend{7}. Each wallet is identified by its address, and no off-chain identity is assumed or recovered.
\item \emph{On-chain reputation score:} A number computed from on-chain data that expresses a wallet's relative position for financial activity under pseudonymity \cend{4}. Scores are ordinal and meant for ranking; their absolute scale has no meaning for credit limits.
\item \emph{PageRank:} A graph ranking algorithm that spreads influence through a directed graph with a damping factor \cend{15}. EndorseRank is PageRank on the latest-allowance endorsement graph, where each owner--spender pair's edge weight is the value of its latest approval in the observation window and no temporal decay is applied. Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) is PageRank on transfer graphs, with each edge weighted by transfer value times a logistic time-decay \cend{3}.
\item \emph{Coupled PageRank (C-PR):} One random walk that moves over both the allowance and the transfer layer. On leaving a wallet, the walk mixes the two row vectors with weights $\lambda$ and $1-\lambda$ and then renormalizes over the layers that wallet actually occupies. On a common node set, $\lambda=1$ recovers EndorseRank and $\lambda=0$ recovers AWP. Endorsement-seeded PageRank (S-PR) is the ablation: it keeps the transfer walk but restarts from the EndorseRank distribution, much as TrustRank \cend{17} restarts from a seed set.
\item \emph{ERC-20 allowance / Approve / Permit:} Contract methods that record how many of the owner's tokens a spender may spend on the owner's behalf \cend{18,19}. \texttt{Approve} is an ordinary on-chain call; EIP-2612 \texttt{permit} makes the same state change through a signature. For each owner--spender pair, EndorseRank uses the latest non-zero allowance snapshot.
\item \emph{Endorsement graph:} A directed graph whose edges denote an explicit delegation of spending authority, as opposed to an actual transfer of assets \cend{18}. In EndorseRank an edge $u \to v$ means that wallet $u$ has approved wallet or contract $v$ to spend tokens belonging to $u$; AWP on the transfer graph is the contrasting baseline \cend{3}.
\item \emph{Domain alignment:} The degree to which a reputation ranking agrees with an observable proxy ranking, measured with Spearman's $\rho$ \cend{20} and Kendall's $\tau$ \cend{21} and interpreted as construct validity in the sense of Cronbach and Meehl\cend{22}. Alignment is specific to a construct. A method could align closely with its own construct graph yet weakly with a different, orthogonal check.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:supplementary-extension]{Temporal holdout}

The contemporaneous allowance Kendall $\tau$ (Table~\ref{tab:alignment-allowance}) is an intended-construct check. PageRank on $G_{\text{approve}}$ should agree with spender-side approval degree, because the score is a global smoothing of that degree. To test whether the score conveys information beyond the same-window graph, the scores are frozen at 28~February~2026 and compared with two labels that only become available in March--May~2026: new owner--spender approval pairs (the endorsement construct) and new transfer senders (the flow construct). The holdout cohort ($n=1{,}335$) consists of every spender that holds at least one positive latest allowance at $t_1$ (28~February~2026, 23:59:59~UTC) in the extracted approval data. Each PageRank is also compared with the raw degree it smooths at $t_1$, so the gain of the propagated score over the local count can be seen directly.

A second window was registered on 28~September~2026, after the results of this spring holdout were known. Its scores are frozen on 31~May~2026 and its labels are counted from June to August~2026, months whose logs had not been extracted when the registration was committed. It repeats the two spender labels and adds one for the matched traders: the share of their GMX closes that did not end in liquidation (Section~\ref{sub:fresh-holdout}).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:research-objectives]{Research objectives}

The research has five objectives, one for each of RQ1--RQ5. Each is stated with the measurement used to operationalize it and with the criterion that Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} applies when judging whether the objective was met.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[O1.] Establish whether EndorseRank and AWP order the wallets of the same Arbitrum One cohort differently (RQ1). Measurement: Kendall $\tau$ between the cohort's EndorseRank and AWP scores ($n=5{,}521$), with a bootstrapped 95\% confidence interval. Criterion: the confidence interval contains neither $0$ nor $1$; $0$ would indicate uncorrelated orderings, and $1$ perfect agreement.
\item[O2.] Assess, on the same cohort, how well EndorseRank and AWP each measure their own construct (RQ2). Measurement: family-level Kendall $\tau$ with 95\% confidence intervals for every construct and method, plus paired contrasts $\Delta\tau$ between the two methods on a given construct. Criterion: on its own construct, each method's score must show a positive $\Delta\tau$ with a confidence interval that excludes $0$.
\item[O3.] Compare PageRank computation times for EndorseRank and AWP at the same number of vertices, and test the effect of node count on the larger vertex set (RQ3). Measurement: wall-clock solve time (mean and standard deviation of five timed runs after one warm-up run), memory use, iteration count, and $|V|$ and $|E|$ for each of the four configurations, all under a single protocol. Criterion: the solve-time ratio is reported only together with the ratio of $|E|$, so that any improvement can be traced to the sparser graph structure.
\item[O4.] Test whether scores obtained at $t_1$ can predict approvals at $t_2$ among the cohort's spenders (RQ4). Measurement: Kendall $\tau$, with a 95\% confidence interval, between the scores computed on 28~February~2026 and the counts of new approvals from March to May~2026, over the $n=1{,}335$ spenders that hold at least one positive latest allowance at $t_1$. The results are compared with those for AWP and with a paired contrast between EndorseRank and the $t_1$ in-approve degree. Criterion: EndorseRank's confidence interval excludes $0$, and the paired difference between EndorseRank and AWP is positive; the paired difference between EndorseRank and the $t_1$ in-approve degree is reported with its confidence interval and judged on that interval.
\item[O5.] Test whether a single PageRank walk across both edge types improves on AWP, and whether it does better than both single-layer methods (RQ5). Measurement: C-PR at $\lambda=0.5$, with $\lambda\in\{0.25,0.75\}$ as a sensitivity test, and S-PR; both are evaluated on the same cohorts and compared with EndorseRank and AWP through paired confidence intervals on $\Delta\tau$, for the holdout labels and for the same-window labels. Criteria, each fixed before the data it is judged on: (a) for beating both single layers, the rule of 14~September~2026: on the spring holdout, C-PR performs as well as EndorseRank on new approval edges and better than AWP on new transfer edges, and in the same window it lies within the 95\% confidence interval of the best single-layer method for the transfer and allowance labels and beats both on Sybil stability; (b) for improving on AWP, the rule registered on 28~September~2026: on the June--August window, C-PR beats AWP on new approval pairs and is not worse than AWP by more than 0.02 on new transfer senders or on the liquidation-free close rate of the traders.
\end{enumerate}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:research-questions]{Research questions}

Three hypotheses go with the first three research questions. H1: on the same wallet set, EndorseRank and AWP produce different wallet orderings. H2: EndorseRank and AWP fare differently when measured against specific construct checks (the allowance check versus the transfer check). H3: at the same value of $n$, EndorseRank is faster to compute than AWP. RQ4 and RQ5 carry no hypothesis of this kind; contrasts and rules fixed before the relevant data answer them (Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol}). Table~\ref{tab:hrq-claim-map} summarizes the questions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[RQ1.] On the same Arbitrum wallet set, do EndorseRank and AWP produce different wallet orderings?
\item[RQ2.] Which of EndorseRank and AWP correlates more strongly with current allowance checks, and which with current transfer checks (Spearman's $\rho$ and Kendall's $\tau$)?
\item[RQ3.] Is EndorseRank faster to compute than AWP on the same wallet set, as measured by time, memory, and iterations?
\item[RQ4.] Given scores computed at time $t_1$, can EndorseRank predict $t_2$ new owner--spender approvals to inbound spenders better than AWP can, and better than the spender's allowance degree at $t_1$?
\item[RQ5.] Does a coupled PageRank running on both the allowance and the transfer layer improve on the transfer-only AWP, and does it rank wallets better than both single-layer methods, out of sample as well as in sample?
\end{enumerate}

RQ1 asks whether the allowance graph and the transfer graph induce different centrality measures, using Kendall's $\tau$ between the score vectors of the methods. Under RQ2 we evaluate how well the different approaches correlate with our intended constructs (local transfer statistics and local allowance statistics). Since every own-construct proxy is computed from the same events that were used to build the graph, RQ2 should be regarded as an observation about construct validity; it does not amount to external validation. For RQ3, PageRank computation time, memory use, and iteration count are measured on the same wallet sets, together with node-count sensitivity as the wallet pool grows. Endorsements remain the construct in RQ4, a time-split test that predicts future new approvals, with new transfer senders as a second label, and not trading profits. RQ4 replaces the trading-success validation of the research proposal, whose outcome-based reference was circular (Section~\ref{sub:gf-pr}); trading outcomes return out of window in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. With RQ5 the question moves from comparing two edge types to combining them. The coupled operator defined in Section~\ref{sub:coupled-graph} contains both single-layer methods as boundary conditions. The rule for beating both of them was fixed in the versioned configuration before any coupled score was computed (Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol}). The rule for improving on AWP alone came later, after the spring labels had been seen, so it is judged only on the June--August window registered on 28~September~2026 (Section~\ref{sub:fresh-holdout}).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:literature-summary]{Summary of the literature review}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} reviews four main areas of literature. Below we summarize what each area has contributed to our approach and where it falls short. In graph ranking, PageRank \cend{15} ranks nodes by recursively propagated endorsements and is computed by power iteration, with a per-iteration cost linear in the number of edges \cend{23}. EigenTrust \cend{16} and TrustRank \cend{17} adapt the approach to trust in peer-to-peer systems and to web spam, respectively, adding normalization and seed sets that prevent inflation. This thesis takes its solver and complexity arguments from these methods, although their edges are limited to links, ratings, or seeds. On-chain reputation is the second area. Do and Do\cend{4} apply PageRank and HodgeRank to Ethereum transaction graphs as a social credit metric, while Do, Do, and Nguyen\cend{3} introduce AWP, which weights transfer edges by value, applies a logistic decay over age, and validates the ranking against inbound transfer count and value. Lin et al.\cend{12} propagate account risk along the edges of the transaction network. Wu et al.\cend{24} show that for phishing detection, graph embeddings of transaction data outperform per-account features, and surveys of blockchain reputation systems \cend{25,26} find explicit ratings and transfer volume to be the most common trust indicators. None of these papers takes the ERC-20 allowance, the on-chain action that logs a deliberate delegation of spending rights \cend{18}, as its graph edge, and the AWP validation checks scores against proxies computed from the same transfer information used to build the graph. In DeFi risk and measurement, surveys of DeFi \cend{8} and of DeFi attacks \cend{27} treat behavioral counterparty risk as one level alongside smart contract, oracle, market, and governance risk. Wash trading \cend{28,29}, extractable value \cend{30}, scam tokens \cend{31}, and airdrop farming \cend{32} all show that much transfer activity is artificial, which motivates the search for a signal other than payment flow. The fourth area is measurement theory and inference. Construct validity \cend{22,33} separates what a score measures from external criteria, and bootstrap resampling \cend{34,35} yields distribution-free intervals for rank statistics. The resulting gap (Section~\ref{sec:research-gap}) is a same-cohort comparison of an allowance-edge ranking with the transfer-edge baseline, backed by confidence intervals, paired difference tests, a fixed timing protocol that reports edge counts, and a temporal holdout that separates same-window agreement from prediction.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:methodology-summary]{Summary of the research methodology}

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} applies a quantitative, comparative study design to public ledger data. Arbitrum One ERC-20 \texttt{Approval} and \texttt{Transfer} event logs from 1~December~2025--31~May~2026 are extracted from Google BigQuery. The matched wallet cohort ($n=5{,}521$) consists of the GMX~V2 wallets with at least three closes in that window, and the most recent allowance graph $G_{\text{approve}}$ and the time-decayed transfer graph $G_{\text{transfer}}$ each keep every edge with at least one endpoint in the cohort. One power-iteration PageRank implementation scores both graphs ($d=0.85$, $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$, max 300 iterations). EndorseRank takes the sum of latest allowances as its edge weights, while AWP takes value-weighted, logistically decayed transfers as defined in the baseline paper\cend{3}. The coupled operator C-PR runs on the same solver, with the transition rows of the two layers mixed node by node ($\lambda=0.5$ primary). Rank concordance is measured with Spearman's $\rho$ and Kendall's $\tau$ against three contemporary proxy groups (transfer, allowance, Sybil-adjusted stability). For RQ4 and RQ5 it is also measured against new owner--spender approvals and new transfer senders in a three-month holdout period after the scores are frozen, alongside the raw $t_1$ degree baselines. A registered second window freezes the scores on 31~May~2026 and counts the same spender labels, plus the liquidation-free close rate of the matched traders, from June to August~2026. Each coefficient is reported with a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval from 400 paired wallet resamples (seed 42), and pre-specified method comparisons are presented as paired mean intervals. Runtime, peak memory, iteration count, and the sizes of $V$ and $E$ are recorded under one measurement regime: an unrecorded initialization pass, then five recorded passes, and each distinct graph is timed once per session, so the same graph shows the same numbers in every table where it appears. Further sensitivity experiments vary the damping, restrict the data to the top tokens, and change the evaluation sample. All of the pipeline code, configuration files, and machine-readable summaries behind every table are version-controlled in a public GitHub repository (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}). The experiment uses only public data and involves no human subjects (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:contributions]{Contributions}

The thesis makes four contributions to the study of on-chain reputation and DeFi risk. Both EndorseRank and AWP apply classic PageRank \cend{15} unchanged, each to a single edge type; the coupled operator is the only place in the pipeline where the random walk itself is modified. What is new, then, is an edge type, a same-cohort contemporaneous comparison, a temporal holdout experiment with degree baselines, and a two-stage registered test of whether the two edge types interact.
\begin{itemize}
\item Latest-allowance edge construction (EndorseRank): an endorsement graph in which an edge $u \to v$ stands for the latest in-window ERC-20 allowance, set by \texttt{approve} or \texttt{permit}, that the spender $v$ holds from the owner $u$. Newer approvals override older ones, so the latest-allowance edge needs no temporal decay; it captures an explicit delegation of spending rights, not a history of payments. None of the reputation methods examined uses this edge (Section~\ref{sec:research-gap}).
\item Same-cohort contemporaneous comparison with statistical inference: EndorseRank and AWP are computed on one matched set of wallets, and their outputs are compared on identical transfer, allowance, and Sybil-stability proxies under the AWP validation protocol \cend{3}, with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals for every coefficient and paired bootstrap intervals for the contrasts \cend{34}. Part of each method's agreement with its own proxies is built in, because those proxies come from the same events as its graph. Because Claim~C1 follows directly from sparsity, runtime is reported alongside raw edge counts.
\item Temporal holdout experiment with degree baselines: for the spender cohort, scores obtained at time $t_1$ are tested against new owner--spender approvals and new transfer senders that occur at time $t_2$, alongside the raw count of $t_1$-degree edges over which PageRank smoothing averages. The design separates in-sample agreement from out-of-sample prediction, and the propagated PageRank score from its local degree. Together with the fully open, versioned evaluation pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}; Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}), this experiment provides the out-of-window evidence behind Claims~C4 and~C5.
\item Coupled operator and its registered evaluation: C-PR blends the allowance and transfer layers node by node with a fixed mixing weight $\lambda$; on a shared node set, $\lambda=1$ gives EndorseRank and $\lambda=0$ gives AWP. S-PR is a seeded ablation. The evaluation has two stages. The first rule, fixed with $\lambda$ on 14~September~2026 before any coupled score was computed, asked C-PR to beat both single layers, and none of the values tested ($\lambda=0.25$, $0.5$ and $0.75$) does so (Claim~C5). Against AWP alone, an exploratory comparison on the spring holdout shows a large gain on new approvals and no detectable loss on new senders. That comparison was made after the labels were known, so it is repeated under a second rule on a window registered before its logs were extracted: \freshpending{outcome of the registered replication}. The ablation adds one more result: the allowance signal survives when allowance edges carry the walk and is lost when they only decide where the walk restarts.
\end{itemize}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} presents the numerical results. In the same window neither PageRank is best on both constructs: EndorseRank is the cheaper score and the better aligned with authorization, and AWP the better aligned with transfer statistics. Out of window EndorseRank ranks both labels better than AWP, and the raw degree counts rank them better still.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:scope-limitations]{Scope and limitations}

The study covers Arbitrum One from 2025-12-01 to 2026-05-31. Its main sample is the $n=5{,}521$ GMX~V2 wallets with at least three closes in that period, and Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime} reports the number of edges in each graph. Runtime is also measured on subgraphs of the cohort graph drawn through a larger address pool ($N=99{,}080$) that adds no edges, and we report sensitivity analyses for damping, for top-token subgraphs, and for the wallet sample. Default labels in unsecured lending are outside the scope of this study. Constructs and ethics are defined in Chapters~\ref{ch:introduction} and~\ref{ch:methodology}. For the holdout claim we use the spender cohort ($n=1{,}335$). EndorseRank scores a wallet by the approvals it receives, so it separates spenders and not the owners who grant approvals; most of the matched traders are owners, which is why the holdout is run on spenders. The registered June--August window adds a trader label (Section~\ref{sub:fresh-holdout}). Extended baselines are left out and archived for future comparison (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-archive}). We make no claim that the results generalize to networks other than Arbitrum One or beyond the months studied. Edge weights are raw token base units, with no decimal or price normalization, so unlimited approvals decide the rows of many owners (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}). Adversarial manipulation of allowances or transfers is a real threat. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} shows that a closed farm of Sybil addresses can inflate EndorseRank cheaply; the Sybil-adjusted proxies describe some manipulation patterns but do not protect the score against it \cend{11}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:roadmap]{Research roadmap}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} surveys graph-based on-chain reputation metrics and ERC-20 allowance semantics. In the same chapter we develop the theoretical framework that informs the primary EndorseRank vs.\ AWP comparison, drawing on hyperlink propagation, domain alignment, and computational complexity.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} covers data sources and sampling, graph construction for EndorseRank and AWP, the contemporaneous construct checks and temporal holdout, computational benchmarks and robustness checks, and reproducibility standards and ethics. There we also define the matched wallet cohort, the spender holdout cohort, the expanded wallet pool used for scaling, and the operational procedures used to produce the Chapter~\ref{ch:results} tables.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:results} we report the empirical results: cohort characteristics, runtime and scaling benchmarks (Claim~C1), allowance and transfer alignment (Claims~C2--C3), rank divergence (H1/RQ1), robustness checks, the same-window placement of the coupled operator, and the temporal holdouts (RQ4, RQ5), among them the registered June--August replication. The primary claims C1--C5 are stated there explicitly.

Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets the findings question by question and works out their theoretical and practical implications. It also formulates a cost model for Sybil attacks on allowance graphs, discusses regulatory and ethical considerations, and weighs threats to validity and limitations.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} we summarize the contribution to knowledge and answer RQ1--RQ5 in turn. We close with avenues for future work, among them cross-protocol validation, adversarial robustness, learned coupling weights, and live oracle integration.

The review in Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} opens with the blockchain and DeFi primitives that the allowance construct uses and ends with the research gap that this study addresses.
