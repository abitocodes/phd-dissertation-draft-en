\begin{abstract}
Blockchain reputation scores usually rank addresses by token payments, yet the same addresses are linked by allowances, through which an owner authorizes a spender to move its tokens. We treat payments and authorizations as two layers of one network and test whether a random walk mixing them at every node ranks smart contracts better than a walk over either layer, using 33 months of Arbitrum One events around contracts approved by three or more owners. Two decision rules were committed to the authors' public repository before this study extracted their evaluation logs: the analysis plan ten seconds before the first query, the second rule eight seconds before its July to September 2026 window was queried by code that checks the committed files. Neither rule was met. In the pre-specified analysis the coupled walk ranked contracts that later gained approvals better than the payment walk (rank-correlation difference 0.101, 95\% interval 0.092 to 0.111), but ranked later new payers worse by more than the 0.01 margin ($-$0.025, interval $-$0.029 to $-$0.021) and did not stay within the margin on the liquidation-free trading of contract traders. In the first window the authorization walk ranked later approvals worse than the raw count of approving owners ($-$0.025, interval $-$0.030 to $-$0.019). A revision to US-dollar amounts over five tokens, made after these results were known, reached the same verdicts. In a post hoc model, 13 colluding addresses approving one target would lift its authorization score above nine tenths of the cohort (22 after the revision).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{L}{ending} and margin protocols in decentralized finance (DeFi) deal with counterparties and integrations that are known only by an address \cite{schar2021defi}. They rely on over-collateralization and automatic liquidation in place of credit histories \cite{gudgeon2020crisis,werner2022sok}, and a line of research tries to recover some of the missing information from the public ledger. Most of it ranks addresses on a graph of token transfers. Do and Do read PageRank and HodgeRank on Ethereum transactions as a measure of social credit \cite{do2023hodgerank}; Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) of Do, Do and Nguyen weights every transfer by its value and age and restarts the walk at recently active senders \cite{do2023awp}; RiskProp propagates account risk through the transaction network \cite{lin2025riskprop}. Surveys of blockchain reputation systems find the same reliance on payment volume or on explicit ratings \cite{bellini2020trust}. Anyone can recompute such scores from public events, which answers part of the worry that decentralized credit scoring turns into a black box \cite{packin2024credit}.

A transfer records a payment. The ledger also records a second relation among the same addresses. Under the ERC-20 (Ethereum Request for Comments 20) token interface an owner calls \texttt{approve} to let a spender draw up to a stated amount of its tokens, and the \texttt{permit} function of Ethereum Improvement Proposal (EIP) 2612 sets the same allowance from an off-chain signature \cite{vogelsteller2015erc20,lundfall2020permit}. An allowance moves no funds. It grants a capability that the spender may exercise later and that the owner may raise, lower or revoke, and many allowances are unlimited \cite{wang2022unlimited}. Allowances point mostly from user accounts to the routers, vaults and trading contracts those accounts use, so they are a natural signal for ranking smart contracts. Payments and authorizations thus form two layers over one set of addresses, the setting of multilayer ranking \cite{halu2013multiplex,dedomenico2015ranking}.

We ask whether one random walk over both layers ranks contracts better than a walk over either layer alone, and in particular whether adding authorization edges improves on a walk over payments. We define a coupled PageRank (\cpr{}) that blends the two transition rows of each node with a fixed weight $\lambda$ on the authorization layer, and an ablation, endorsement-seeded PageRank (\spr{}), that walks the payment layer but draws its restarts from the authorization walk, as TrustRank draws its restarts from a vetted seed set \cite{gyongyi2004trustrank}. The end points of the \cpr{} family, $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=0$, walk one layer alone and are the comparators of both decision rules. The scored objects are smart contracts throughout; externally owned accounts enter the graphs as owners, senders and recipients and are never scored.

The evaluation looks forward in time. Scores are frozen at a date and judged by their Kendall rank correlation \cite{kendall1938rank} with outcomes that arrive afterwards: the new owners that approve a contract, the new addresses that pay it, and, for contracts that trade on the GMX~V2 perpetual-futures exchange \cite{gmxnddocs}, the share of their position closes that escaped forced liquidation. The first two outcomes are arrivals of the same kinds of edge that build the layers; the third is built from neither layer and is the outcome closest to counterparty risk in these data \cite{perez2021liquidations}.

Two decision rules were fixed before this study extracted the data that judge them. The analysis plan, which fixes Rule~A and every score, was committed to the authors' public repository ten seconds before the BigQuery job that copied the observation window was created. The registration of the second window, which fixes Rule~B with a non-inferiority margin, was committed eight seconds before the job that copied the logs of July to September 2026, and the script that runs that job refuses to start unless the registration files are committed unchanged; it then records their SHA-256 hashes in a manifest. We call this arrangement commit-gated. The precedence of both commits over the extractions rests on commit and job times, not on a third-party timestamp (Section~\ref{sec:gate}). Both rules failed. After these results were known, we found three defects in how the plan weighted amounts and revised the weighting: amounts in US dollars, five tokens with reliable prices, and a cap on unlimited allowances. The revision was committed before any revised score existed, but it is a deviation decided after the results, and we report its verdicts as a re-analysis under the same rules, beside the pre-specified test and never in its place.

The contributions are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A two-layer model of contract reputation with a coupled walk, a seeded ablation and two analytic remarks: for $\lambda$ strictly between 0 and 1 the weight acts only through nodes with out-edges in both layers, and on a graph whose spenders are approved by owners without approvals of their own the authorization walk reduces to an owner-normalized in-strength.
\item A pre-specified, commit-gated protocol for ranking experiments on ledger data, with the record that dates each commitment (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) and the limits of that record: commit times are set by the committer's clock, and no third-party timestamp anchors them.
\item A negative result on Arbitrum One. \cpr{} met neither rule in the pre-specified analysis or in the re-analysis. Its gain over the payment walk on later approvals held in a window that this study extracted after the rule was fixed, but on later payers it fell behind by more than the registered margin, against AWP by more still, and on the trader outcome it did not stay within the margin. In the first window the authorization walk ranked later approvals worse than the raw count of approving owners, by a paired contrast computed after the labels were known, and no walk of the coupled family was shown to beat that count.
\item A post hoc calibration of known link-farm results to the authorization layer under uniform restarts. On the revised EndorseRank graph a closed farm collects 3.17 times its population share at damping 0.85, and a star of 22, about 2,074 or about 135,895 farm addresses lifts its target past the 90th percentile, the 99th percentile or the top of the cohort's EndorseRank scores; on the registered graph the figures are 2.96, 13, 1,253 and about 1,080,271.
\end{enumerate}

A companion manuscript \cite{kwon2026companion} compares the two edge types as separate scores, sets each against its raw degree and studies the trader outcomes, built from neither edge type, under three rules (R1--R3) registered in the same commit as Rule~B. Both papers describe the same data and report the spender holdout coefficients of the raw degrees, EndorseRank and AWP with their degree contrasts, the closed form of the authorization walk and the solver runtimes. The allowance-against-transfer comparison on trader outcomes (rules R1--R3 of that study) is reported only there; on the same trader cohort this paper reports only the contrasts of \cpr{} that Rule~B and its registered sensitivity analyses name. The companion reports the revised analysis in its Results, with the registered values beside them; this paper leads with the registered analysis because its subject is the two rules fixed for it, and both papers report the same values for both analyses. This paper concerns the coupling operator, its two pre-specified rules and their record, and the manipulability of the authorization layer. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the model, Section~\ref{sec:protocol} states the rules and their record, Section~\ref{sec:data} describes data and measurement, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, Section~\ref{sec:sybil} calibrates farm attacks, Section~\ref{sec:threats} discusses threats to validity, Section~\ref{sec:implications} draws implications for lending and margin protocols, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Appendix~\ref{app:explore} collects exploratory checks.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Reputation From Ledger Graphs}
PageRank ranks the nodes of a directed graph by the stationary distribution of a damped random walk \cite{page1999pagerank,langville2006pagerank}. Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search (HITS) separates hubs from authorities \cite{kleinberg1999hits}, and EigenTrust normalizes local trust values so that peers in a file-sharing network cannot inflate their own standing \cite{kamvar2003eigentrust}. On public ledgers these ideas have been applied almost entirely to transfers. Besides the methods named above \cite{do2023hodgerank,do2023awp}, network embeddings of transaction graphs detect phishing accounts better than per-account features \cite{wu2022phishers}, and a survey of knowledge discovery in cryptocurrency transactions lists degree- and value-based centralities as the usual address descriptors \cite{liu2021knowledge}. Approvals appear in the literature in two roles: as a clustering heuristic that links addresses of one entity \cite{victor2020clustering}, and as a security risk when unlimited \cite{wang2022unlimited}. Deployed tooling such as Human Passport scores the holder of an address from weighted credentials and does not rank relations between addresses \cite{humanpassportnddocs}. The companion study \cite{kwon2026companion} uses the latest allowance as the edge of a ranking; the present paper combines that edge with the payment edge in one walk.

\subsection{Multilayer and Personalized Ranking}
Multiplex PageRank lets the centrality a node earns in one layer bias the walk in another \cite{halu2013multiplex}, and the interconnected-multilayer framework of De Domenico \emph{et al.} lets a walker move inside layers and switch between them, ranking nodes by their versatility across layers \cite{dedomenico2015ranking}. Topic-sensitive and personalized PageRank change the teleportation vector instead of the transition matrix \cite{haveliwala2003topic,jeh2003personalized}, and TrustRank confines teleportation to a hand-checked seed set to demote web spam \cite{gyongyi2004trustrank}. \cpr{} is a special case of the first family: a per-node convex combination of two row-stochastic matrices with one global weight and no copies of a node across layers. \spr{} belongs to the second family. Proposition~\ref{prop:localize} below is a first-order perturbation bound of the standard kind for PageRank \cite{langville2006pagerank}, specialized to this convex combination. We have not found in the literature a test of either construction on two on-chain relations among the same addresses, judged by a rule that the study fixed before it extracted its evaluation data.

\subsection{Degree Baselines}
Under preferential attachment new links go disproportionately to nodes that already have many \cite{barabasi1999scaling}, and measurements of growing networks show the same tendency \cite{newman2001clustering}. Degree-based scores are therefore standard baselines in link prediction \cite{libennowell2007link}, including on Ethereum transaction networks \cite{lin2022tracking}, and on graphs with weak degree correlations PageRank is on average proportional to in-degree \cite{fortunato2008indegree}. A claim that a walk adds information should thus be tested against the raw degree it smooths, and we set every walk against it.

\subsection{Manipulation and Sybil Resistance}
Where identities are free, one actor can appear as many \cite{douceur2002sybil}. Gy\"{o}ngyi and Garcia-Molina derive the link farms and alliances that raise a target's PageRank the most \cite{gyongyi2005alliances}. Cheng and Friedman show that no symmetric reputation function, PageRank included, is Sybil-proof, whereas some asymmetric functions computed from flows or paths out of a trusted source are \cite{cheng2005sybilproof}. Section~\ref{sec:sybil} applies these results to the authorization layer; it calibrates them rather than deriving new ones. On ledgers, wash trading inflates payment activity \cite{victor2021washtrading,cong2023washtrading}, proof-of-personhood schemes trade Sybil resistance against privacy \cite{siddarth2020watchmen}, and a recent review of decentralized systems names credential farming among the remaining weaknesses of such gates \cite{bordeianu2026sybil}.

\subsection{Pre-Specified Analyses}
Preregistration separates the analyses that test a hypothesis from those that generate one, and it can be applied to data that already exist, provided the analysts have not yet examined the outcomes \cite{nosek2018preregistration}. With observational ledger data the events of a test window exist on chain from the moment they occur, so the strongest practical guarantee a study can give is that it had not extracted them when its rule was fixed. Construct-validity theory adds a caution that matters below: a score should agree with checks of what it was designed to track, so such agreement is not independent evidence \cite{cronbach1955construct}.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two Layers on One Set of Addresses}
Let $V=V_A\cup V_T$ be the set of addresses in either layer (Fig.~\ref{fig:layers}). We use \emph{authorization} for the layer, its edges and its walk, \emph{approval} for the event that creates an edge, and \emph{allowance} for the approved amount; likewise \emph{payment} for the second layer and \emph{transfer} for its events. An authorization edge $u\to v$ exists when the latest \texttt{Approval} event from owner $u$ to spender $v$ up to the anchor date carries a positive value on at least one token; a latest value of zero removes the pair. This is what the pipeline measures, and it differs from the allowance in force in three ways: allowances granted before the observation window and not re-emitted inside it are invisible; a finite allowance that \texttt{transferFrom} has since drawn down keeps its approved value, because a token need not emit an \texttt{Approval} event when an allowance is spent; and an owner who approves an allowance manager such as Uniswap's Permit2 appears with an edge to that manager only. A payment edge $u\to v$ exists when $u$ sent $v$ at least one transfer with a positive amount. Native ether moves without \texttt{Transfer} logs and enters the payment layer only as wrapped ether.

The operator studied here weights both layers by amounts:
\begin{equation}
\tilde w_A(u,v)=\sum_{\text{token}} g\bigl(a^{\star}(u,v,\text{token})\bigr),
\label{eq:layerA}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\tilde w_T(u,v)=\sum_{e:\,u\to v} x_e\,\sigma(\Delta t_e),
\label{eq:layerT}
\end{equation}
where $a^{\star}$ is the latest allowance, $x_e$ the amount of transfer $e$, and $\sigma(\Delta t)=1/(1+\exp(k(\Delta t-t_0)))$ a logistic discount on the age $\Delta t$ in days, the form used by AWP \cite{do2023awp}; that paper fixes no parameter values, and $k=0.01$ and $t_0=180$ are our choices. Ages are measured back from the anchor. The authorization layer is not discounted. The plan fixed amounts in raw token base units, every ERC-20 token and $g(a)=a$; the revision of Section~\ref{sec:revision} values amounts in US dollars over five tokens and caps each allowance, $g(a)=\min(a,U)$ with $U=20{,}000$ dollars. Write $o_A(u)$ and $o_T(u)$ for the out-strengths and $P_A$, $P_T$ for the row-normalized matrices, whose rows are zero where a node has no out-edge in that layer.

\subsection{PageRank With a Restart Distribution}
Every score below is the fixed point of
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r^{(t+1)}=(1-d)\,\boldsymbol\pi+d\,P^{\top}\mathbf r^{(t)}+d\,\bigl(\mathbf 1_{\mathrm{dang}}^{\top}\mathbf r^{(t)}\bigr)\,\boldsymbol\pi,
\label{eq:pr}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the transition matrix, $\boldsymbol\pi$ the restart distribution, and $\mathbf 1_{\mathrm{dang}}$ the indicator of nodes with an all-zero row, whose mass is sent back through $\boldsymbol\pi$. We use $d=0.85$, start from the uniform vector, and stop when the $\ell_1$ change falls below $10^{-8}$ or after 300 iterations.

\subsection{Coupled PageRank}
\cpr{} mixes the two rows of each node, renormalizing over the layers in which the node has out-edges:
\begin{equation}
P^{(\lambda)}_{u\cdot}=\alpha_A(u)\,P_{A,u\cdot}+\alpha_T(u)\,P_{T,u\cdot},
\label{eq:cpr}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\alpha_A(u)=\frac{\lambda\,\mathbf 1[o_A(u)>0]}{D_\lambda(u)},\quad
\alpha_T(u)=\frac{(1-\lambda)\,\mathbf 1[o_T(u)>0]}{D_\lambda(u)},
\label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
with $D_\lambda(u)=\lambda\,\mathbf 1[o_A(u)>0]+(1-\lambda)\,\mathbf 1[o_T(u)>0]$. A node with out-edges in one layer follows that layer with weight one, and a node with none in either is dangling. Restarts are uniform. The primary setting $\lambda=0.5$ and the sensitivity values $0.25$ and $0.75$ were fixed in the plan (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

At $\lambda=1$ the payment layer is dropped together with the nodes only it contains, and the operator becomes PageRank on the authorization layer alone; at $\lambda=0$ it becomes PageRank on the payment layer alone. We call these the authorization walk and the payment walk; they are the comparators of both rules. The family is discontinuous at both ends. For every $\lambda$ strictly between 0 and 1, a node whose out-edges are all payments, such as a contract that pays out but approves no one, follows the payment layer, whereas at $\lambda=1$ the same node is dangling or absent. Symmetrically, a node whose out-edges are all authorizations follows the authorization layer at every interior $\lambda$ but is dangling or absent at $\lambda=0$. Unit tests on small fixtures check that each end point reproduces its single-layer walk.

\subsection{Seeded Ablation}
\spr{} keeps the payment walk and replaces its uniform restart vector by the authorization walk's score $\mathbf r_A$, restricted to $V_T$ and renormalized:
\begin{equation}
\pi_u=\frac{r_{A,u}}{\sum_{v\in V_T} r_{A,v}},\qquad u\in V_T,
\label{eq:spr}
\end{equation}
with $r_{A,u}=0$ for $u\notin V_A$ and a uniform fallback if the denominator vanishes. Authorization then decides where the walker lands after a jump, and payments decide where it goes between jumps. Comparing \spr{} with \cpr{} separates the authorization edge as a route from the authorization edge as a seed.

\subsection{Reference Scores}
Two published or companion scores and two counts serve as references. AWP \cite{do2023awp} walks the payment layer with weights $\sigma(\Delta t_e)V(x_e)$ summed over transfers, where $V(z)=2/(1+e^{-bz})-1$ is a bounded value transform, and restarts at each address in proportion to its recent sending activity. EndorseRank \cite{kwon2026companion} walks the authorization layer with the same bounded weights, applied to the latest allowance and its age, and with uniform restarts. In the plan $b=1$ on raw base units, which makes $V(z)$ within $10^{-4}$ of one for any amount of ten base units or more; in the revision $b=\ln 3/m$, with $m$ the median amount up to 31 March 2026 rounded to two significant digits, so that an amount of $m$ weighs one half ($m_T=200$ dollars for transfers, from 203.7, and $m_A=62$ dollars for finite allowances, from 61.5). Both reference walks bound the weight of each event, whereas \cpr{} lets amounts matter without a bound up to the cap. The counts are the in-approve degree, the number of distinct owners holding a positive latest allowance to the contract, and the transfer in-degree, the number of distinct addresses other than itself that sent it a transfer, both taken at the freeze.

\subsection{Where the Weight Acts}
Let $B=\{u: o_A(u)>0 \text{ and } o_T(u)>0\}$ be the set of nodes with out-edges in both layers.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:localize}
For $\lambda\in(0,1)$ and uniform $\boldsymbol\pi$, the rows of $P^{(\lambda)}$ outside $B$ do not depend on $\lambda$, and the derivative $\mathbf r'(\lambda)$ of the stationary vector of (\ref{eq:pr}) with respect to $\lambda$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r'(\lambda)=d\,\bigl(I-d\,G_\lambda^{\top}\bigr)^{-1}\sum_{u\in B} r_u\bigl(\mathbf p_{A,u}-\mathbf p_{T,u}\bigr),
\label{eq:sens}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf p_{A,u}$ and $\mathbf p_{T,u}$ are the transposed rows of $P_A$ and $P_T$ and $G_\lambda$ is $P^{(\lambda)}$ with each dangling row replaced by $\boldsymbol\pi^{\top}$. Hence $\|\mathbf r'(\lambda)\|_1\le 2d\,r(B)/(1-d)$ with $r(B)=\sum_{u\in B}r_u$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
Outside $B$ one indicator in (\ref{eq:alpha}) is zero, so the row is a single layer's row or zero whatever $\lambda$ is, and the dangling set does not change on $(0,1)$. For $u\in B$, $D_\lambda(u)=1$ and the row is $\lambda P_{A,u\cdot}+(1-\lambda)P_{T,u\cdot}$, whose derivative is $P_{A,u\cdot}-P_{T,u\cdot}$. Because $\mathbf r$ sums to one, (\ref{eq:pr}) can be written $\mathbf r=(1-d)\boldsymbol\pi+d\,G_\lambda^{\top}\mathbf r$; differentiating gives (\ref{eq:sens}). The matrix $G_\lambda^{\top}$ is column-stochastic, so $\|(I-dG_\lambda^{\top})^{-1}\|_1\le 1/(1-d)$, and each difference of two probability rows has $\ell_1$ norm at most 2.
\end{IEEEproof}

The proposition says where the weight can act, not that its action is small: when $r(B)$ is large the bound exceeds the trivial bound of 2 on the $\ell_1$ distance between two distributions. In our graphs almost every node of the authorization layer is an owner with out-edges, so every owner that also appears as a sender of an extracted transfer belongs to $B$. We did not compute $r(B)$; Appendix~\ref{app:explore} reports how far the rankings move between interior values of $\lambda$.

\subsection{The Authorization Walk on Depth-One Spenders}
\label{sec:closed}
Our graphs keep only events with a cohort contract at one end, so most nodes of the authorization layer either only grant approvals (pure owners) or only receive them (pure spenders). The companion study derives a closed form for this structure \cite{kwon2026companion}; we restate it with its proof.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:closed}
Under (\ref{eq:pr}) with uniform $\boldsymbol\pi$, every node without in-edges has the same score $c$, and a node $v$ whose in-neighbors all lack in-edges scores $r_v=c\,(1+d\,\delta(v))$, where $\delta(v)=\sum_u w(u,v)/o(u)$ is its owner-normalized in-strength.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
With uniform restarts and dangling mass $r_D$, (\ref{eq:pr}) reads $r_v=(1-d+d\,r_D)/|V|+d\sum_u r_u\,w(u,v)/o(u)$. A node without in-edges receives only the first term, which we call $c$. If every in-neighbor $u$ of $v$ lacks in-edges, then $r_u=c$, and substituting gives $r_v=c+d\,c\,\delta(v)$.
\end{IEEEproof}

The statement holds for any edge weights, so it covers EndorseRank and \cpr{} at $\lambda=1$, each with its own $\delta$. On the EndorseRank graph at the end of the observation window, 12,039 of the 14,406 cohort spenders of the revised analysis have depth one, and the computed score lies within 1\% of the closed form for every one of them; over all spenders the share is 87.9\%, and 26.4\% of the deeper ones \cite{kwon2026companion}. In the registered analysis the figures are 20,942 of 26,512, every one of them, 86.4\% and 35.1\%. For most spenders the authorization walk is therefore a reweighted count of approving owners, and \cpr{} inherits that limit wherever it follows the authorization layer.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}
One iteration of (\ref{eq:pr}) costs $O(|E|)$ \cite{leskovec2020mining}, and \cpr{} walks the union of both edge sets. Table~\ref{tab:bench} reports the solver time of each score on the full graphs at the end of the observation window, with $|V|$ and $|E|$ of each solver graph. In the revised analysis \cpr{} takes 36.16\,s on 9,151,010 nodes and 31,552,359 edges, 1.62 times AWP's time on 1.26 times its edges; in the registered analysis 105.51\,s on 25,304,106 nodes and 71,375,442 edges, 1.51 times AWP's time on 1.19 times its edges. EndorseRank is the fastest score because its graph is the sparsest: the authorization graph holds 44.8\% of the payment graph's edges in the revised analysis and 37.4\% in the registered one, and the time ratio of AWP to EndorseRank (2.17 and 2.36) follows the edge ratio (2.23 and 2.68) from below. The speed reflects the smaller edge count, not the algorithm, and any walk that uses the payment layer pays for its edges. Times are means of five runs after one untimed warm-up on one machine (Intel Core Ultra~9 185H, 64\,GB of memory, Windows~11, Python 3.11.9, NumPy 2.3.2, SciPy 1.16.1).

\subsection{Rule A (Analysis Plan, 9 October 2026, 13:41:27 UTC)}
Rule~A asked whether \cpr{} beats both layers walked alone. Its primary part used the first holdout window, W0 (scores frozen on 31 March 2026, labels from April to June 2026): at $\lambda=0.5$, \cpr{} should do no worse than the authorization walk on new approval pairs, meaning that the 95\% interval of $\Delta\tau=\tau_{\text{\cpr{}}}-\tau_{\lambda=1}$ contains zero or lies above it (A1), and should beat the payment walk on new transfer senders, meaning that the interval of $\tau_{\text{\cpr{}}}-\tau_{\lambda=0}$ lies above zero (A2). Its secondary part used the matched cohort in the observation window: \cpr{}'s point estimate had to lie inside the 95\% interval of the better single layer on a family of transfer checks and on a family of authorization checks, and \cpr{} had to exceed both layers on a family of three Sybil-adjusted stability checks (the plan's name; the share of distinct inbound counterparties, tenure and active months). If the primary part failed, the question whether \cpr{} beats a walk over either layer alone was to be answered in the negative, and no $\lambda$ was to be tuned after labels were seen.

Two criteria of the rule are weak by design. ``No worse'' as defined rewards imprecision, since a wider interval contains zero more easily, and the secondary part compares a point estimate with another score's marginal interval rather than using a paired contrast. Anyone reusing the protocol should replace the first by a non-inferiority margin and the second by paired contrasts; we report the paired contrasts beside the rule.

\subsection{Rule B (Registration, 9 October 2026, 16:49:32 UTC)}
Rule~B asked whether \cpr{} improves on the payment walk, $\Delta\tau=\tau_{\text{\cpr{}}}-\tau_{\lambda=0}$, in a second window, W1, frozen on 30 June 2026 with labels counted from 1 July to 30 September 2026:
\begin{itemize}
\item F1 (spenders; new approval pairs), superiority: the whole 95\% interval of $\Delta\tau$ must be positive;
\item F2 (spenders; new transfer senders), non-inferiority: the lower end of the interval must lie above $-\delta$;
\item F3 (traders; liquidation-free close rate), non-inferiority with the same margin.
\end{itemize}
All three had to hold. Because the decision is an intersection of three conditions, testing each at the 5\% level keeps the probability of a false claim of improvement at or below 5\% without a multiplicity correction. The margin $\delta=0.01$ is the half-width of the W0 interval of the contrast that F2 repeats ($-0.030$, $[-0.034,-0.026]$, half-width 0.0040), rounded up to two decimals. It measures the precision of a spender contrast, not a difference judged to matter in practice, and the registration applied it to the trader contrast F3 as well, whose interval rests on far fewer contracts (Section~\ref{sec:precision}). The registration also recorded a weakness of F3 in advance: it could pass with both coefficients near zero if neither walk predicted liquidation, so both coefficients are reported beside the verdict.

Three further results were to be reported in a fixed order outside the decision: F4, superiority of \cpr{} over the payment walk on the liquidation-free close rate; F5, superiority over the authorization walk on that label; and F6, the primary part of Rule~A rerun on W1. ``Fixed order'' meant only the order of reporting; there was no gatekeeping, so F4 to F6 are nominal results with unadjusted 95\% intervals. Two sensitivity analyses were registered with them: S1 repeats F1 to F4 with AWP as the comparator, and S2 repeats F1 to F6 with cohort contracts that a graph leaves out kept as isolated nodes rather than scored zero. Neither enters the decision. Scores, solver, queries, cohort filter, exchange-event decoder and bootstrap (400 paired resamples, seed 42) were those of the plan.

\subsection{Gate on Extraction}
\label{sec:gate}
The configuration carries a status field for W1, which the registration commit set to ``registered''. Before it queries anything, the extraction script checks that the registration and the configuration are committed without local changes and that the status is ``registered''; it then records the commit and the SHA-256 hashes of both files in a manifest. The gate compares no hash; the hashes let a reader check afterwards that the committed files are the ones the script saw. The script has no override, and the manifest's field \texttt{override\_used} is a constant that records nothing. Table~\ref{tab:ids} gives the full identifiers of the commits and the hashes. The manifest hashed the files on the authors' Windows checkout, with CRLF line ends; the same text with LF line ends, as git stores it, gives the second pair of hashes, and both were checked against the committed files.

What the record shows depends on who keeps it. The commit times are set by the clock of the machine that made the commits and can be chosen by their author. The BigQuery job times (creation of scan~A at 13:41:37 and of scan~B at 16:49:40, Table~\ref{tab:timeline}) are kept by the cloud provider in the project's job history, which only members of the project can read; we copied them into the data description committed in \sha{f33a87a} (23:24:39~UTC on 9 October). Outsiders can therefore check the content of the registration, the hashes and the order of the commits, but the claim that the registration preceded the W1 extraction rests on the commit and job times, not on a third-party timestamp. A trusted timestamp, such as anchoring the commit hash on chain or in an independent registry before extraction, would remove this gap.

\subsection{The Revision of the Amounts}
\label{sec:revision}
After the registered results of all windows were known, three defects of the plan's weighting came to light. First, with $b=1$ on raw base units $V(z)$ is within $10^{-4}$ of one for 99.52\% of the transfers and 99.78\% of the latest allowances, so the amount played no part in EndorseRank or AWP. Second, each layer of \cpr{} summed raw base units over tokens, although a USDC has $10^{6}$ base units and a wrapped ether $10^{18}$, and an unlimited allowance ($2^{256}-1$ base units, 45.3\% of the latest allowances) absorbed nearly all of the row of any owner holding one. Third, the logs of every ERC-20 contract entered the graphs, although a token contract can write logs that name any address as an owner or a sender whether or not that address acted.

The revision values every amount in US dollars at the daily close of the DefiLlama price series \cite{defillamanddocs}, keeps the five tokens with the most rows among those priced by a Chainlink market-price feed of low or medium market risk \cite{chainlinknddatafeeds} (WETH, USDC, USDT0, ARB and WBTC, which hold 75.75\% of the transfers and end-of-window latest allowances of the plan's ego data), sets $b=\ln 3/m$ for EndorseRank and AWP, and caps each allowance of the \cpr{} authorization layer at $U=20{,}000$ dollars, the 99th percentile of single transfers of the five tokens up to 31 March 2026 (20,266 dollars) rounded to two significant digits. All cohorts, graphs, proxies and spender labels were recomputed from the five tokens. The revision was committed in three steps (Table~\ref{tab:timeline}); the third amended the cap after the constants had been computed and before any revised score existed, because the first definition, a percentile of the finite allowances, lay near $10^{47}$ dollars. Rules A and B, their thresholds, $\delta$ and $\lambda$ were not changed. The revision is a deviation from the plan and the registration, decided with the registered results in view, and every verdict on the revised scores is a re-analysis.

\subsection{What Was Not Pre-Specified}
Every contrast outside A1, A2, F1--F6, S1 and S2 was computed after the labels of its window were known: the comparison of \cpr{} with the payment walk on new approval pairs in W0, the \spr{} contrasts, every contrast against a raw degree, and every comparison of \cpr{} with EndorseRank or AWP outside S1. The W0 profit labels of the traders had been computed in an exploratory run, and their coefficients were committed with the registration; the liquidation label of W0 had not been computed for any score of this study. The plan commit holds the plan, the configuration, the query that copies the logs and the BigQuery runner. The queries and scripts that build the cohort, the ego network, the pair tables and the W0 labels, the implementations of every score and of the bootstrap, and the drivers of the same-window and W0 evaluations entered the repository with the registration commit, after they had run on the observation window and after Rule~A had been judged, so their text before that point is not on record; the unit tests of Section~\ref{sec:model} entered with them. The W1 extraction and evaluation used them as committed. Every check in Appendix~\ref{app:explore} was run on 11 October 2026, after all of the above.

Each graph is therefore the one-hop ego network of the cohort. In the observation window the plan's ego tables hold 389,866,882 approval and 1,538,404,067 transfer logs, and the revised ones 224,954,202 and 1,149,168,538.

At the end of the observation window the authorization graph has 13,055,150 nodes and 22,321,470 edges in the registered analysis and 6,221,665 nodes and 11,210,114 edges in the revised one; the payment graph has 23,029,298 nodes and 59,737,617 edges, and 7,884,602 nodes and 25,001,216 edges. \subsection{Cohorts and Labels}
The spender cohort of a window holds every contract that has a positive latest allowance at the freeze in the extracted approval data; it includes contracts outside the matched cohort that a cohort contract approved. Their first label counts new approval pairs: owners that gave the spender a positive approval during the label window although the pair held no positive allowance at the freeze. Their second label counts new transfer senders: addresses other than the spender that sent it a transfer during the label window but had not done so between 1 October 2023 and the freeze. In W0 the spender cohort holds 33,101 contracts in the registered and 17,050 in the revised analysis, and in W1 34,587 and 18,152; Table~\ref{tab:holdout} gives the number that gained each label.

The W1 trader cohort consists of contract accounts on GMX~V2 that appear in either freeze-date graph and closed three or more positions between July and September 2026: 70 contracts in both analyses, 9 of them with at least one liquidation and 1 with every close liquidated. Their label is the liquidation-free close rate, one minus the share of their closes in the label window that ended in liquidation. It is built from neither layer, and as a rate it does not reward trading more. Contract accounts closed 133,685 positions in the observation window (6,473 through liquidation) and 6,805 in the third quarter of 2026 (182 through liquidation).

\subsection{Statistics}
Every coefficient is the tie-corrected Kendall $\tau_b$ \cite{kendall1938rank}, computed with SciPy, with a 95\% percentile interval from a paired bootstrap over the contracts of the cohort \cite{efron1979bootstrap,diciccio1996bootstrap}. Resample indices are drawn with NumPy's default generator from seed 42, one index matrix per cohort, and the same matrix serves every score on a label, so each difference between two scores is a paired difference. Every result uses 400 resamples. The supplementary script recomputes every decision contrast except those of S2 from the published per-contract score files and reproduces the published values exactly (Appendix~\ref{app:explore}).

The margin came from the precision of a spender contrast and was applied unchanged to the trader contrast. \section{Sybil Exposure of the Authorization Layer}
\label{sec:sybil}

The results above concern honest data. A reputation layer must also be judged by what it costs to fake. This section calibrates the farm results of Gy\"{o}ngyi and Garcia-Molina \cite{gyongyi2005alliances} to EndorseRank's authorization walk with uniform restarts, on its graph of the revised analysis at the end of the observation window (6,219,063 nodes and 11,210,114 edges), with the registered graph for comparison. The closed-farm bound (\ref{eq:farm}) holds for any uniformly restarted walk on this layer, \cpr{} at $\lambda=1$ included, but the star counts below are read against the cohort's EndorseRank scores. The model was computed after the W1 labels were known and enters no rule.

\subsection{Cost of an Edge}
Approving a spender reserves nothing: no tokens are locked, and the approving address may hold none of the token. Under bounded weights an approval of a few hundred dollars weighs almost as much as an unlimited one, and after row normalization a single out-edge carries the whole row whatever its amount. An edge between two attacker addresses therefore exposes nothing and costs only the fee. Because EndorseRank reads only the latest approvals up to the anchor and discounts them by age, an attacker who wants its edges at full weight re-emits them before each refresh, so over $q$ refreshes a closed farm costs
\begin{equation}
C=q\,\bigl(|E_{\mathcal S}|\,c_{\text{gas}}+c_{\text{rev}}\bigr),
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $c_{\text{gas}}$ is the fee of one approval and $c_{\text{rev}}$ a per-refresh allowance for revocations or detection avoidance. We did not measure $c_{\text{gas}}$; whatever its value, the cost is linear in edges and refreshes. The undiscounted authorization layer of \cpr{} keeps an edge at full weight, with no refresh, for as long as the approval stays inside the observation window. Restricting the graph to five widely traded tokens does not raise this cost, since approving one of them costs a farm no more than approving any other token; what it removes are logs that a token contract writes on behalf of owners who never acted.

\subsection{What a Closed Farm Collects}
Let every node of a farm $\mathcal S$ have out-edges, all inside $\mathcal S$, and let no honest owner approve a farm address. Write $r_D$ for the stationary mass on dangling nodes, all of which are honest. Summing (\ref{eq:pr}) over $\mathcal S$ with uniform $\boldsymbol\pi$ gives
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal S} r_i=\frac{|\mathcal S|}{|V|}\cdot\frac{1-d+d\,r_D}{1-d}\le\frac{|\mathcal S|}{(1-d)|V|+d|\mathcal S|},
\label{eq:farm}
\end{equation}
where the bound uses $r_D\le 1-\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}r_i$. The second factor is the multiple of its population share that the farm captures, and it exceeds one whenever dangling nodes exist, because their redistributed mass reaches the farm. On the revised graph $r_D=0.355$, $0.382$ and $0.404$ at $d=0.75$, $0.85$ and $0.95$, so a closed farm captures 2.06, 3.17 and 8.68 times its population share; a ring of 20 addresses, each approving the next, inserted into the graph captures 3.17 times its share at $d=0.85$. On the registered graph the factors are 2.01, 2.96 and 6.62.

\subsection{Concentrating the Mass}
The bound limits a farm's total mass, but the attacker can concentrate it. In a star, $f$ farm addresses each approve a target $t$ and $t$ approves each of them back. With $c=(1-d+d\,r_D)/|V|$, the mass each node receives from jumps and dangling rows, the balance equations give
\begin{equation}
r_t=\frac{c\,(1+d\,f)}{1-d^{2}}.
\label{eq:star}
\end{equation}
On the revised graph $c=7.64\times10^{-8}$, the common score of every node without in-edges. With $f=10$, eleven addresses and twenty approvals per refresh, the target scores $2.62\times10^{-6}$ on the graph with the star added, the value of (\ref{eq:star}), above the median of the cohort's scores ($1.88\times10^{-7}$) and about half of their 90th percentile ($5.28\times10^{-6}$). Each further farm address adds $d\,c/(1-d^2)$ at the price of two approvals per refresh. Solving (\ref{eq:star}) for $f$ shows what each rank costs. The median takes no star at all, because a pair of addresses that approve each other already reaches $c/(1-d)$. The 90th percentile takes 22 farm addresses, the 99th ($4.86\times10^{-4}$) about 2,074, and the highest score among the cohort's contracts (0.0318) about 135,895, a count that ignores how a farm that large would itself enlarge $|V|$ and lower $c$. On the registered graph the numbers are 13, 1,253 and about 1,080,271. A farm thus reaches the upper tenth of the cohort's scores with 44 approvals per refresh (26 on the registered graph), whose fee we did not measure, while the very top takes a farm thousands of times larger, because the leading contracts hold a large share of the stationary mass.

\subsection{The Same Argument for the Coupled Walk}
In \cpr{} at any $\lambda>0$, the renormalization in (\ref{eq:alpha}) lets a node whose out-edges are all approvals follow them with weight one. A farm whose addresses approve only one another and make no payments is therefore closed in \cpr{} exactly as in the authorization walk, and (\ref{eq:farm}) applies with \cpr{}'s dangling mass. We did not compute that mass or run the farms on \cpr{}'s graph, so the multipliers above are not claimed for it; the argument shows only that coupling does not close the gap. In the capped \cpr{} layer a purchased approval from an honest owner shifts much mass only when it is large relative to that owner's other allowances, and an approval at the cap lets the spender draw at least $U$ dollars from the owner. In EndorseRank's bounded layer, by contrast, a fresh approval of a few hundred dollars takes 95.7\% of the median owner's row, because most owners' latest approvals are old or small.

\subsection{Remedy, Untested}
The asymmetry that Cheng and Friedman identify points to the fix \cite{cheng2005sybilproof}. When jumps and the mass of dangling nodes are confined to a vetted seed set, as in TrustRank \cite{gyongyi2004trustrank}, a farm outside the seeds gains mass only along edges from honest owners, and those edges must be bought. We did not evaluate seeded restarts, and \spr{} is not such a remedy, because its seeds are authorization scores that a farm can inflate. Credential gates such as Human Passport \cite{humanpassportnddocs} address a different question, whether an address belongs to a distinct person, which has no direct counterpart for a contract, and they carry their own weaknesses \cite{bordeianu2026sybil}. The analysis is model-based and was checked with the solver on the observed graph; no attack was run on chain.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Construct Validity}
The spender labels are arrivals of the same kinds of edge that build the layers, so they measure future attachment and favor scores that track current counts; neither is a credit, safety or loss outcome. The trader label is the outcome closest to default in these data, but forced liquidation on one exchange is not default on a loan, and only 9 of the 70 W1 traders had a liquidation. The authorization edge is the latest approval up to the anchor, not the allowance in force (Section~\ref{sec:model}); reading allowances from contract state at the freeze block would settle how many pairs counted as new were old relationships re-emitted. The layer weights matter: in the plan the raw-amount layers let unlimited approvals and high-decimal tokens dominate rows, and the revision changed that. The two analyses reached the same verdicts, but they are two weightings, not a sweep over weightings, and other couplings, such as layers with EndorseRank's and AWP's bounded weights, were not tested on these data.

\subsection{Internal Validity}
Rule~A was fixed before any log of this study was extracted. Rule~B's hypothesis came from an exploratory W0 contrast, and its margin from a W0 interval. The registration is a commit in the authors' own repository, not an entry in an independent registry, and its precedence over the W1 extraction rests on commit and job times rather than on a third-party timestamp (Section~\ref{sec:gate}). The revised analysis was defined after every registered result was known; three facts limit the risk that the revision favors one outcome, without removing it: it was committed before any revised score was computed, its slopes and cap come from quantiles of events up to the W0 freeze, and it reached the same verdicts under every rule. The rule that scores absent contracts as zero is a pipeline choice; S2 shows that it changes F5 in the registered analysis through the tie structure but no part of the decision.

\subsection{Statistical Conclusion Validity}
Rule-bound intervals come from 400 paired resamples, as fixed in the plan; their Monte Carlo error is small against the distances that decided the verdicts (Appendix~\ref{app:explore}). The decision is an intersection of three conditions, so no multiplicity correction applies to it, but the many contrasts reported beside it are nominal and should not be read as a family of tests. The contract bootstrap treats spenders as independent although one new owner can approve several spenders. The trader cohort is small, and the margin taken from the spenders was not attainable for the traders (Section~\ref{sec:precision}).

\subsection{External Validity}
The evidence comes from one rollup, its contracts approved by at least three owners, 33 months of logs and two three-month label windows. Each graph is the one-hop ego network of the cohort, so the out-strengths and walk paths belong to the ego network, not to the whole chain; deeper samples, in which endorsements travel more than one step, could behave differently. Only fixed global weights with uniform teleportation were tested; node-dependent or learned weights, mixtures of stationary vectors, interlayer copies of nodes and per-layer damping remain open.

\section{Implications for Lending and Margin Protocols}
\label{sec:implications}

A protocol that screens counterparties or integrations from on-chain relations \cite{bartoletti2021lending,perez2021liquidations} can take four practical points from these results. First, the cheapest score was hard to beat on its own construct: counting the distinct owners that approve a contract anticipated its later approvals better than the authorization walk and, by point estimate, about as well as or better than the coupled walk, with no solver and no restart vector to defend. The count is no harder to fake: approvals from $N$ fresh addresses raise it by $N$, for $N$ approval fees. Second, mixing an authorization layer into a payment walk changed the ranking mainly by switching the authorization layer on, and the gain on later approvals came with a loss on later payments larger than the registered margin; a protocol that wants both signals can report the two counts, or AWP and an approval count, side by side rather than fold them into one stationary distribution. Third, any score that walks authorization edges with uniform restarts can be inflated by a closed farm that needs no tokens, only approval fees, which we did not measure; on the revised graph about two dozen farm addresses would reach the top tenth of the cohort's EndorseRank scores (22, against 13 on the registered graph). Restricting restarts to vetted seeds is the remedy the theory points to, but we did not test it. Finally, approval logs are not allowance state: a protocol that acts on allowances should read them from contract state at the decision block rather than reconstruct them from a window of events.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Authorization and payment give two readings of the same contracts. On this sample a walk that blends them at every node ranked later approvals better than either layer alone, though only slightly better than the authorization walk, but ranked later payers worse than the payment walk, so it did not beat both layers on both labels. Under the rule fixed in the plan before this study extracted any log, \cpr{} did no worse than the authorization walk on later approvals but did not beat the payment walk on later payers. Under the rule registered before this study extracted its window, it repeated its gain over the payment walk on later approvals ($+0.101$ $[0.092,0.111]$), but it ranked later payers worse by more than the registered margin ($-0.025$ $[-0.029,-0.021]$), against AWP by more still, and on the liquidation-free trading of contract traders it did not stay within the margin ($-0.044$ $[-0.100,-0.002]$). A revision of the amounts, made after these results were known and applied under the same rules, reached the same verdicts. The gain on approvals appears as soon as the authorization layer is switched on and changes little across interior weights; seeding the payment walk with authorization scores recovered only part of it; and in the first window the authorization walk ranked later approvals worse than the raw count of approving owners, which no walk of the coupled family was shown to beat. Under uniform restarts the authorization layer can also be inflated with a few dozen approvals per refresh, and coupling does not remove that exposure. Future work should fix in advance, anchor with a third-party timestamp and test on unextracted data couplings with comparable weights on both layers, samples deep enough for multi-hop propagation, trader cohorts large enough for the margin, and seed-restricted restarts under explicit attack.

\label{app:explore}

All checks below were run on 11 October 2026, after every result of both analyses was known, by the script in the supplement, which reads the published per-contract score files and summaries and builds no graph. None of them enters a rule. Table~\ref{tab:explore} lists the results.

\emph{Recomputation.} From the score files the script recomputes A1, A2, F1--F6 and S1 in both analyses, with the pipeline's resampling and SciPy's $\tau_b$; every recomputed value equals the published one. S2 cannot be recomputed, because the isolated-node scores of the single-layer walks are not in the score files.

\emph{Seed spread.} Across 50 further seeds of 400 resamples, no decision verdict changes in either analysis (Table~\ref{tab:explore}(a)). One of the 20,000 trader resamples of each analysis was skipped because the label was constant on it.

\emph{Steps of the weight.} Switching the authorization layer on ($\lambda$ from 0 to 0.25) raises $\tau_b$ on new approval pairs by 0.092 to 0.110 and lowers it on new senders by 0.020 to 0.027, with intervals that exclude zero in both windows and both analyses (Table~\ref{tab:explore}(b)). On the trader label only the registered step from 0 to 0.25 has an interval that excludes zero ($-0.037$ $[-0.085,-0.001]$); the registered step from 0.75 to 1 is $+0.222$ $[-0.062,0.487]$.

\emph{Rank agreement.} The \cpr{} rankings at $\lambda=0.25$ and $\lambda=0.75$ agree at $\tau_b$ between 0.869 and 0.884 on every cohort of both analyses, with intervals no wider than 0.006, whereas each end point agrees with its nearest interior value at 0.583 to 0.624 ($\lambda=0$ and 0.25) and 0.618 to 0.661 ($\lambda=0.75$ and 1) (Table~\ref{tab:explore}(c)). The interior is flat on the labels because the ranking itself changes little between interior weights, while the two end points are different operators.
