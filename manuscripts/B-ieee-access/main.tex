\begin{abstract}
Wallets, explorers and integrators must judge which of a rollup's million-plus smart contracts users rely on. Ledger PageRank variants rank addresses by transfers, walk over user accounts that cost nothing to create, and omit token allowances, which let spenders draw an owner's tokens; we found no pre-specified test of a walk over both relations. We test on Arbitrum One whether a walk mixing allowance and transfer edges ranks contracts better than a walk on either alone. Two decision rules were fixed before their evaluation data were extracted. The main analysis, a re-analysis defined after the registered results were known, uses contract-only graphs, US-dollar amounts on five tokens, one-year score windows and allowances capped at owner balances. Neither rule was met in either analysis. On contract graphs the coupled walk ranked later approvals below the allowance walk in the first window (rank-correlation difference $-$0.025, 95\% interval [$-$0.045, $-$0.004]); in the second it ranked them above the transfer walk (0.074 [0.060, 0.087]) but later senders worse than the 0.01 margin allows ($-$0.016 [$-$0.022, $-$0.012]). We conclude that on contract graphs the coupled walk beat neither of its layers and, post hoc, a single count did better on each label; wallets, explorers and integrations can show both counts as weak hints and should leave contracts without contract-to-contract edges, about 27\% of the spenders here, unranked.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{A}{n} Ethereum rollup holds a very large number of smart contracts. In eighteen months of events of five widely used tokens on Arbitrum One \cite{kalodner2018arbitrum}, 1,829,719 addresses hold contract code and are not user accounts (Section~\ref{sec:types}). A wallet that shows a user what a transaction will touch, an explorer that lists contracts and a developer who chooses contracts to integrate all need to know which of these contracts users rely on. The ledger records two relations that bear on the question. A contract receives a transfer when tokens are sent to it, and it receives an allowance when an owner lets it spend the owner's tokens: under the ERC-20 (Ethereum Request for Comments 20) token interface an owner calls \texttt{approve} to let a spender draw up to a stated amount, and the \texttt{permit} function of Ethereum Improvement Proposal (EIP) 2612 sets the same allowance from an off-chain signature \cite{vogelsteller2015erc20,lundfall2020permit}. An allowance moves no funds; it grants a capability that the spender may exercise later, and many allowances are unlimited \cite{wang2022unlimited}.

Ranking by links has a long record on the web. PageRank orders pages by the stationary distribution of a random walk over hyperlinks \cite{page1999pagerank}, and Cho, Garcia-Molina and Page showed that the order in which a crawler visits pages decides how soon it reaches the important ones \cite{cho1998crawling}. On ledgers the idea has been applied almost entirely to transfers and to all addresses at once. Do and Do read PageRank and HodgeRank on Ethereum transactions as a measure of social credit \cite{do2023hodgerank}; Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) of Do, Do and Nguyen weights every transfer by its value and age and restarts the walk at recently active senders \cite{do2023awp}; RiskProp propagates account risk through the transaction network \cite{lin2025riskprop}. Surveys of blockchain reputation systems find the same reliance on transfer volume or on explicit ratings \cite{bellini2020trust}. Three things are missing for the question above. These walks leave out allowances, although allowances link the same addresses. Their graphs hold user accounts, which cost no deployment and whose controllers such a study does not measure, and user accounts hold most of the allowances toward a contract (Section~\ref{sec:cohorts}), so a walk over all accounts is largely a walk over them. And we found no test of a walk over both relations under rules that the study fixed before it extracted its evaluation data.

We ask whether one random walk over both relations ranks contracts better than a walk over either relation alone, and in particular whether adding allowance edges improves on a walk over transfers. Transfers and allowances form two layers over one set of nodes, the setting of multilayer ranking \cite{halu2013multiplex,dedomenico2015ranking}. We define a coupled PageRank (\cpr{}) that blends the two transition rows of each node with a fixed weight $\lambda$ on the allowance layer, and an ablation, endorsement-seeded PageRank (\spr{}), that walks the transfer layer but draws its restarts from the allowance walk, as TrustRank draws its restarts from a vetted seed set \cite{gyongyi2004trustrank}. The end points of the \cpr{} family, $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=0$, walk one layer alone and are the comparators of both decision rules. In the main analysis every node of every graph is a contract: user accounts, smart accounts among them, are nodes of no graph, restarts run over contracts, and an allowance counts only up to the balance its owner holds.

The evaluation looks forward in time. Scores are frozen at a date and judged by their Kendall rank correlation \cite{kendall1938rank} with what arrives afterwards: the new owners that approve a contract and the new addresses that send it transfers. Both labels are arrivals of the same kinds of edge that build the layers, and both count every account, user accounts included, so they measure later use by accounts that no score reads.

Two decision rules were fixed before this study extracted the data that judge them. The analysis plan, which fixes Rule~A and every score, was committed to the authors' Git repository before any log of this study was extracted. The registration of the second window, which fixes Rule~B with a non-inferiority margin, was committed before the logs of July to September 2026 were extracted (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). The plan and the registration fixed an analysis in raw token units over every ERC-20 token, on graphs that hold every account of the cohort's events; we call it the registered analysis. The main analysis of this paper, the contract-graph analysis, was defined after the registered results were known (Section~\ref{sec:main}). Its verdicts are therefore a re-analysis under the same rules, none of them is a registered result, and the registered analysis is reported beside it in the tables and figures of the rules. This paper applies Rule~A and two of the registered criteria of Rule~B, F1 and F2; under the registration the rule holds only if each of its criteria holds. Neither rule is met in either analysis.

The contributions are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A two-layer model for ranking contracts with a coupled walk, a seeded ablation and two analytic remarks: for $\lambda$ strictly between 0 and 1 the weight acts only through nodes with out-edges in both layers, and for a spender approved by owners that nobody approves the allowance walk reduces to an owner-normalized in-strength.
\item A negative result on Arbitrum One, on graphs whose nodes are contracts only. \cpr{} met neither rule, in the contract-graph analysis or in the registered one. On the contract graph it ranked later approvals below the allowance walk and later senders below the transfer walk in the first window; in the second window its gain over the transfer walk on later approvals came with a loss on later senders beyond the registered margin. In contrasts computed after the labels were known, \cpr{} ranked later approvals below the count of approving contracts and later senders below the count of sending contracts in both windows; against the union in-degree, the count of both kinds of edge together, it lay slightly above on later approvals and below on later senders.
\end{enumerate}

A companion manuscript \cite{kwon2026companion} reports the discovery-order test, a crawl-ordering experiment on the same contract graph that asks whether ordering a crawl by a walk brings it to the contracts with the most user-account demand sooner than ordering it by a count, and it sets the single-layer walks, EndorseRank and AWP, against the counts they smooth. Both papers describe the same data, account types and graphs, and both report the spender holdout coefficients of the raw counts, EndorseRank and AWP with their count contrasts, the closed form of EndorseRank and the solver runtimes, with the same values. The discovery-order test is reported only there; Section~\ref{sec:counts} quotes what it found about \cpr{}. \cpr{}'s two rules, applied to the scores of the contract graph as a re-analysis, the weight sweep and the seeded ablation are reported only here. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the model, Section~\ref{sec:protocol} states the rules and the order in which they were fixed, Section~\ref{sec:data} describes data and measurement, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, Section~\ref{sec:threats} discusses threats to validity, Section~\ref{sec:implications} draws implications for wallets, explorers and integrations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Appendix~\ref{app:explore} collects exploratory checks.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Reputation From Ledger Graphs}
PageRank ranks the nodes of a directed graph by the stationary distribution of a damped random walk \cite{page1999pagerank,langville2006pagerank}. Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search (HITS) separates hubs from authorities \cite{kleinberg1999hits}, and EigenTrust normalizes local trust values so that peers in a file-sharing network cannot inflate their own standing \cite{kamvar2003eigentrust}. On public ledgers these ideas have been applied almost entirely to transfers. Besides the methods named above \cite{do2023hodgerank,do2023awp}, network embeddings of transaction graphs detect phishing accounts better than per-account features \cite{wu2022phishers}, and a survey of knowledge discovery in cryptocurrency transactions lists degree- and value-based centralities as the usual address descriptors \cite{liu2021knowledge}. Approvals appear in the literature in two roles: as a clustering heuristic that links addresses of one entity \cite{victor2020clustering}, and as a security risk when unlimited \cite{wang2022unlimited}. Relations among contracts have been studied as compositions of decentralized-finance protocols \cite{kitzler2023defi}, not as a ranking. Deployed tooling such as Human Passport scores the holder of an address from weighted credentials and does not rank relations between addresses \cite{humanpassportnddocs}. The companion study \cite{kwon2026companion} uses the latest allowance as the edge of a ranking and repeats the crawl-ordering experiment of Cho, Garcia-Molina and Page \cite{cho1998crawling} on the contract graph; the present paper combines that edge with the transfer edge in one walk.

\subsection{Multilayer and Personalized Ranking}
Multiplex PageRank lets the centrality a node earns in one layer bias the walk in another \cite{halu2013multiplex}, and the interconnected-multilayer framework of De Domenico \emph{et al.} lets a walker move inside layers and switch between them, ranking nodes by their versatility across layers \cite{dedomenico2015ranking}. Topic-sensitive and personalized PageRank change the teleportation vector rather than the transition matrix \cite{haveliwala2003topic,jeh2003personalized}, and TrustRank confines teleportation to a hand-checked seed set to demote web spam \cite{gyongyi2004trustrank}. \cpr{} is a special case of the first family: a per-node convex combination of two row-stochastic matrices with one global weight and no copies of a node across layers. \spr{} belongs to the second family. Proposition~\ref{prop:localize} below is a first-order perturbation bound of the standard kind for PageRank \cite{langville2006pagerank}, specialized to this convex combination. We have not found in the literature a test of either construction on two on-chain relations among the same addresses, judged by a rule that the study fixed before it extracted its evaluation data.

\subsection{Degree Baselines}
Under preferential attachment new links go disproportionately to nodes that already have many \cite{barabasi1999scaling}, and measurements of growing networks show the same tendency \cite{newman2001clustering}. Degree-based scores are therefore standard baselines in link prediction \cite{libennowell2007link}, including on Ethereum transaction networks \cite{lin2022tracking}, and on graphs with weak degree correlations PageRank is on average proportional to in-degree \cite{fortunato2008indegree}. A claim that a walk adds information should thus be tested against the raw count it smooths. We set every walk against that count, and the coupled walk also against the union in-degree, the number of other contracts with an edge to a contract in either layer.

\subsection{Artificial Activity}
Recorded activity need not reflect use. In wash trading one actor trades with itself to inflate volume, which has been measured on decentralized exchanges \cite{victor2021washtrading} and, at a larger scale, on unregulated centralized exchanges \cite{cong2023washtrading}; a score built from transfers can carry such activity.

\subsection{Pre-Specified Analyses}
Preregistration separates the analyses that test a hypothesis from those that generate one, and it can be applied to data that already exist, provided the analysts have not yet examined the outcomes \cite{nosek2018preregistration}. With observational ledger data the events of a test window exist on chain from the moment they occur, so the strongest practical guarantee a study can give is that it had not extracted them when its rule was fixed. Construct-validity theory adds a caution that matters below: a score should agree with checks of what it was designed to track, so such agreement is not independent evidence \cite{cronbach1955construct}.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two Layers on One Set of Contracts}
Let $V=V_A\cup V_T$ be the set of contracts in either layer (Fig.~\ref{fig:layers}). Every node is a contract, an address with code that is not a user account under the rule of Section~\ref{sec:types}. Externally owned accounts, accounts with an EIP-7702 delegation and smart accounts are user accounts: they are nodes of no graph, and an approval or a transfer with a user account at either end is an edge of no graph. The allowance layer holds the latest allowances between contracts, each capped at the owner's balance, and the transfer layer holds the token transfers between contracts; an approval is the event that sets an allowance.

An allowance edge $u\to v$ exists when, for at least one token, the latest \texttt{Approval} event from owner contract $u$ to spender contract $v$ in the score window carries a positive value and $u$ holds a positive balance of that token at the freeze. The edge counts the allowance only up to that balance, since a spender cannot draw more than the owner holds: the capped allowance is $a^{\mathrm{eff}}=\min(a^{\star},\,\mathrm{bal}_u)$, in US dollars at the price of the freeze day, with $a^{\star}$ the latest allowance. We call this the balance cap. A latest value of zero deletes the pair, and an allowance whose owner holds no balance is capped to zero and is no edge. This is what the pipeline measures, and it differs from the allowance in force in three ways: allowances set before the start of the score window and not re-emitted inside it are invisible; a finite allowance that \texttt{transferFrom} has since drawn down keeps its approved value, because a token need not emit an \texttt{Approval} event when an allowance is spent; and an owner who approves an allowance manager such as Uniswap's Permit2 appears with an edge to that manager only. A transfer edge $u\to v$ exists when contract $u$ sent contract $v$ at least one transfer with a positive amount in the score window. Native ether moves without \texttt{Transfer} logs and enters the transfer layer only as wrapped ether.

The operator studied here weights both layers by amounts:
\begin{equation}
\tilde w_A(u,v)=\sum_{\text{token}} \min\bigl(a^{\mathrm{eff}}(u,v,\text{token}),\,U\bigr),
\label{eq:layerA}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\tilde w_T(u,v)=\sum_{e:\,u\to v} x_e\,\sigma(\Delta t_e),
\label{eq:layerT}
\end{equation}
where $x_e$ is the amount of transfer $e$ in US dollars at the price of its day and $\sigma(\Delta t)=1/(1+\exp(k(\Delta t-t_0)))$ a logistic discount on the age $\Delta t$ in days, the form used by AWP \cite{do2023awp}; that paper fixes no parameter values, and $k=0.01$ and $t_0=180$ are our choices. Ages are measured back from the freeze. The allowance layer is not discounted. The bound $U=16{,}000$ dollars limits what one allowance can add to a row (Section~\ref{sec:constants}). The plan fixed raw token base units, every ERC-20 token, no cap and graphs that hold every account of the cohort's events; that is the registered analysis. Write $o_A(u)$ and $o_T(u)$ for the out-strengths and $P_A$, $P_T$ for the row-normalized matrices, whose rows are zero where a node has no out-edge in that layer.

\subsection{PageRank With a Restart Distribution}
Every score below is the fixed point of
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r^{(t+1)}=(1-d)\,\boldsymbol\pi+d\,P^{\top}\mathbf r^{(t)}+d\,\bigl(\mathbf 1_{\mathrm{dang}}^{\top}\mathbf r^{(t)}\bigr)\,\boldsymbol\pi,
\label{eq:pr}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the transition matrix, $\boldsymbol\pi$ the restart distribution over the contracts of the graph, and $\mathbf 1_{\mathrm{dang}}$ the indicator of nodes with an all-zero row, whose mass is sent back through $\boldsymbol\pi$. We use $d=0.85$, start from the uniform vector, and stop when the $\ell_1$ change falls below $10^{-8}$ or after 300 iterations.

\subsection{Coupled PageRank}
\cpr{} mixes the two rows of each node, renormalizing over the layers in which the node has out-edges:
\begin{equation}
P^{(\lambda)}_{u\cdot}=\alpha_A(u)\,P_{A,u\cdot}+\alpha_T(u)\,P_{T,u\cdot},
\label{eq:cpr}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\alpha_A(u)=\frac{\lambda\,\mathbf 1[o_A(u)>0]}{D_\lambda(u)},\quad
\alpha_T(u)=\frac{(1-\lambda)\,\mathbf 1[o_T(u)>0]}{D_\lambda(u)},
\label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
with $D_\lambda(u)=\lambda\,\mathbf 1[o_A(u)>0]+(1-\lambda)\,\mathbf 1[o_T(u)>0]$. A node with out-edges in one layer follows that layer with weight one, and a node with none in either is dangling. Restarts are uniform over the contracts. The primary setting $\lambda=0.5$ and the sensitivity values $0.25$ and $0.75$ were fixed in the plan (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

At $\lambda=1$ the walk reads the allowance layer alone, and the contracts that only the transfer layer contains are no part of its graph; at $\lambda=0$ it reads the transfer layer alone. We call these the allowance walk and the transfer walk; they are the comparators of both rules. The family is discontinuous at both ends. For every $\lambda$ strictly between 0 and 1, a node whose out-edges are all transfers, such as a contract that sends transfers but approves no one, follows the transfer layer, whereas at $\lambda=1$ the same node is dangling or absent. Symmetrically, a node whose out-edges are all allowances follows the allowance layer at every interior $\lambda$ but is dangling or absent at $\lambda=0$. Unit tests on small fixtures check that each end point reproduces its single-layer walk.

\subsection{Seeded Ablation}
\spr{} keeps the transfer walk and draws its restarts from the allowance walk's score $\mathbf r_A$, restricted to $V_T$ and renormalized:
\begin{equation}
\pi_u=\frac{r_{A,u}}{\sum_{v\in V_T} r_{A,v}},\qquad u\in V_T,
\label{eq:spr}
\end{equation}
with $r_{A,u}=0$ for $u\notin V_A$ and a uniform fallback if the denominator vanishes. Allowances then decide where the walker lands after a jump, and transfers decide where it goes between jumps. Comparing \spr{} with \cpr{} separates the allowance edge as a route from the allowance edge as a seed.

\subsection{Reference Scores}
Two walks and four counts serve as references. AWP \cite{do2023awp} walks the transfer layer with weights $\sigma(\Delta t_e)V(x_e)$ summed over transfers, where $V(z)=2/(1+e^{-bz})-1$ is a bounded value transform, and restarts at each contract in proportion to its recent sending activity. EndorseRank \cite{kwon2026companion} walks the allowance layer with the same bounded weights, applied to the capped allowance and the age of its approval, and with uniform restarts; a variant restarts in proportion to approving activity. In the main analysis $b=\ln 3/m$, so that an amount of $m$ weighs one half, with $m_T=160$ dollars for transfers and $m_A=0.071$ dollars for capped allowances (Section~\ref{sec:constants}); in the plan $b=1$ on raw base units, which makes $V(z)$ within $10^{-4}$ of one for any amount of ten base units or more. Both reference walks bound the weight of each event, whereas \cpr{} lets amounts matter up to $U$. The counts are taken on the contract graph at the freeze: the in-approve degree, the number of owner contracts that hold a capped allowance to the contract; the in-approve value, the sum of $\tilde w_A$ over those owners, that is, of their capped allowances counted up to $U$ each; the transfer in-degree, the number of other contracts that sent it a transfer; and the union in-degree, the number of other contracts with an edge to it in either layer, which counts, apart from self-pairs, the edges that \cpr{} walks. No count reads a user account.

\subsection{Where the Weight Acts}
Let $B=\{u: o_A(u)>0 \text{ and } o_T(u)>0\}$ be the set of nodes with out-edges in both layers.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:localize}
For $\lambda\in(0,1)$ and uniform $\boldsymbol\pi$, the rows of $P^{(\lambda)}$ outside $B$ do not depend on $\lambda$, and the derivative $\mathbf r'(\lambda)$ of the stationary vector of (\ref{eq:pr}) with respect to $\lambda$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r'(\lambda)=d\,\bigl(I-d\,G_\lambda^{\top}\bigr)^{-1}\sum_{u\in B} r_u\bigl(\mathbf p_{A,u}-\mathbf p_{T,u}\bigr),
\label{eq:sens}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf p_{A,u}$ and $\mathbf p_{T,u}$ are the transposed rows of $P_A$ and $P_T$ and $G_\lambda$ is $P^{(\lambda)}$ with each dangling row set to $\boldsymbol\pi^{\top}$. Hence $\|\mathbf r'(\lambda)\|_1\le 2d\,r(B)/(1-d)$ with $r(B)=\sum_{u\in B}r_u$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
Outside $B$ one indicator in (\ref{eq:alpha}) is zero, so the row is a single layer's row or zero whatever $\lambda$ is, and the dangling set does not change on $(0,1)$. For $u\in B$, $D_\lambda(u)=1$ and the row is $\lambda P_{A,u\cdot}+(1-\lambda)P_{T,u\cdot}$, whose derivative is $P_{A,u\cdot}-P_{T,u\cdot}$. Because $\mathbf r$ sums to one, (\ref{eq:pr}) can be written $\mathbf r=(1-d)\boldsymbol\pi+d\,G_\lambda^{\top}\mathbf r$; differentiating gives (\ref{eq:sens}). The matrix $G_\lambda^{\top}$ is column-stochastic, so $\|(I-dG_\lambda^{\top})^{-1}\|_1\le 1/(1-d)$, and each difference of two probability rows has $\ell_1$ norm at most 2.
\end{IEEEproof}

The proposition says where the weight can act, not that its action is small: when $r(B)$ is large the bound exceeds the trivial bound of 2 on the $\ell_1$ distance between two distributions. In the contract graph few nodes can belong to $B$, because only an owner contract has an allowance out-edge: the allowance layer has 9,459 owner contracts at the freeze of 30 June 2026 and 10,153 at that of 31 March 2026, in union graphs of 1,299,438 and 1,111,554 contracts (Table~\ref{tab:graph}). We did not compute $r(B)$; Appendix~\ref{app:explore} reports how far the rankings move between interior values of $\lambda$.

\subsection{The Allowance Walk on Depth-One Spenders}
\label{sec:closed}
In the allowance layer between contracts most nodes either only grant approvals or only receive them: at the freeze of 30 June 2026 it has 9,459 owner contracts and 100,112 spender contracts on about 109,000 nodes, so few contracts are both. The companion study derives a closed form for this structure \cite{kwon2026companion}; we restate it with its proof.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:closed}
Under (\ref{eq:pr}) with uniform $\boldsymbol\pi$, every node without in-edges has the same score $c$, and a node $v$ whose in-neighbors all lack in-edges scores $r_v=c\,(1+d\,\delta(v))$, where $\delta(v)=\sum_u w(u,v)/o(u)$ is its owner-normalized in-strength.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
With uniform restarts and dangling mass $r_D$, (\ref{eq:pr}) reads $r_v=(1-d+d\,r_D)/|V|+d\sum_u r_u\,w(u,v)/o(u)$. A node without in-edges receives only the first term, which we call $c$. If every in-neighbor $u$ of $v$ lacks in-edges, then $r_u=c$, and substituting gives $r_v=c+d\,c\,\delta(v)$.
\end{IEEEproof}

The statement holds for any edge weights, so it covers EndorseRank and \cpr{} at $\lambda=1$, each with its own $\delta$. On the EndorseRank graph at the freeze of 30 June 2026, 926 contracts of the matched cohort have an allowance in-edge. Of these, 651 have depth one, and the computed score lies within 1\% of the closed form for every one of them; over all 926 the share is 80.3\%, and 33.8\% of the deeper ones \cite{kwon2026companion}. In the registered analysis the figures are 20,942 of 26,512, every one of them, 86.4\% and 35.1\%. For most of these spenders the allowance walk is therefore a reweighted count of approving owners, and \cpr{} inherits that limit wherever it follows the allowance layer.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}
One iteration of (\ref{eq:pr}) costs $O(|E|)$ \cite{leskovec2020mining}, and \cpr{} walks the union of both edge sets. Table~\ref{tab:bench} reports the solver time of each score at the freeze of 30 June 2026, with $|V|$ and $|E|$ of each solver graph. In the main analysis the timed graph is the whole contract graph, that is, all pairs between contracts whether or not they touch the matched cohort; in the registered analysis it is the graph of the cohort's events. In the main analysis \cpr{} takes 3.08\,s on 1,299,479 nodes and 2,591,253 edges, 1.09 times AWP's time on 1.004 times its edges; in the registered analysis 105.51\,s on 25,304,106 nodes and 71,375,442 edges, 1.51 times AWP's time on 1.19 times its edges. EndorseRank is the fastest score because its graph is the sparsest: the allowance graph holds 4.5\% of the transfer graph's edges in the main analysis and 37.4\% in the registered one. The time ratio of AWP to EndorseRank, 27.9 and 2.36, combines that edge ratio (22.4 and 2.68) with the iteration counts of Table~\ref{tab:bench}: AWP needs 98 iterations to EndorseRank's 56 in the main analysis and 93 to 84 in the registered one. The speed reflects the smaller edge count and the iterations that graph needs, not the algorithm, and any walk that uses the transfer layer bears the cost of its edges. Times are means of five runs after one untimed warm-up on one machine (Intel Core Ultra~9 185H, 64\,GB of memory, Windows~11, Python 3.11.9, NumPy 2.3.2, SciPy 1.16.1).

\section{Pre-Specified Rules}
\label{sec:protocol}

Table~\ref{tab:timeline} lists the commitments in the order in which they were made and what was known at each step. Three features of this history matter for interpretation. First, Rule~A and the coupled operator were fixed before any log of this study was extracted, so no score or label of this study had been computed. Second, the comparison that motivated Rule~B was noticed in the first window after its labels were known, so the evidence from that window is exploratory. Third, the main analysis was defined after every result of the registered analysis was known; it applies the same rules and thresholds, but its verdicts are not registered tests. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports it first, with the registered analysis beside it.

\input{tab_timeline}

\subsection{Rule A (Analysis Plan)}
Rule~A asked whether \cpr{} beats both layers walked alone. Its primary part used the first holdout window, W0 (scores frozen on 31 March 2026, labels from April to June 2026): at $\lambda=0.5$, \cpr{} should do no worse than the allowance walk on new approval pairs, meaning that the 95\% interval of $\Delta\tau=\tau_{\text{\cpr{}}}-\tau_{\lambda=1}$ contains zero or lies above it (A1), and should beat the transfer walk on new transfer senders, meaning that the interval of $\tau_{\text{\cpr{}}}-\tau_{\lambda=0}$ lies above zero (A2). Its secondary part used the matched cohort in the window of the scores themselves: \cpr{}'s point estimate had to lie inside the 95\% interval of the better single layer on a family of transfer checks and on a family of allowance checks, and \cpr{} had to exceed both layers on the stability family, three transfer-based checks (the inbound counterparty ratio, the transfer tenure in days and the active months). If the primary part failed, the question whether \cpr{} beats a walk over either layer alone was to be answered in the negative, and no $\lambda$ was to be tuned after labels were seen.

Two criteria of the rule are weak by design. ``No worse'' as defined rewards imprecision, since a wider interval contains zero more easily, and the secondary part compares a point estimate with another score's marginal interval rather than using a paired contrast. Anyone reusing the protocol should set a non-inferiority margin for the first and paired contrasts for the second; we report the paired contrasts beside the rule.

\subsection{Rule B (Registration of W1)}
Rule~B asked whether \cpr{} improves on the transfer walk, $\Delta\tau=\tau_{\text{\cpr{}}}-\tau_{\lambda=0}$, in a second window, W1, frozen on 30 June 2026 with labels counted from 1 July to 30 September 2026. This paper applies two of the registered criteria of Rule~B, F1 and F2; under the registration the rule holds only if each of its criteria holds.
\begin{itemize}
\item F1 (spenders; new approval pairs), superiority: the whole 95\% interval of $\Delta\tau$ must be positive;
\item F2 (spenders; new transfer senders), non-inferiority: the lower end of the interval must lie above $-\delta$.
\end{itemize}
Because the rule is an intersection of its criteria, a failure of F1 or F2 settles that it is not met, and testing each criterion at the 5\% level keeps the probability of a false claim of improvement at or below 5\% without a multiplicity correction. Had both held, the two alone could not have shown the rule met, and we would have reported only that both applied criteria hold. The margin $\delta=0.01$ is the half-width of the W0 interval of the contrast that F2 repeats ($-0.030$, $[-0.034,-0.026]$, half-width 0.0040), rounded up to two decimals. It measures the precision of a spender contrast, not a difference judged to matter in practice.

With the rule the registration fixed F6, the primary part of Rule~A rerun on W1, to be reported outside the decision, and two sensitivity analyses. S1 takes AWP as the comparator in place of the transfer walk; the registration defines it without F6, and we report it for F1 and F2. S2 keeps cohort contracts that a graph does not contain as isolated nodes rather than scoring them zero, and we report it for F1, F2 and F6. Neither enters the decision; their verdicts are nominal results with unadjusted 95\% intervals. Scores, solver, queries, cohort filter and bootstrap (400 paired resamples, seed 42) were those of the plan. The script that extracts the W1 logs runs only once the registration and the configuration, with the window's status set to registered, are committed without local changes.

\subsection{The Main Analysis}
\label{sec:main}
After the registered results were known, the revised analysis was defined: amounts in US dollars on five tokens; scores from the twelve months before each freeze; graphs whose nodes are contracts other than smart accounts, with edges read from the whole chain; each allowance counted up to its owner's balance at the freeze, with balances reconstructed from \texttt{Transfer} logs; constants set on the contract graph; and the discovery-order test. This revised analysis is the main analysis of the paper, and we call it the contract-graph analysis; the discovery-order test is reported in the companion manuscript \cite{kwon2026companion}.

Each definition follows from what the scores are meant to rank. Raw base units cannot be summed over tokens, since a USDC has $10^{6}$ base units and a wrapped ether $10^{18}$, and with the plan's $b=1$ on raw base units $V(z)$ is within $10^{-4}$ of one for 99.52\% of the transfers and 99.78\% of the latest allowances of the registered analysis; amounts are therefore valued in US dollars, on five tokens with reliable prices. One year per freeze scores both freezes on windows of equal length and keeps events older than a year out of every score. Nothing has to be deployed for a user account, and who controls one is not measured here; user accounts also hold most of the allowances toward a contract (Section~\ref{sec:cohorts}), so in a graph of all accounts a walk is largely a walk over them. With contracts as the only nodes every node costs a deployment, and every edge records a relation that one contract holds toward another. An allowance beyond the owner's balance cannot be spent, and about two thirds of the positive latest allowances between contracts are unlimited (Table~\ref{tab:graph}), so each allowance counts up to the balance. The constants of the weights are quantiles of the events between contracts in the twelve months up to the W0 freeze, so that no later event enters them.

Rule~A and the criteria F1 and F2 of Rule~B are applied to the scores of the contract graph with their thresholds, $\delta$ and $\lambda$. The main analysis is a deviation from the plan and the registration, defined with the registered results in view and with the labels of both windows known. Every verdict on its scores is a re-analysis, and none is a registered result.

\subsection{What Was Not Pre-Specified}
Nothing in the main analysis was pre-specified: its definitions and every number computed from them came after the registered results. What it takes from the plan and the registration are the rules, the margin, the definitions of the scores as far as Section~\ref{sec:main} leaves them, the definitions of the labels and the bootstrap. In the registered analysis every contrast outside A1, A2, F1, F2, F6, S1 and S2 was computed after the labels of its window were known: the comparison of \cpr{} with the transfer walk on new approval pairs in W0, the \spr{} contrasts, every contrast against a raw count, and every comparison of \cpr{} with EndorseRank or AWP outside S1. The code that built the cohort, the pair tables and the W0 labels and computed the scores and the bootstrap was committed with the registration, after it had run on the observation window; the W1 extraction and evaluation used it as committed. Every check in Appendix~\ref{app:explore} was run after all of the above.

\subsection{Sources and Events}
All logs come from Arbitrum One (chain ID 42161), an optimistic rollup on Ethereum \cite{kalodner2018arbitrum,thibault2022rollups}, through the public BigQuery table
\begin{center}
\texttt{\scriptsize bigquery-public-data.goog\_blockchain\_arbitrum\_one\_us.logs}.
\end{center}
Scan~A copied the logs of the observation window, 1 October 2023 to 30 June 2026, and scan~B those of July to September 2026. Approval and transfer logs are selected by their ERC-20 event signatures and exactly three topics, which excludes ERC-721 tokens.

The main analysis reads from these copies every \texttt{Approval}, of any value, and every non-zero \texttt{Transfer} of five tokens from 1 April 2025 to 30 September 2026, wherever on the chain they occurred: 1,240,275,042 rows that name 25,537,280 distinct addresses other than the zero address. A zero-value approval is read because it deletes an allowance. The five tokens, WETH, USDC, USDT0, ARB and WBTC, are those with the most rows in the twelve months before 30 June 2026 among the tokens priced by a Chainlink market-price feed of low or medium market risk \cite{chainlinknddatafeeds}. Every amount is valued in US dollars at the daily close of the DefiLlama price series \cite{defillamanddocs}, a transfer at the price of its day and an allowance at the price of the freeze day. Each score uses the twelve months that end at its freeze: July 2025 through June 2026 for W1 and the same-window checks, April 2025 through March 2026 for W0. Of the rows, 603,475,784 have a contract at both ends; only these can build an edge.

The registered analysis scores every event of the observation window up to its freeze, in raw base units and on every ERC-20 token, and it reads only the events that name a contract of its cohort as owner, spender, sender or recipient: 389,866,882 approval and 1,538,404,067 transfer logs.

\subsection{Account Types}
\label{sec:types}
An address is a user account if it sent a transaction, if it holds no code, if its code is an EIP-7702 delegation designator \cite{buterin2024eip7702}, or if it is a smart account, by which we mean a Safe \cite{safenddocs} or an ERC-4337 account \cite{buterin2021erc4337}. All other addresses that hold code are contracts, and only they are nodes.

Of the 25,537,280 addresses, 17,477,779 sent a transaction and are user accounts by that fact; apart from an EIP-7702 delegation, an account with code cannot send one (EIP-3607 \cite{feist2021eip3607}). We read the code of 6,230,270 addresses with \texttt{eth\_getCode} at block 513,216,506, the block at which the plan's cohort candidates had been typed: every address that sent no transaction and has some counterparty other than a transaction sender or the zero address, the members of the cohorts, and two audit samples of 1,000 addresses each. An address outside this set that sent no transaction has only user accounts and the zero address as counterparties and cannot end an edge between contracts. Of the addresses read, 2,909,509 hold no code and 1,165,025 an EIP-7702 delegation. A candidate contract is a Safe when \texttt{getThreshold()} answers a value from 1 to 1,000 and \texttt{getOwners()} a non-empty list (46,204 addresses). It is an ERC-4337 account when \texttt{entryPoint()} answers a canonical EntryPoint and its code, or its implementation's code for a proxy, carries a \texttt{validateUserOp} selector and no paymaster selector (87,157). Accounts that this test misses because a proxy hides the selector, or because the getter has another name, are typed from listed code families, 20 code hashes and 7 pairs of code hash and implementation whose accounts answer a canonical EntryPoint; they add 192,656 ERC-4337 accounts. The remaining 1,829,719 addresses are contracts, against 21,877,279 typed user accounts. No call was aborted at every attempt, and none of the 1,000 audited transaction senders held code. A kind of smart account that the rule does not recognize stays a contract (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{Balances and the Balance Cap}
\label{sec:balances}
The balance of an owner in a token at a freeze is the net flow of the token's \texttt{Transfer} logs to the owner up to the freeze, from the public logs before the observation window and the copied logs since. A value that is negative or below $10^{-6}$ token counts as zero. To check the reconstruction we sampled 1,500 pairs of a token and an owner, for each token 150 with a zero and 150 with a positive balance, and compared them with \texttt{balanceOf} at the last block of 30 June 2026: 1,493 agree (99.5\%; the threshold set for the check was 98\%).

An allowance counts up to this balance. Of the 339,282 positive latest allowances between contracts at the freeze of 30 June 2026, 223,892 (66.0\%) are unlimited. The cap sets 222,559 (65.6\%) to zero, since their owners hold nothing of the token, and leaves 116,723 positive, 114,454 of them at less than the approved amount (Table~\ref{tab:graph}). At the freeze of 31 March 2026 it sets 93.6\% of 308,922 to zero. Most allowances that one contract holds over another are thus backed by no balance at the freeze.

\subsection{Constants}
\label{sec:constants}
The constants are set on the contract graph from the twelve months up to the W0 freeze and rounded to two significant digits. The median dollar value of a transfer between contracts gives $m_T=160$ dollars (164.05 before rounding), and the 99th percentile of the same values gives $U=16{,}000$ dollars (16,412). The median positive capped allowance between contracts at the W0 freeze, unlimited allowances included, gives $m_A=0.071$ dollars: half of the allowances that survive the cap are worth less than eight cents. EndorseRank's value weight therefore saturates early, at $V=1.000$ for a capped allowance of one dollar.

\subsection{Graphs}
\label{sec:graphs}
Table~\ref{tab:graph} gives the graph at both freezes. At the freeze of 30 June 2026 the allowance layer has 115,006 pairs from 9,459 owner contracts to 100,112 spender contracts, and the transfer layer 2,580,709 pairs. The union graph joins the two layers, without the pairs of a contract with itself, and counts 2,575,632 pairs among 1,299,438 contracts. The walks keep those self-pairs: at this freeze 25 allowance pairs and 15,596 transfer pairs join a contract to itself, which is why the solver graph of \cpr{} in Table~\ref{tab:bench} has more edges (2,591,253) than the union graph and, with the contracts whose only pair is with themselves, more nodes (1,299,479). We did not identify that contract (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). \subsection{Cohorts, Coverage and Labels}
\label{sec:cohorts}
The matched cohort holds every contract to which at least three distinct owners gave a non-zero approval of one of the five tokens within the score window. The rule counts every approving owner, whatever its type; the cohort member itself is a contract. Of the 6,483 addresses with code that meet the rule in the twelve months before 30 June 2026, 40 are smart accounts and 6,443 are contracts, the matched cohort; at the W0 freeze 6,292 of 6,343 are contracts. The plan's cohort counts the approvals of every token in the observation window: of the 29,022 spender addresses that met the rule, 27,844 held contract code at the same block, while 1,121 held none and 57 held an EIP-7702 delegation designator.

The spender cohort of a window holds every contract with a positive latest allowance at the freeze in the approvals of the score window that name an address meeting the cohort rule as owner or spender; it includes contracts outside the matched cohort that such an address approved. Of the 7,962 spenders at the W0 freeze, 7,864 are contracts (97 are user accounts and one is a system address), and of the 7,995 at the W1 freeze, 7,915 (79 and one). Their first label counts new approval pairs: owners that gave the spender a positive approval during the label window although the pair held no positive allowance at the freeze. Their second label counts new transfer senders: addresses other than the spender that sent it a transfer during the label window but had not done so between the start of the score window and the freeze. The labels are counted on the same events as the spender list, without the balance cap, and they count every account: 90.4\% of the 748,258 new approval pairs of W0 and 87.9\% of the 702,275 of W1 come from user accounts, as do 86.3\% and 82.3\% of the new senders (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). The same holds for the allowances already in place: of the 3,809,524 pairs of an owner and a W1 spender with a positive allowance at the freeze, 3,611,886 have a user account as owner.

A cohort contract that ends no pair between two different contracts has no place in the union graph, and every score and count gives it zero. That is the case for 27.5\% of the W0 spenders, 27.2\% of the W1 spenders and 33.7\% of the matched cohort, and only 24.2\%, 25.0\% and 19.4\% of each cohort, in the same order, are ends of an allowance pair (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). The graph between contracts thus covers two thirds to three quarters of the contracts it is asked to rank, and its allowance layer a fifth to a quarter.

Contracts that one actor deploys can send transfers or grant allowances only among themselves, and a walk keeps the mass that reaches a group with no edge out of it \cite{langville2006pagerank}; link farms raise PageRank in this way \cite{gyongyi2005alliances}. A check made after every result was known, which decides no rule, looked in the graph of each walk for such closed groups: sets of two or more strongly connected contracts with no edge to a contract outside the set. The allowance layer has none at either freeze. The transfer layer has 6,860 at the freeze of 30 June 2026, with 31,563 contracts (2.4\%) and 4.9\% of AWP's stationary mass, and 492 at that of 31 March 2026, with 2,846 contracts and 0.3\% of that mass; in the graph of \cpr{} they hold 6.0\% and 0.7\% of its mass. They contain 72 of the 6,443 contracts of the matched cohort and 82 of the 6,292 of the W0 cohort, and at most one of the hundredth that any walk ranks highest in a cohort or spender set, so they do not shape the rankings reported here.

Each subsection reports the main analysis first, a re-analysis under the rules, and then the registered analysis, the test that the rules were fixed for; Tables~\ref{tab:holdout} and~\ref{tab:contrasts} and Figs.~\ref{fig:forest} and~\ref{fig:sweep} give both. Two facts frame every out-of-window number of the main analysis (Section~\ref{sec:cohorts}). Roughly 27\% of each spender cohort has no edge in the union graph, so every score and count gives it zero, and a contrast over a whole cohort also reflects how two scores order the contracts with an edge against those without one. And the labels count mostly user accounts, which no score reads. The coefficients are accordingly small.

\subsection{Same Window: Rule A's Secondary Part Fails}
\label{sec:res-window}
On the matched cohort, \cpr{} at $\lambda=0.5$ lies between its two layers on every family. In the main analysis it reaches 0.738 $[0.729,0.747]$ on the transfer family, below the transfer walk's interval $[0.799,0.809]$; 0.433 $[0.419,0.445]$ on the allowance family, far below the allowance walk's interval $[0.827,0.850]$; and 0.574 $[0.564,0.585]$ on the stability family, above the allowance walk (0.222 $[0.207,0.238]$) but below the transfer walk (0.637 $[0.628,0.646]$). The registered scores give the same pattern: 0.296 $[0.289,0.303]$ against $[0.522,0.534]$, 0.418 $[0.412,0.423]$ against $[0.506,0.517]$, and 0.129 $[0.124,0.134]$ between 0.020 $[0.015,0.026]$ and 0.311 $[0.304,0.317]$. All three parts of the secondary criterion therefore fail in both analyses. The paired contrasts agree: \cpr{} minus the transfer walk on the transfer family is $-0.066$ $[-0.074,-0.058]$ in the main and $-0.232$ $[-0.239,-0.224]$ in the registered analysis, and \cpr{} minus the allowance walk on the allowance family $-0.406$ $[-0.418,-0.393]$ and $-0.094$ $[-0.099,-0.088]$. \subsection{W1: Rule B Is Not Met}

\subsection{The Weight Sweep}
\label{sec:sweep}
Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} plots each label against $\lambda$. On new approval pairs the first step is the large one: switching the allowance layer on lifts \cpr{} from 0.030 to 0.103 in the W1 of the main analysis and from 0.136 to 0.235 in the registered W1, and the interior values differ by less than 0.003. On new transfer senders the interior curve falls slowly as $\lambda$ rises (0.239, 0.236, 0.234 in the main W1; 0.240, 0.235, 0.228 in the registered one), and every interior value lies below the transfer walk. The two analyses differ at the upper end. In the main analysis the allowance walk ranks new approval pairs above every interior value (0.147 against 0.104 in W1), so the interior of the family lies below both end points, each on its own label; in the registered analysis the interior lay above the allowance walk (0.237 against 0.227). In both, the allowance walk falls far on new senders (0.064 and 0.133), because at $\lambda=1$ the transfer rows of contracts without allowance out-edges are no part of the walk. The F1 gain over the transfer walk is thus a property of switching the allowance layer on, not of the value $\lambda=0.5$, and no interior value recovers the transfer walk's ranking of later senders. Appendix~\ref{app:explore} gives the paired steps from $\lambda=0$ to 0.25, from 0.25 to 0.75 and from 0.75 to 1, and the rank agreement between these values.

\subsection{Seeded Restarts}
\spr{} stays close to the transfer walk on new senders and far below the allowance walk on new approval pairs. In W0, \spr{} minus the allowance walk on new approval pairs is $-0.077$ $[-0.098,-0.055]$ in the main and $-0.051$ $[-0.060,-0.043]$ in the registered analysis, and \spr{} minus the transfer walk on new senders is $-0.003$ $[-0.013,0.008]$ and $-0.009$ $[-0.012,-0.006]$. Its restarts lift its coefficient on new approval pairs only part of the way from the transfer walk toward the allowance walk (0.091 against 0.070 and 0.167 in the main W0; 0.177 against 0.142 and 0.228 in the registered one; no paired contrast with the transfer walk was computed). With $d=0.85$ only 15\% of the mass, plus the dangling mass, passes through the restart vector. In contrasts computed after the labels were known, \cpr{} ranks new approval pairs above \spr{} in both windows of the main analysis ($+0.051$ $[0.038,0.064]$ and $+0.049$ $[0.036,0.063]$); on new senders it lies slightly below \spr{} in W0 ($-0.011$ $[-0.021,-0.0004]$) and level with it in W1 ($-0.006$ $[-0.015,0.002]$). Allowance edges thus help the walk on later approvals more when they carry it, fully in the allowance walk and mixed in \cpr{}, than when they only decide where it restarts.

\subsection{Walks Against Counts}
\label{sec:counts}
In the main analysis no walk ranked the spenders on new approval pairs as well as the in-approve degree. EndorseRank falls short of it by $-0.036$ $[-0.046,-0.027]$ in W0 and $-0.034$ $[-0.043,-0.025]$ in W1, EndorseRank with activity restarts by $-0.041$ $[-0.053,-0.029]$ and $-0.033$ $[-0.044,-0.022]$, the allowance walk by $-0.037$ $[-0.046,-0.028]$ in W0, and \cpr{} by $-0.062$ $[-0.082,-0.037]$ and $-0.077$ $[-0.097,-0.057]$ (Table~\ref{tab:cpr}). The registered analysis has one exception. EndorseRank falls short there by $-0.011$ $[-0.016,-0.006]$ and $-0.029$ $[-0.032,-0.025]$ and the allowance walk by $-0.025$ $[-0.030,-0.019]$ in W0, but EndorseRank with activity restarts ranked new approval pairs above the in-approve degree in both windows ($+0.043$ $[0.036,0.049]$ in W0, 0.295 against 0.252; $+0.018$ $[0.012,0.023]$ in W1, 0.280 against 0.262).

On new transfer senders the result has two sides. AWP ranks them slightly above the transfer in-degree on the whole cohorts ($+0.012$ $[0.003,0.023]$ in W0 and $+0.011$ $[0.001,0.022]$ in W1; $+0.038$ $[0.035,0.042]$ and $+0.020$ $[0.016,0.023]$ in the registered analysis). Among the 4,569 W0 spenders of the main analysis that had received a transfer from another contract before the freeze, the spenders AWP can order, it ranks them below the in-degree ($-0.035$ $[-0.049,-0.022]$); that subset was chosen after the results were known. \cpr{} ranks new senders below the transfer in-degree in both windows ($-0.064$ $[-0.075,-0.054]$ and $-0.066$ $[-0.075,-0.057]$).

The union in-degree counts, apart from self-pairs, the edges that \cpr{} walks. \cpr{} lies slightly above it on new approval pairs ($+0.016$ $[0.007,0.024]$ in W0; $+0.011$ $[0.003,0.019]$ in W1) and below it on new senders ($-0.053$ $[-0.061,-0.043]$; $-0.056$ $[-0.065,-0.047]$). Inside the union graph the directions are the same with larger gaps, and without the balance cap the lead on approval pairs is not resolved (Table~\ref{tab:union}). On each label, then, a count of one edge type ranks the spenders better than the coupled walk, and the walk improves on the count of both edge types only on later approvals and by less than 0.02.

The companion study reports the reference comparisons in full and adds a test of a different design, in which a crawl of the contract graph chooses the next contract by a score computed on the contracts read so far \cite{kwon2026companion}. Its verdict on \cpr{} is also that a count did better: ``Ordered by C-PR on the contracts read so far, a crawl from a random contract of the giant strongly connected component reached the contracts of the universe with the most user-account inflow and those with the most user-authorised value sooner than a breadth-first crawl, but not sooner than a crawl ordered by the number of contract backlinks. A crawl ordered by the number of contract backlinks reached them sooner.'' Proposition~\ref{prop:closed} accounts for the allowance side: for most spenders the walk with uniform restarts is a reweighted owner count, and on this arrival label the reweighting did not help. The count results agree with preferential attachment \cite{barabasi1999scaling,newman2001clustering} and with the strength of degree baselines in link prediction \cite{libennowell2007link,lin2022tracking}.

\subsection{Against the Reference Walks, After the Labels}
\label{sec:reference}
Rules~A and~B compare \cpr{} with its own layers, because only that contrast changes the coupling alone. Table~\ref{tab:cpr} sets \cpr{} beside every other score of the main analysis; apart from the marked cells none of these contrasts belongs to a rule, and all were computed after the labels were known. Against EndorseRank, \cpr{} lies below on new approval pairs ($-0.026$ $[-0.046,-0.004]$ in W0; $-0.043$ $[-0.062,-0.025]$ in W1) and far above on new senders ($+0.170$ $[0.148,0.191]$; $+0.172$ $[0.152,0.193]$); in the registered analysis the W1 contrast on approval pairs is $+0.004$ $[-0.001,0.008]$. Against EndorseRank with activity restarts the picture is the same ($-0.022$ $[-0.042,-0.0004]$ and $-0.044$ $[-0.063,-0.025]$; $+0.164$ $[0.142,0.187]$ and $+0.164$ $[0.144,0.186]$). Against AWP the signs reverse: \cpr{} leads on new approval pairs ($+0.044$ $[0.029,0.058]$; $+0.040$ $[0.026,0.055]$, the F1 of S1) and trails on new senders ($-0.077$ $[-0.087,-0.068]$; $-0.077$ $[-0.086,-0.069]$). Among the spenders with a transfer in-edge at the freeze, 4,569 in W0 and 4,642 in W1, its lead on approval pairs is smaller in W0 ($+0.026$ $[0.013,0.041]$) and its interval contains zero in W1 ($+0.007$ $[-0.008,0.021]$), while it trails AWP on new senders by more ($-0.084$ $[-0.097,-0.069]$; $-0.094$ $[-0.108,-0.079]$).

The same-window families give the matching picture. \cpr{} agrees with the transfer family far better than EndorseRank does ($+0.441$ $[0.423,0.457]$) and with the allowance family better than AWP does ($+0.206$ $[0.188,0.224]$), and it agrees worse than each on that walk's own family ($-0.406$ $[-0.419,-0.393]$ against EndorseRank; $-0.017$ $[-0.028,-0.006]$ against AWP). \cpr{} thus sits between the two single-layer walks on every comparison: it gains part of each walk's strength on the other walk's label and gives up part of each walk's strength on its own.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Construct Validity}
The labels are arrivals of the same kinds of edge that build the layers, so they measure future attachment and favor scores that track current counts; neither is a safety or loss outcome, and ``the contracts users rely on'' is measured only through them. They count mostly user accounts, which no score reads, so a score is judged on demand it cannot see. The node rule depends on detecting smart accounts. It finds Safes and ERC-4337 accounts. With the listed code families applied, 115,084 addresses typed as contracts still have code with a \texttt{validateUserOp} selector but without a paymaster selector, and contracts that a service creates per user or per order are not singled out either. Where such contracts are nodes, the graph holds accounts of the kind that the node rule is meant to keep out. Types are read from code at one block after the end of the data. The allowance edge is the latest approval in the score window, capped at the owner's balance at the freeze, not the allowance in force (Section~\ref{sec:model}); a balance held only at the freeze counts in full.

The allowance layer between contracts is thin. It has 115,006 pairs against 2,580,709 transfer pairs at the freeze of 30 June 2026 and 17,940 against 2,269,073 at that of 31 March 2026, and a fifth to a quarter of each cohort is an end of an allowance pair, so every result on the allowance side rests on few contracts. At the freeze of 30 June 2026 one owner contract holds 98,073 of the 115,006 pairs. The W1 scores and the same-window checks are computed on a layer that this one contract largely shapes; we neither identified it nor repeated the analysis without it, and the two freezes differ so much in this layer that W0 and W1 are not replications of each other on the allowance side. Coverage limits the other side: the contracts outside the union graph, about 27\% of each spender cohort, are tied at zero by construction, and Table~\ref{tab:union} shows that two contrasts read differently without them. The two analyses differ in graph, weighting, tokens and score windows at once and are not a sweep over any of them, and other couplings, such as layers with EndorseRank's and AWP's bounded weights, are not evaluated in this paper.

\subsection{Internal Validity}
Rule~A was fixed before any log of this study was extracted. Rule~B's hypothesis came from an exploratory W0 contrast, and its margin from a W0 interval. The registration is a commit in the authors' own repository, not an entry in an independent registry. The main analysis was defined after every registered result was known and with the labels of both windows known, so its choices could favor one outcome. Two facts limit that risk without removing it. Its constants come from quantiles of events in the twelve months up to the W0 freeze, so no event of a label window enters them. And its verdicts on the two rules, neither met, agree with those of the registered analysis, as do its readings of A2, F1 and F2: A2 fails, F1 holds and F2 fails in both. The agreement stops there. A1 and F6a, the contrasts with the allowance walk, read the other way, against \cpr{} on the contract graph: A1 is $-0.025$ $[-0.045,-0.004]$ in the main analysis and $+0.008$ $[0.003,0.013]$ in the registered one, and F6a $-0.043$ $[-0.061,-0.024]$ and $+0.010$ $[0.005,0.014]$. Reporting it first is likewise a choice made after the results; the registered analysis stays in the tables and figures of the rules as the test that the rules were fixed for. Rule~B is read here through two of its criteria, which can show that it is not met and could not have shown that it is. The rule that scores absent contracts as zero is a pipeline choice; S2 shows that it changes no reading, and Table~\ref{tab:union} shows what it does to the contrasts with the allowance walk.

\subsection{Statistical Conclusion Validity}
Rule-bound intervals come from 400 paired resamples, as fixed in the plan. Across 50 further seeds no verdict of A1, A2, F1 or F2 changes in either analysis; the interval end nearest to its threshold, the upper end of A1 in the main analysis, stays below zero in every seed (Appendix~\ref{app:explore}). The many contrasts reported beside the rules are nominal and should not be read as a family of tests. The contract bootstrap treats spenders as independent although one new owner can approve several spenders. The labels are sparse: 1,491 of the 7,915 W1 spenders gained a new approval pair and 1,369 a new sender, so each coefficient rests largely on how those subsets are ordered.

\subsection{External Validity}
The evidence comes from one rollup, five tokens, contracts approved by at least three owners, scores built from the year before each freeze (from every event since October 2023 in the registered analysis), two three-month label windows and account types read at one block. A graph of contracts on another chain, or with other tokens, can have a thicker allowance layer and behave differently. Only fixed global weights with uniform teleportation were tested; node-dependent or learned weights, mixtures of stationary vectors, interlayer copies of nodes and per-layer damping remain open.

\section{Implications for Wallets, Explorers and Integrations}
\label{sec:implications}

A wallet that warns before an approval, an explorer that orders contracts and a team that chooses contracts to integrate can take four points from these results, each at the size the results support. First, the coefficients are small. The best score on each label reaches about 0.2 on later approvals and about 0.3 on later senders, on labels that count mostly user accounts, so any of these scores is a weak hint for a human reader and not a basis for an automatic decision. Second, the cheapest scores were hard to beat. Counting the owner contracts that hold a capped allowance to a contract anticipated its later approvals better than every walk of the main analysis, the coupled walk included, and counting the contracts that send it transfers anticipated its later senders better than the coupled walk, with no solver and no restart vector to defend. AWP was slightly ahead of the transfer in-degree on the whole cohorts and, in the first window, behind it among the contracts it can order. Third, folding the two relations into one stationary distribution beat neither relation walked alone: mixing an allowance layer into a transfer walk moved the ranking mainly by switching the layer on, and the gain on later approvals came with a loss on later senders larger than the registered margin. An interface that wants both signals can show the two counts, or AWP and an approval count, side by side. Fourth, a ranking read from edges between contracts is silent on the contracts that only user accounts touch. About 27\% of each spender cohort has no such edge, and three quarters have no allowance edge; an interface should show these contracts as unranked, not as ranked low. Beside these points, approval logs are not allowance state: most positive allowances between contracts are backed by no balance at the freeze, and an integration that acts on an allowance should read the allowance and the balance from contract state at the decision block.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Allowances and transfers give two readings of the same contracts. On graphs whose nodes are contracts only, a walk that blends the two at every node did not beat either layer walked alone. Under the rule fixed in the plan before this study extracted any log, applied to the contract graph as a re-analysis, \cpr{} ranked later approvals below the allowance walk ($-0.025$ $[-0.045,-0.004]$) and later senders below the transfer walk ($-0.014$ $[-0.019,-0.008]$) in the first window. In the window registered before this study extracted it, \cpr{} repeated its gain over the transfer walk on later approvals ($+0.074$ $[0.060,0.087]$) but ranked later senders worse by more than the registered margin ($-0.016$ $[-0.022,-0.012]$), and against AWP by more still; Rule~B is not met. The registered analysis in raw token units, the test that both rules were written for, met neither rule either ($+0.101$ $[0.092,0.111]$ and $-0.025$ $[-0.029,-0.021]$ on the two criteria). The gain on approvals appears as soon as the allowance layer is switched on and changes little across interior weights, and seeding the transfer walk with allowance scores recovered only part of it. On the contract graph, contrasts made afterwards placed \cpr{} between the single-layer walks, above AWP and below EndorseRank on later approvals and the reverse on later senders. The counts were hard to beat: \cpr{} ranked later approvals below the count of approving contracts and later senders below the count of sending contracts in both windows, and against the count of both kinds of edge it led slightly on approvals and trailed on senders. These numbers describe contracts that other contracts send transfers to or approve; about 27\% of each spender cohort has no such edge, and the labels count mostly user accounts. Future work should fix in advance and test on unextracted data couplings with comparable weights on both layers, importance labels from outside the chain and a better detection of smart accounts. Whether such a ranking could later serve as one input to credit decisions about contracts was not tested here.
