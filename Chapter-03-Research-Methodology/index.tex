\chapter{Research Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} ended with five gaps. No reviewed method ranks wallets on allowance edges; the transfer-graph baseline is validated against proxies derived from its own input; efficiency claims are seldom tied to edge counts under a fixed protocol; same-window agreement is often presented as if it were prediction; and no study has tested whether an authorization layer and a payment layer combine. This chapter compares EndorseRank with Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) as social reputation scores on Arbitrum One and gives each gap a measurable procedure. In the primary comparison, EndorseRank runs on the ERC-20 allowance graph and AWP on the time-decayed transfer graph \cend{3}; both are solved with classical PageRank \cend{15}.

Methodologically, the study follows construct (domain) alignment. Reputation scores are continuous rankings, and the checks rest on observable wallet-level statistics instead of binary default labels \cend{22}. In measurement terms, every ranking method is one indicator of a reputation construct that cannot be observed directly. Graph coherence is checked with contemporaneous transfer and allowance statistics. For the out-of-window check we use a temporal holdout of future new owner--spender approvals. The alignment statistics then ask whether, on the same wallets, the ordering a method induces co-varies with the ordering each check induces. This design is appropriate because the scientific claim is comparative (EndorseRank versus AWP) and is not a prediction of trading profit or credit default.

Every procedure runs on public blockchain logs inside a fixed observation window. Seeds are deterministic and the evaluation pipeline is open, so the reported tables can be regenerated from cached parquet.

Four principles guide each operational choice.
\begin{itemize}
\item Same-seed comparability: EndorseRank and AWP always score an identical wallet set, so no difference between them can stem from inconsistent sampling.
\item Fixing choices in advance: the observation window, topic hashes, damping, decay parameters, bootstrap resamples and seed, and benchmark seeds are set in a versioned configuration. The coupling weights and the first rule for RQ5 were added on 14~September~2026, before any coupled score was computed. The second rule for RQ5, and the June--August window on which it is judged, were fixed on 28~September~2026, before any log from that window was extracted (Section~\ref{sub:fresh-holdout}).
\item Construction and timing: each cohort's edge lists are built only once, before any timer starts; the computational benchmarks time the solver call, which assembles the sparse matrix and runs the power iteration.
\item Scope: Claims C1--C4 and hypotheses H1--H3 concern EndorseRank and AWP; claim C5 concerns the coupled operator and its ablation. RQ4 and RQ5 draw on the spender holdout cohort; the registered replication adds a trader cohort, and an exploratory trader run is reported beside it.
\end{itemize}

Except where stated otherwise, PageRank means the weighted variant of Page et al.\cend{15}, whose update at iteration $t$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pagerank}
\mathbf{r}^{(t+1)}
=
(1-d)\,\boldsymbol{\pi}
+
d\,P^{\top}\mathbf{r}^{(t)}
+
d\,\bigl(\mathbf{r}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{dang}}\bigr)\,\boldsymbol{\pi},
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the transition matrix of Equation~\eqref{eq:transition-impl} below, $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is the uniform teleportation distribution over the nodes ($\pi_u=1/|V|$), and $\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{dang}}$ is the indicator vector of the dangling nodes, with entries $\mathbf{1}[o(u)=0]$. The third term therefore collects the mass held by nodes without out-edges. With $w(u,v)$ denoting the weight of the directed edge from $u$ to $v$ and $o(u)=\sum_v w(u,v)$ the out-strength, the transition matrix is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transition-impl}
P_{u,v}
=
\begin{cases}
w(u,v)/o(u) & \text{if }o(u)>0,\\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The rows of dangling nodes are zero, so $P$ is substochastic, not row-stochastic. The mass that $P^{\top}$ loses at those nodes comes back through the third term of Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank} and is spread according to $\boldsymbol{\pi}$, as is standard for sparse web and transaction graphs. Filling each zero row with $1/|V|$ gives the row-stochastic matrix of Equation~\eqref{eq:transition}; the second and third terms then merge into one, and Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank} is equivalent to the Google-matrix form of Equation~\eqref{eq:google-matrix}. The iteration starts from the uniform vector $\mathbf{r}^{(0)}=\mathbf{1}/|V|$ and stops once $\|\mathbf{r}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{r}^{(t)}\|_1 < \varepsilon$ or after $I_{\max}$ iterations. Here $V$ is the node set of the graph passed to the solver, that is, the end points of the retained edges, and the final vector is normalized to sum to one over $V$. The pipeline also lists wallets in descending order of score (rank $1$ is the highest), breaking ties by wallet address. These ordinal ranks serve only for listing. The correlations of Section~\ref{sec:statistical-tests} are computed on the raw scores, where tied scores stay tied.

The damping factor $d$ interpolates between pure propagation along the graph (as $d\to 1$) and uniform scores (as $d\to 0$). Its default of $d=0.85$ matches the classical web setting and the AWP baseline\cend{3}. The robustness sweeps vary $d$ with edge weights held fixed (Section~\ref{sec:scaling-robustness}). We set $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$ and $I_{\max}=300$, stricter than typical interactive analytics would require, so that the measured runtimes track the structural properties of the graph and are not driven by early termination.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:data-sources]{Data sources and sampling}

This subsection specifies the empirical space, that is, the chain, time interval, public tables, events, and wallets that make up the main sample. No later step of graph or proxy construction brings in events from outside it.

\begin{itemize}
\item Primary chain: All events in the analysis are drawn from Arbitrum One (chain ID $42161$), an Ethereum Layer-2 rollup with heavy DeFi use and public transaction logs \cend{1}. Staying on one chain removes the problem of cross-chain aliases, where a single user running different addresses on several chains would show up as distinct nodes. It also means that every wallet in the sample faces the same gas fees and network latencies. Arbitrum One is the only source of empirical data for our comparison.

\item Observation window: The study covers six months, from 2025-12-01 00:00:00 UTC to 2026-05-31 23:59:59 UTC. The SQL queries take 2026-06-01 00:00:00 UTC as an exclusive end timestamp, which makes 2026-05-31 the inclusive upper bound. The window is long enough to fit the BigQuery data limits of a month-by-month extraction, yet short enough for the snapshot dates for max allowance to match the date ranges of the transaction history. AWP's time decay is anchored at the last moment of the window, $T_{\mathrm{obs}}=$ 2026-05-31 23:59:59 UTC. An event on the final day therefore has $0\le\Delta t<1$, and one on the first day is about $182$ days old.

\item BigQuery source: Logs are read from the public BigQuery table
\begin{center}
\texttt{bigquery-public-data.goog\_blockchain\_arbitrum\_one\_us.logs}.
\end{center}
One record corresponds to one event emitted by a contract and contains its block timestamp, address, event topics, and event data. Queries filter records by block timestamp, by contract address where relevant, and by event topic hashes. Because the dataset is public, no dedicated archival node is needed and the extraction is fully verifiable: anyone with a Google Cloud account can rerun the exact query against the same table. Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval} lists the queries with their estimated costs and other metadata.

\item Reputation events: EndorseRank uses ERC-20 \texttt{Approval} logs \cend{18}, a set that also covers approvals made through EIP-2612 \texttt{permit} transactions \cend{19}. AWP uses ERC-20 \texttt{Transfer} logs. For both, we keep only events in which a sampled wallet is the owner or spender (approval events) or the sender or receiver (transfer events). Filtering inside the query, instead of pulling every event on the chain, keeps the extracted data volume to a minimum and isolates the subgraph that bears on our cohort's interactions. The extraction runs in six monthly batches for two reasons: (a) each query is subject to a byte limit, and (b) if one month of extraction fails, the run must be able to resume from that point.

\item Matched cohort: Alignment is applied to the $n=5{,}521$ GMX~V2 wallets on Arbitrum One that closed at least three positions (\texttt{PositionDecrease} events) between 2025-12-01 and 2026-05-31. The list comes from GMX event data alone. It is fixed before any approval or transfer event is extracted, and the BigQuery approval and transfer queries are then filtered to it, so the cohort does not depend on the reputation graphs. Because EndorseRank and AWP are evaluated on the same seed set, any difference in alignment or runtime can be traced to the ranking logic and the graph structure; sampling differences cannot account for it. A cohort wallet need not appear in either graph. Of the $5{,}521$, $966$ are touched by no allowance edge and $381$ by no transfer edge ($370$ by neither); the method concerned gives such a wallet a score of $0$ (Section~\ref{sec:graph-construction}).

\item Exclusions: Mempool transactions, off-chain order books, centralized exchange user accounts, and multi-chain bridges all lie outside the data we process. We also make no attempt to resolve multiple addresses controlled by a single operator into one entity. The public log table is our ground truth, and all decoding takes place in the open pipeline.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:collection-strategy]{Two-phase collection strategy}

Data collection and evaluation run as two separate phases. The first gathers the data, and the second performs all evaluation locally. Keeping them apart lets us rebuild the alignment tables and rerun the benchmarks and robustness experiments as often as needed without incurring cloud costs, and other researchers need the same ability to replicate our results.

\begin{itemize}
\item Phase 1 (reputation streams): The matched cohort ($n=5{,}521$ wallets) is fixed first, from GMX~V2 event data alone. We then extract every ERC-20 \texttt{Approval} event in which a cohort wallet is the owner or the spender, and every \texttt{Transfer} event in which it is the sender or the recipient. A separate query covers each month, so if one query fails, the results already obtained for earlier months are unaffected. Every query also receives its own dry-run estimate, which tells us in advance how much it will be charged. Approvals are reduced to the most recent allowance per owner--spender--token triple. When a triple has several approvals in the window, only the last one in (block number, log index) order is kept; an observed allowance of $0$ drops the edge. Transfer amounts stay in each token's raw base units, with no decimal scaling, and zero-value transfers are dropped. The resulting tables contain sender, recipient, value, token and block timestamp, and they are the only inputs to graph construction for both EndorseRank and AWP.

\item Phase 2 (local evaluation): From the preprocessed Phase~1 parquet files we compute, entirely on a local machine, the reputation ranks, the transfer and allowance checks, the alignment metrics (Spearman correlation, Kendall $\tau$), the computational benchmarks (time and memory use), and the scaling and robustness extensions. To form the expanded pool of active wallets, we simply add addresses that appear in the reputation subgraphs (or in supplemental parquet files). The robustness experiments therefore need no second large chain scan to find these wallets. Once the Phase~1 extracts and the supplemental active-wallet parquet files are available, no further BigQuery scans are required.

\item Cost guardrails: Before any remote query starts, a dry run records the estimated number of bytes it will process. A query whose estimate exceeds our per-query budget of $150$\,GiB\footnote{This per-query byte limit is enforced by the study's own pipeline; BigQuery does not impose it.} is aborted. Scanning beyond the budget goes ahead only if the operator confirms it with an extra flag. For each query we log details such as the number of wallets scanned, the rows returned, the total bytes processed, and the query timestamp; these entries form part of our public acquisition log (see Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). These guardrails keep the study's use of public cloud resources responsible and allow the cost of its acquisition phase to be audited.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:event-decoding]{Event decoding}

A log emitted by a smart contract consists of topics and a data payload; it has no field names. Decoding turns each log row into a typed record, which graph construction and check generation then consume. An error in this step would silently corrupt both reputation graphs. The topic hashes and field mappings used by the decoder are listed below.

\begin{itemize}
\item Log anatomy: Each smart contract log records the address of the contract that emitted it, a sequence of topics, and an ABI-encoded data blob. Its first topic is always the keccak-256 hash of the event signature string; among the standard specifications, that hash identifies the event type uniquely. Indexed parameters are stored in the following topics as $32$-byte words (addresses are left-padded), and non-indexed parameters go into the data field in ABI order \cend{18}. BigQuery exposes these fields as table columns. SQL does the topic filtering, and the Python script decodes the numeric fields.

\item ERC-20 \texttt{Approval}: Its topic hash is
\begin{center}
\texttt{0x8c5be1e5ebec7d5bd14f71427d1e84f3dd0314c0f7b2291e5b200ac8c7c3b925},
\end{center}
the keccak-256 hash of the string \texttt{Approval(address,address,uint256)}. The owner and spender addresses, left-padded, occupy Topics~$1$ and~$2$; the allowance value is decoded from the data field as a number of the token's base units, without scaling by the token's decimals. Unlimited allowances (equal to \texttt{type(uint256).max}) are kept at that raw value. We include them because on-chain they amount to a strong authorization. Under the EIP-2612 \texttt{permit} standard no new event type is emitted. A permit produces the same \texttt{Approval} event, so EndorseRank scores count the resulting allowance whether the authorization came from an on-chain \texttt{approve()} call or from an off-chain signature \cend{19}.

\item ERC-20 \texttt{Transfer}: Its topic hash is
\begin{center}
\texttt{0xddf252ad1be2c89b69c2b068fc378daa952ba7f163c4a11628f55a4df523b3ef},
\end{center}
obtained by applying keccak-256 to the string \texttt{Transfer(address,address,uint256)}. Sender and recipient are indexed parameters, and the transfer value is kept in the token's base units. Mint and burn logs, which use the zero address, are retained whenever one of the cohort wallets is involved, although in the filtered extract they are rare compared with the volume of wallet-to-wallet transfers. Self-transfers remain in the events table. They are left out of the Sybil-stability inbound-counterparty computations (Section~\ref{sec:proxy-formulas}), however, because a wallet sending tokens to itself reveals no counterparty of any kind.

\item Address normalization and units: All addresses are lower-cased before the join, so the checksummed and non-checksummed hex forms of an address match. Token amounts are stored in each token's raw base units, as the \texttt{uint256} value converted to a double-precision float. They are not divided by the token's decimals, not converted into USD, and not capped or winsorized, so amounts of different tokens enter the sums on their raw scales. An unlimited allowance therefore enters the allowance graph as $2^{256}-1\approx1.16\times10^{77}$; Section~\ref{sub:endorserank-graph} describes what this does to the transition probabilities.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:graph-construction]{Graph construction}

EndorseRank and AWP run the same PageRank solver, Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank}, and differ in how they define edges. In both, the wallet addresses are the nodes of a directed weighted graph. Each method keeps only the subgraph around the seed wallet set (edges with at least one endpoint in that set, which brings in the 1-hop neighbor addresses as well) and returns a score for every address in the seed wallet set; any address missing from the subgraph is assigned a score of $0$ before ranking. The restriction is needed both for performance and for ease of interpretation. A seed wallet may accrue score mass from outside addresses that approve or transfer to it, yet we report scores only for the seed wallet set, i.e.\ $\mathcal{W}$.

Edges in EndorseRank stand for endorsements, and edges in AWP stand for transfers. An allowance is a deliberate and revocable authorization for another party to spend tokens on the owner's behalf. A transfer is a movement of token value that has already been completed; it may belong to a payment, a routing path, market making or even wash trading, and none of these commits the sender to any endorsement \cend{4,18}. EndorseRank can thus be viewed as treating the latest allowance snapshots as a network of endorsements, much like the citation network of Page et al.\cend{15}. AWP, by contrast, counts each individual transfer as a citation and weights it by how long ago it happened, so that recent transfers count for more than older ones.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:endorserank-graph]{EndorseRank allowance graph}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ denote the set of latest allowances after preprocessing. For each (token, owner--spender) pair, only the most recent \texttt{Approval} event inside the observation window is kept, and a zero allowance deletes the edge. When a pair spans several tokens, its allowance weights are summed across them:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:approve-weight}
w_A(u,v)
=
\sum_{\mathrm{token}}
a^{\star}(u,v,\mathrm{token}),
\end{equation}
where $a^{\star}(u,v,\mathrm{token})$ is the value of the last \texttt{Approval} event for that triple in the window, in (block number, log index) order, taken in the token's raw base units with no decimal adjustment and no cap. A directed edge $u\to v$ means that owner $u$ endorses spender $v$. Damping is set at $d=0.85$. Unlimited approvals dominate these weights. Of the $21{,}585$ latest allowances, $13{,}055$ ($60.5\%$) are unlimited, and in the EndorseRank subgraph $51\%$ of the edges carry one; $3{,}986$ of the $4{,}590$ owners hold at least one. In double precision a finite allowance next to an unlimited one gets a negligible share of the owner's row, so such an owner's row is split among its unlimited approvals, and the finite allowances of the $1{,}987$ owners who hold both kinds have almost no effect. No time decay is applied. Within a pair, on each token, the newest approval replaces the older ones \cend{18}; across pairs, however, an approval made early in the window counts as much as a recent one.

The steps for building the graph and running PageRank are listed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:endorserank}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{EndorseRank graph construction and scoring}
\label{alg:endorserank}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Latest allowances $\mathcal{A}$, seed wallets $\mathcal{W}$, damping $d$, tolerance $\varepsilon$, max iterations $I_{\max}$
\Ensure Scores $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{ER}}$ on $\mathcal{W}$
\State $E \gets \emptyset$
\For{each $(u,v)$ in unique owner--spender pairs in $\mathcal{A}$}
  \State $w \gets \sum_{\mathrm{token}} a^{\star}(u,v,\mathrm{token})$
  \If{$w > 0$}
    \State add edge $(u\to v, w)$ to $E$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $E \gets$ edges in $E$ incident to at least one wallet in $\mathcal{W}$
\State $\mathbf{s} \gets \mathrm{WeightedPageRank}(E, d, \varepsilon, I_{\max})$ \Comment{Equations~\eqref{eq:pagerank}--\eqref{eq:transition-impl}}
\State \Return $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{ER}}[i] \gets \mathbf{s}[i]$ for $i\in\mathcal{W}$ (else $0$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:awp-graph]{AWP transfer graph}

AWP follows the method presented at IEEE RIVF 2023\cend{3}. In that method, a transfer $e$ of token amount $x_e$ made $\Delta t$ days before the observation window closes at $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$ contributes its amount times the decay factor
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:logistic-decay}
\sigma(\Delta t)
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp\bigl(k(\Delta t-t_0)\bigr)},
\qquad
k=0.01,\quad t_0=180\text{ days}.
\end{equation}
The edge weights are sums over transfers:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transfer-weight}
w_T(u,v)
=
\sum_{e:\,u\to v}
x_e\,\sigma(\Delta t_e).
\end{equation}
The sum runs over every transfer from $u$ to $v$ in the window, whatever the token, so raw base-unit amounts of different tokens are added together. A self-transfer ($u=v$) is kept and becomes a self-loop on $u$. The damping factor is again $d=0.85$. The decay curve is the logistic function of Equation~\eqref{eq:logistic}, and Figure~\ref{fig:logistic-decay-methods} shows its shape. Ages in the window run from $0$ to $182$ days, where the curve is close to linear. The weight falls from $\sigma(0)=0.858$ through $\sigma(90)=0.711$ to $\sigma(182)=0.495$, a factor of about $1.7$, and only events from the first two days of the window are older than $t_0$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:awp} states the construction and scoring steps formally.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{AWP graph construction and scoring}
\label{alg:awp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Transfers $\mathcal{T}$, seed wallets $\mathcal{W}$, observation end $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$, parameters $k,t_0,d,\varepsilon,I_{\max}$
\Ensure Scores $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{AWP}}$ on $\mathcal{W}$
\State $E \gets \emptyset$
\For{each transfer $e=(u,v,x_e,t_e)$ in $\mathcal{T}$}
  \State $\Delta t \gets (T_{\mathrm{obs}}-t_e)$ in days
  \State $w_e \gets x_e\cdot\sigma(\Delta t)$ with $\sigma$ as in Equation~\eqref{eq:logistic-decay}
  \State accumulate $w_e$ on edge $(u\to v)$ \Comment{$u=v$ gives a self-loop}
\EndFor
\State remove non-positive edges; retain edges incident to $\mathcal{W}$
\State $\mathbf{s} \gets \mathrm{WeightedPageRank}(E, d, \varepsilon, I_{\max})$
\State \Return $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{AWP}}[i] \gets \mathbf{s}[i]$ for $i\in\mathcal{W}$ (else $0$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The graph $G_{\text{approve}}$ records lasting authorizations and $G_{\text{transfer}}$ records past value flows, so the two graphs differ in density and in sensitivity to time. Approvals are fairly rare, high-intent events. Transfers are common and often mechanical. On the matched cohort the approval subgraph is much sparser than the transfer subgraph (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}), and this sparsity is the structural basis of the computational-efficiency hypothesis (H3). Since the cost of one PageRank iteration scales with $|E|$, fewer edges mean faster solves and less memory for the adjacency structures in Equation~\eqref{eq:transition-impl}.

The implementation details below apply to both methods. Edge lists are stored in memory as (from node, to node, weight) triples. Before the solve, duplicate edges are merged by summing their weights, and non-positive weights are dropped. The solver builds a sparse compressed-row transition matrix, iterates Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank} and returns a dictionary that maps each node identifier to its score. Cohort scores are then aligned to $\mathcal{W}$, with zero filled in for missing entries, and the correlations are computed on these raw scores (Section~\ref{sec:statistical-tests}).

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:coupled-graph]{Coupled operator over both layers (C-PR) and the seeded ablation (S-PR)}

EndorseRank and AWP each walk on a single edge type, and RQ5 asks whether one walk over both edge types ranks wallets better than either. To answer it, we treat the allowance graph and the transfer graph as two layers on the union node set $V=V_A\cup V_T$. Their layer transition matrices, $P_A$ and $P_T$, follow from Equation~\eqref{eq:transition-impl} applied to $w_A$ and $w_T$ respectively. For wallet $u$, the coupled transition row is a convex mixture of the two layer rows, renormalized over those layers where $u$ has out-edges:
\begin{align}
P^{(\lambda)}_{u,\cdot}
&=
\alpha_A(u)\,P_{A,u,\cdot}+\alpha_T(u)\,P_{T,u,\cdot},
\label{eq:coupled-transition}\\
\alpha_A(u)
&=
\frac{\lambda\,\mathbf{1}[o_A(u)>0]}{D_{\lambda}(u)},
\qquad
\alpha_T(u)=\frac{(1-\lambda)\,\mathbf{1}[o_T(u)>0]}{D_{\lambda}(u)},
\nonumber
\end{align}
where $D_{\lambda}(u)=\lambda\,\mathbf{1}[o_A(u)>0]+(1-\lambda)\,\mathbf{1}[o_T(u)>0]$, and $o_A(u)$ and $o_T(u)$ are the out-strengths of $u$ in the two layers. If a wallet has out-edges in only one layer, it follows that layer with weight one, so no step is lost to a layer the wallet never uses. The implementation adds two rules. A layer whose weight is $0$ is dropped together with its edges and nodes. If $D_{\lambda}(u)=0$, because $u$ has no out-edges in any remaining layer, both weights are set to $0$ and $u$ is dangling; its mass is handled by the third term of Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank}. Teleportation is uniform and the damping factor is $d=0.85$. The score vector $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{CPR}}$ is the PageRank vector of Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank} with $P=P^{(\lambda)}$, computed with the iteration scheme, tolerance and cap used for the single-layer methods.

The operator contains both compared methods as special cases. At $\lambda=1$ the transfer layer is dropped, and the operator runs on $G_{\mathrm{approve}}$ alone with the same nodes, rows and teleportation as EndorseRank, so it reproduces the EndorseRank score vector exactly; at $\lambda=0$ it reproduces AWP in the same way. The pipeline's unit tests check this identity on a fixture. The family is not continuous at these end points, however. For every $\lambda$ strictly between $0$ and $1$ the node set is $V_A\cup V_T$, and a node with out-edges in only one layer follows that layer with weight one whatever the value of $\lambda$. At $\lambda=1$ a node whose out-edges are all transfers is dangling instead, or is not in the graph at all. As $\lambda$ approaches $1$, C-PR therefore tends to a walk that still follows the transfer edges of such nodes, and not to EndorseRank; the same holds at the other end. C-PR is not a third edge swap. It is a one-parameter family that coincides with the two compared methods at $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=0$ exactly, and RQ5 asks whether an interior point improves on AWP and whether it beats both methods. The primary value $\lambda=0.5$ and the sensitivity values $\lambda\in\{0.25,0.75\}$ were fixed in the versioned configuration on 14~September~2026, before any coupled score had been computed.

In the S-PR ablation the transfer walk is kept, but the uniform teleportation vector $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank} is replaced by EndorseRank restricted to $V_T$ and renormalized over it. Let $\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{ER}}$ be the EndorseRank vector on $V_A$, extended by $r_{\mathrm{ER},u}=0$ for $u\notin V_A$. Then
\[
\pi_u=\frac{r_{\mathrm{ER},u}}{\sum_{v\in V_T} r_{\mathrm{ER},v}}\qquad(u\in V_T),
\]
so a transfer-graph node without an EndorseRank score receives zero restart mass. If no node of $V_T$ lies in $V_A$, the denominator is zero and $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ falls back to the uniform distribution on $V_T$. The same vector redistributes dangling mass. The result is the TrustRank\cend{17} construction, but with allowance-derived seeds instead of hand-labeled ones. Allowances decide where the walk restarts; transfers decide how it spreads. A comparison of S-PR with C-PR separates what the allowance edge contributes as a propagation medium from what it contributes as a seed.

The edge set determines what the coupled operator costs. Because C-PR walks on $E_A\cup E_T$, one of its iterations costs as much as an AWP iteration plus an EndorseRank iteration, and its runtime is expected to be at or above AWP's. The speed result of Claim~C1 is therefore EndorseRank's alone. S-PR needs 1 EndorseRank solve and 1 transfer solve. Both operators are timed under the benchmark protocol of Section~\ref{sec:computational-benchmark}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:holdout-protocol]{Temporal holdout protocol}

In the spring holdout, scores use every extracted event from the start of the data, December~1, 2025, up to $t_1=$ February~28, 2026, 23:59:59 UTC. Labels are counted on events from March~1, 2026, 00:00:00 UTC, up to $t_2=$ May~31, 2026, 23:59:59 UTC. The cohort consists of the $n=1{,}335$ spenders that hold at least one positive latest allowance at $t_1$ in the extracted approval data. Every score in the holdout table (EndorseRank, AWP, C-PR at the three values of $\lambda$, S-PR and two raw-degree baselines) is computed from events up to $t_1$ alone. AWP's decay is anchored at $t_1$ as well: $\Delta t$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:logistic-decay} is measured back from $t_1$, so ages stay below $90$ days and the weights lie between about $0.71$ and $0.86$.

We count two labels per wallet, one for each construct, so that in the future window each single-layer method is tested on its own construct and the hybrids on both:
\begin{enumerate}
\item New approval pairs: the count of owner--spender pairs, attributed to the spender, that receive a positive approval in the label window but were absent from the latest-positive snapshot at $t_1$. This label measures the endorsement construct.
\item New transfer senders: the count of distinct addresses that send the wallet a transfer in the label window without having sent it one between the data start (December~1, 2025) and $t_1$, self-transfers excluded. It measures the flow construct and is the transfer-side counterpart of the first label.
\end{enumerate}
For baselines we take each wallet's in-approve degree (the number of distinct owners with a positive allowance) and its transfer in-degree (the number of distinct senders other than the wallet itself), both at $t_1$. Each single-layer PageRank smooths one of these local statistics. Because their rows appear in the table, the increment of each PageRank over its own raw degree can be read off directly. That increment answers RQ4; the PageRank coefficient on its own does not.

Uncertainty is estimated with the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:statistical-tests}: a paired wallet bootstrap with $B=400$ resamples drawn through one shared index matrix, which makes every difference between two coefficients on the same label a paired difference. Eight contrasts were fixed before the run. They are EndorseRank minus in-approve degree on new approval pairs; AWP minus transfer in-degree on new transfer senders; EndorseRank minus AWP on new approval pairs, with AWP minus EndorseRank on new transfer senders; C-PR minus EndorseRank on new approval pairs, with C-PR minus AWP on new transfer senders; and the same two contrasts for S-PR.

The first rule for RQ5, fixed on 14~September~2026, has two parts. The primary part uses the holdout: C-PR at $\lambda=0.5$ should do no worse than EndorseRank on new approval pairs (the interval of $\Delta\tau=\tau_{\mathrm{CPR}}-\tau_{\mathrm{ER}}$ contains zero or lies above it) and should be better than AWP on new transfer senders (the interval of $\tau_{\mathrm{CPR}}-\tau_{\mathrm{AWP}}$ lies above zero). The secondary part uses the same window and the matched cohort. Its condition is that C-PR's point estimate lies within the 95\% interval of the better single-layer method on the transfer family and on the allowance family, and that C-PR exceeds both single-layer methods on the Sybil-stability family. The paired contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:tau-diff-hybrid} are reported beside this rule as a stricter check; they do not decide it. If its primary part fails, the second half of RQ5, whether C-PR beats both single layers, receives a negative answer, and the result is reported with its intervals. The first half, whether C-PR improves on AWP, is not covered by this rule; Section~\ref{sub:fresh-holdout} describes how it is tested. Once labels have been observed, $\lambda$ is not tuned.

The pipeline also computed other quantities over the label window: a revoke rate, an approval-then-outbound drain heuristic and Aave borrower liquidations. None of them is offered as supporting evidence, but Chapter~\ref{ch:results} gives the revoke and drain coefficients. Both rise with the number of owners a spender has, so they cannot separate trust from use. On this spender set the Aave events were either empty or measured the wrong construct.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:trader-holdout]{Trader outcomes in the spring window}

The spender cohort says little about the traders who make up the matched cohort, because most of them receive no approvals. On 29~August~2026, before the coupled operator existed, an exploratory run applied the spring freeze to the matched cohort. It scored EndorseRank and AWP for the 4,227 matched wallets in the freeze-date graph and compared them with GMX success labels counted from March to May: the number of profitable closes, the realized gain and the share of closes that were profitable. These labels are defined for the 1,860 wallets with at least three closes in that window. The run used 200 bootstrap resamples instead of 400. It scored no C-PR and had no GMX liquidation label; its other labels, among them inverse-risk measures and Aave liquidations, are in the same summary file. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports it as exploratory.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:fresh-holdout]{Registered replication: June to August 2026}

The spring holdout also showed something that no rule had asked about. Against AWP alone, C-PR ranked the spenders that later gained new approvals far better, and it was level with AWP on new senders. I noticed this only after the labels were in, so it counts as a hypothesis and not as a finding, and it is tested again on a window that had not been extracted when the test was fixed. The design was fixed on 28~September~2026 in a configuration file and a written plan, both committed to the public repository before the extraction (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). The extraction script refuses to run until both files are committed with the status set to registered; a testing override exists, and any use of it is written into the extraction manifest.

Nothing else changes. C-PR is used at $\lambda=0.5$, as fixed on 14~September, together with the two sensitivity values and S-PR. The solver settings, the SQL, the extraction filter of 5,521 wallets, the GMX decoder and the paired bootstrap (400 resamples, seed 42) are those used for the spring. Scores are frozen on 31~May~2026 at 23:59:59~UTC, using every extracted event from 1~December~2025 onwards, and labels are counted from 1~June to 31~August~2026.

Two cohorts are evaluated. The spender cohort is defined as before: every spender holding at least one positive latest allowance at the freeze. The trader cohort is the matched cohort restricted to the wallets in the freeze-date graph, and its membership rests on closes made before the freeze. The spenders keep their two labels. The traders get the liquidation-free close rate, one minus the share of a wallet's June--August closes that ended in liquidation, defined for wallets with at least three closes in the window. It is the nearest thing to a default that these data contain, it is built from neither graph, and because it is a rate it does not reward a wallet merely for trading more. Two things about it were known when it was chosen. One is its prevalence: in the spring window, 1,434 of the 2,962 matched wallets with at least three closes had at least one liquidation. The other is its same-window counterpart. The liquidation-free rate over December to May is one of the three proxies behind the archived liquidation family of Table~\ref{tab:alignment-summary}, and on its own it gives $\tau=0.075$ for EndorseRank and $0.083$ for AWP. It was never computed for C-PR, and never out of window.

The rule compares C-PR with AWP through the paired difference $\Delta\tau=\tau_{\mathrm{CPR}}-\tau_{\mathrm{AWP}}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item F1, spenders, new approval pairs: the 95\% interval of $\Delta\tau$ lies above zero;
\item F2, spenders, new transfer senders: the lower end of the interval lies above $-0.02$;
\item F3, traders, liquidation-free close rate: the lower end of the interval lies above $-0.02$.
\end{itemize}
C-PR counts as improving on AWP only if all three hold, so no correction for multiple testing is needed. The margin of 0.02 is the half-width of the paired interval for the F2 contrast in the spring; a smaller difference could not be resolved at these sample sizes. One weakness of F3 was written down with the rule. If neither score predicts liquidation, F3 can pass with both coefficients near zero, so the two coefficients are reported next to the verdict. Three further results are reported in a fixed order and do not enter the decision: C-PR against AWP on the liquidation-free close rate as a superiority test (F4), C-PR against EndorseRank on the same label (F5), and the primary part of the 14~September rule, rerun on the new window (F6).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:proxy-evaluation]{Construct-check design}

Only EndorseRank and AWP enter the primary evaluation. For each method $M$ and each check $q$ the pipeline computes the scores $\mathbf{s}_M$ and the check values $\mathbf{p}_q$, then reports Spearman's $\rho$ and Kendall's $\tau$ with 95\% bootstrap intervals (Section~\ref{sec:statistical-tests}). Before any correlation is taken, each check is oriented so that a higher value means higher expected reputation, as in the AWP validation protocol\cend{3}.

The same-window comparison is organized around two contemporaneous families:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Transfer (AWP baseline proxies\cend{3}): the wallet's inbound transfer activity.
\item Allowance: inbound approval activity, where the wallet is the spender.
\end{enumerate}
Both families are graph-adjacent. Their inputs are the same event classes that make up $G_{\text{transfer}}$ and $G_{\text{approve}}$, although each check is a local statistic and not a global PageRank score. A strong alignment of AWP with the transfer checks, or of EndorseRank with the allowance checks, shows coherence within a construct; it is not evidence of independent predictive power. Sybil-adjusted activity summaries appear only in the robustness tables and do not support any headline claim.

The temporal holdout of Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol} provides the out-of-window check. Packin and Lev-Aretz\cend{2} warn that decentralized credit scores can become opaque. Publishing explicit formulas (Section~\ref{sec:proxy-formulas}) and keeping same-window checks distinct from the time split reduce that opacity.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:proxy-formulas]{Operational definitions of proxies}

In what follows, $\mathcal{W}$ denotes the matched cohort. The notation is that of the check-computation module in the open evaluation pipeline.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:proxy-transfer]{Transfer family}

Write $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)$ for the set of transfers received by wallet $w$ in the window and $u_e$ for the sender of transfer $e$. Self-transfers belong to $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)$, so a wallet that sends tokens to itself counts itself as one sender, and amounts $x_e$ are summed in raw base units across tokens. Define
\begin{align}
\mathrm{in\_degree}(w)
&=
\bigl|\{\,u_e : e\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)\,\}\bigr|,
\label{eq:in-degree}\\
\mathrm{in\_value}(w)
&=
\sum_{e\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)} x_e.
\label{eq:in-value}
\end{align}
Both are domain-intuition baselines defined in the AWP paper\cend{3}. Their premise is that economically important addresses tend to receive transfers from more distinct counterparties and in greater total value. Unlike PageRank, $\mathrm{in\_degree}$ and $\mathrm{in\_value}$ are local to each address. Neither takes the reputations of the senders into account. Comparing AWP, which is global and time-decayed, with these local baselines tests whether propagation and recency weighting add ordering information beyond the raw magnitude of the inflow.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:proxy-allowance]{Allowance family}

On the allowance side, $w$ is the spender (owner $\to w$). With $a^{\star}$ the latest allowances of Equation~\eqref{eq:approve-weight}, define
\begin{align}
\mathrm{in\_approve\_degree}(w)
&=
\bigl|\{\,u : a^{\star}(u,w,\cdot)>0\,\}\bigr|,
\label{eq:in-approve-degree}\\
\mathrm{in\_approve\_value}(w)
&=
\sum_{u,\mathrm{token}} a^{\star}(u,w,\mathrm{token}).
\label{eq:in-approve-value}
\end{align}
These checks measure the endorsement that EndorseRank was designed to rank. High concordance between EndorseRank and this family is expected, and we interpret it as within-construct validity. As with the transfer baselines, the allowance checks are local; they count and sum incoming approvals without following multi-hop endorsement chains. Evidence from beyond the time window is reserved for the temporal holdout.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:proxy-sybil]{Sybil-adjusted stability family}

The inbound counters leave out self-transfers ($u=w$). Let $N_{\mathrm{tx}}(w)$ be the number of transfers that $w$ receives from other addresses and $N_{\mathrm{uniq}}(w)$ the number of distinct inbound counterparties. Tenure and active months use a second set of rows. Every transfer that involves a cohort wallet is attributed to its sender if the sender is in the cohort, and otherwise to its recipient; a transfer between two cohort wallets thus counts only for the sender. We take $t_{\min}(w)$ and $t_{\max}(w)$ to be the earliest and latest timestamps among the rows attributed to $w$. $N_{\mathrm{mon}}(w)$ is the larger of two counts of distinct calendar months: the months in which $w$ received a transfer from another address, and the months of the rows attributed to $w$. Define
\begin{align}
\mathrm{inbound\_counterparty\_ratio}(w)
&=
\frac{N_{\mathrm{uniq}}(w)}{\max(N_{\mathrm{tx}}(w),1)},
\label{eq:counterparty-ratio}\\
\mathrm{transfer\_tenure\_days}(w)
&=
\frac{t_{\max}(w)-t_{\min}(w)}{86400}\quad\text{(seconds to days)},
\label{eq:tenure}\\
\mathrm{active\_months}(w)
&=
N_{\mathrm{mon}}(w).
\label{eq:active-months}
\end{align}
The counterparty ratio penalizes wallets that receive many transfers from few addresses, a simple Sybil/wash pattern. Tenure and active months track how persistent on-chain activity is over the observation window. These proxies are imperfect Sybil mitigations, because colluding adversaries can still maintain diverse counterparties. They do, however, operationalize the intuition that sustained, multi-counterparty activity says more about reputation than bursty self-dealing \cend{2}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:statistical-tests]{Statistical tests}

Neither the reputation scores nor the validation proxies are binary default indicators; both are continuous and are handled as ranks. Following the AWP validation protocol\cend{3} and Cronbach and Meehl's approach to construct validity\cend{22}, concordance is measured with rank correlations, which are invariant under any monotonic re-scaling of either variable. Each coefficient is reported with a bootstrap confidence interval.

\begin{itemize}
\item Spearman's $\rho$: With $R_i$ and $S_i$ the mid-ranks of the raw score and the raw proxy value of wallet $i=1,\ldots,n$ (tied values share the average of the positions they occupy), Spearman's coefficient is the Pearson correlation computed on those ranks:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spearman-ops}
\rho
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_i-\bar R)(S_i-\bar S)}
{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_i-\bar R)^2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(S_i-\bar S)^2}}.
\end{equation}
Spearman's $\rho$ measures monotonic agreement. If higher reputation consistently goes with higher proxy values, $\rho$ is positive regardless of any nonlinearity in the raw scores.

\item Kendall's $\tau$: This coefficient is obtained by counting concordant and discordant pairs among all pairs $i<j$. Every $\tau$ reported in this thesis is the tie-corrected variant
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kendall-ops}
\tau_b
=
\frac{n_c-n_d}{\sqrt{(n_0-n_1)(n_0-n_2)}},
\qquad
n_0=\frac{n(n-1)}{2},
\end{equation}
computed on the raw scores and proxies. Here $n_c$ counts the pairs that both rankings put in the same order and $n_d$ those they put in opposite order, while $n_1$ and $n_2$ count the pairs tied in the first and in the second ranking (a group of $g$ tied values contributes $g(g-1)/2$ pairs). Without ties, Kendall's $\tau$ is the easier of the two to interpret: it is the probability that a randomly chosen pair is ordered the same way by both rankings minus the probability that it is ordered the opposite way, so $(1+\tau)/2$ is the probability of agreement. With ties that reading belongs to $\tau_a=(n_c-n_d)/n_0$ and not to $\tau_b$, and ties are common here: on the matched cohort, $67.5\%$ of all wallet pairs are tied on EndorseRank. Under certain heavy-tailed distributions Kendall's $\tau$ is also less affected than Spearman by large rank shifts.

\item Why both: Spearman describes a global monotonic association, while Kendall puts more weight on the correct ordering of pairs. The AWP protocol \cend{3} requires both, and we report both so that our conclusions do not hinge on a single family of rank correlation. The score distributions for these wallets are heavy-tailed, with most wallets near zero and a handful of hubs accounting for a large share of the mass. Under such distributions Kendall remains well-defined, whereas a Pearson correlation on raw scores is dominated by the hubs.

\item Family aggregation: A family is summarized by the plain average over its proxies. The transfer-family mean $\tau$, for example, is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\tau_{\mathrm{in\_degree}}+\tau_{\mathrm{in\_value}})$. On a fixed cohort and fixed proxies, a comparison between methods is only a relative statement about those two methods and implies no universal property of reputation. Our conclusions are drawn from the confidence intervals, and no absolute $\tau$ cutoff is used as a decision criterion.

\item Uncertainty: Sampling uncertainty is estimated with a paired wallet bootstrap \cend{34}. We resample the matched cohort with replacement $B=400$ times (seed 42, hard-coded in the configuration) and reuse one index matrix for every method and proxy, so that the resampled statistics are paired across methods. For each proxy $\tau$, each family mean and each inter-method difference, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution form a 95\% percentile interval \cend{35}. Because the population Kendall $\tau$ is a U-statistic, at $n=5{,}521$ the percentile interval is first-order accurate. The number of resamples, however, was deliberately kept small. That choice makes the $O(B\,n\log n)$ cost of computing the tie-corrected $\tau$ across all methods and proxies tractable, but it limits the precision of the interval endpoints to about $\pm 0.005$.

\item Difference tests: Every comparison our argument depends on was fixed in advance as a paired contrast and is tested on the same resamples. The one exception, C-PR against AWP on new approval pairs in the spring holdout, is marked as exploratory wherever it appears, and the registered replication fixes it in advance for new data (Section~\ref{sub:fresh-holdout}). Resample by resample, we compute $\Delta\tau=\tau_A-\tau_B$ and then report the 95\% percentile interval of that bootstrap distribution. A contrast counts as a difference when its interval excludes zero. Five of the pre-specified contrasts compare the two single-layer methods: (i) EndorseRank on its allowance family minus EndorseRank on the transfer family; (ii) AWP minus EndorseRank, both on the transfer family; (iii) EndorseRank minus AWP, both on the allowance family; (iv) AWP minus EndorseRank on the Sybil-stability family; and (v) EndorseRank on $\mathrm{in\_approve\_degree}$ minus EndorseRank on $\mathrm{in\_degree}$. The remaining eight set the hybrid operators against whichever single-layer method leads each family. Specifically, they are C-PR and S-PR each minus AWP on the transfer family, minus EndorseRank on the allowance family, and minus both on the Sybil-stability family. The secondary rule of Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol} is judged on the family means and their intervals; these hybrid contrasts are reported beside it as a stricter check. Because resampling is paired, the contrast keeps the covariance between the two estimates and subtracts it, $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\tau_A-\hat\tau_B)=\operatorname{Var}\hat\tau_A+\operatorname{Var}\hat\tau_B-2\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\tau_A,\hat\tau_B)$. When the covariance is positive, the contrast interval is therefore narrower than the two separate confidence intervals would suggest.

\item Directionality and imputation: Each proxy is oriented so that a higher value means a better reputation. After re-alignment to $\mathcal{W}$, a wallet absent from a given proxy source is imputed as zero. With sparse transactions this is a conservative choice, and both scoring approaches apply it in the same way. After this step every cohort wallet has a value in every score and proxy vector, so each correlation uses the whole of $\mathcal{W}$.

\item Non-inferiority: Two of the three conditions of the registered replication ask only that C-PR not fall behind AWP by more than a margin fixed in advance, $\delta=0.02$. Such a contrast passes when the lower end of its 95\% interval lies above $-\delta$. The test is weaker than a difference test and says nothing about superiority on that label. It is used where the claim under test is that C-PR gives up nothing that matters.

\item What is excluded: The study attempts no causal inference (reputation does not cause future approvals in the experimental sense). It trains and evaluates no supervised learning models on separate training and testing sets, and it runs no hypothesis tests with accompanying $p$-values. We look instead at whether the confidence interval for the pre-specified contrast excludes the null value. The aim is to measure how far our proposed reputation metric aligns with the metrics established in prior work, and to compare how much computation each scoring procedure requires.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:computational-benchmark]{Computational benchmark (Claim~C1)}

Claim~C1 compares the computational performance of EndorseRank with that of AWP. The benchmark leaves out the one-time cost of building the edge lists, because that cost is largely data loading and pre-processing overhead, which a production scoring service that recomputes reputation scores only periodically would likely amortize. It times the solver call that turns an edge list into scores.

\begin{itemize}
\item Timed component: The edge lists for each scoring procedure are built once, before the timer starts; extraction, decoding, allowance aggregation and the time decay of transfers are all left out of the measurement. The timed call indexes the nodes, assembles the sparse weighted adjacency matrix from the edge list, row-normalizes it and runs power iteration with damping factor $d=0.85$, convergence threshold $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$ and an iteration cap of $I_{\max}=300$. In a separate profiling run of the same call on the same edge lists, matrix assembly took about $94\%$ of the wall time and the power iterations under $2\%$; node indexing accounts for the rest. Both steps are $O(|E|)$, and a production deployment would have to repeat both at every update of the scoring function. These parameters are the ones used to compute the alignment scores, which implies that the alignment scores and the execution times are based on the same underlying graph.

\item Measurement protocol: Every configuration is measured in a single session under one fixed protocol. A first, untimed run of the solver warms the internal caches and triggers any lazy initialization routines. Peak memory is tracked on this run only, because tracking it during the timed runs would add substantially to the elapsed time. Five timed runs follow. From them we report the mean and standard deviation of the run times and the average number of iterations to convergence, together with the peak memory seen in the warm-up run and the size of the input graph in nodes and edges. Within the session, results are memoized under a key formed from the edge multiset signature, damping, tolerance, iteration limit and repeat number. The same graph under the same settings occurs in the main benchmark, in the $d=0.85$ row of the damping sweep, in the in-cohort scaling stage with $n=5{,}521$, and in the expanded pool stage, whose induced subgraph is the cohort graph (Section~\ref{sub:expanded-pool}); in those cases the tables report the same values by design, and they are not independent measurements of the same quantity. I list edge counts in every runtime table, since edges are the main structural source of runtime variation in algorithms with $O(|E|)$ per-iteration cost \cend{15}. I also write the runtimes themselves to the machine-readable output (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}).

\item Cohorts: The main benchmark runs on the matched wallet cohort ($n=5{,}521$). For node-count sensitivity I draw deterministic subsamples from the expanded wallet pool (Section~\ref{sec:scaling-robustness}).

\item Fairness: To keep the comparison of AWP with EndorseRank fair, I run both with the same solver, tolerance, iteration limit, damping (apart from the damping sensitivity sweep) and seed wallet set. Differences can therefore be attributed to the density and weight structure of the two graphs (a snapshot of allowances vs.\ a time-decayed transfer history) and not to the implementation. My evaluation manifest records hardware and software details, so absolute runtimes can be read against the experimental machine. For comparing methods I prefer speedup ratios (AWP time divided by EndorseRank time), and each speedup is always reported alongside the corresponding edge ratio.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:scaling-robustness]{Scaling and robustness extensions}

I also examine how runtime responds to node count on a larger address set, and whether EndorseRank remains aligned with AWP when parameters and samples are perturbed. These analyses qualify Claim~C1, by showing how runtime behaves as the number of sampled nodes changes, and let me see whether my family-level conclusions extend beyond the sample I chose. Neither supplies an alternative primary sample. Unless stated otherwise, alignment claims in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} refer to the matched cohort, and node-count sensitivity refers to the expanded pool.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:expanded-pool]{Expanded wallet pool and node-count sensitivity of runtime}

I define the expanded wallet pool $\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{pool}}$ as the union of the matched cohort ($5{,}521$ addresses) and $93{,}559$ additional addresses sampled from monthly Arbitrum One ERC-20 activity in my observation window, taken from the supplemental active-wallet parquet that my sampling script produces. This gives $N=|\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{pool}}|=99{,}080$ addresses for the study. Intermediate-sized benchmarks are drawn from the pool by deterministic SHA256 subsampling with seed string \texttt{benchmark-tier2-v1}: wallets are sorted by
\begin{center}
\texttt{SHA256(}\textit{seed}\texttt{:}\textit{address}\texttt{)} with \textit{seed} $=$ \texttt{benchmark-tier2-v1},
\end{center}
that is, by the hash of one string made of the seed, a colon and the lower-cased address, and the subsample is the first $n$ wallets in that order. The seed string is a constant fixed in my configuration. I report three stages, $n=10{,}000$, $n=50{,}000$ and the full pool $n=N=99{,}080$, all on the same approval and transfer parquet; in-cohort runs with $n\le 5{,}521$ are in Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}. Since the hashing is deterministic, the subsamples cannot drift between library versions the way pseudorandom draws do.

Two features of this design limit what the experiment can show. At every stage the graph keeps the edges of the cohort-window approval and transfer parquet that have at least one end point among the sampled wallets, and the solver's node set consists of the end points of those edges. The edge count $|E|$ at a given stage thus depends on how many cohort wallets the hash order selects, so it does not follow $n$: for both methods the pool stage with $n=10{,}000$ has fewer edges than the in-cohort stage with $n=2{,}000$. The other feature concerns the supplemental pool addresses, whose own approval and transfer logs were meant to be extracted in a secondary step. That extraction did not finish (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). As a result, the full-pool stage adds no edges beyond the cohort parquet, and it reproduces the cohort graph exactly. It adds no nodes either, because a sampled address that touches no retained edge never enters the solver graph; the full-pool allowance graph has the cohort's $6{,}442$ nodes. The pool stages therefore do not measure how runtime reacts to added isolated nodes, and they do not scale the edge count that drives per-iteration cost. Every scaling table reports per-stage $|V|$ and $|E|$ for both methods, and the scaling statement in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} goes no further than those columns allow.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:damping-protocol]{Damping sensitivity}

On the matched cohort we vary the PageRank damping over $d\in\{0.75,0.85,0.95\}$, with the graphs and proxies held fixed. The default, $d=0.85$, is the value used in classical web PageRank \cend{15} and in the AWP baseline \cend{3}. A smaller $d$ raises teleportation and shrinks scores. With a larger $d$, multi-hop paths receive more weight and convergence may slow down. The sweep asks whether alignment on the allowance and transfer families is sensitive to the teleportation probability.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:top-tokens]{Top-token subgraph}

For this check we extract a subgraph restricted to the top-$20$ ERC-20 tokens by combined allowance and transfer volume over the window. EndorseRank and AWP are recomputed on it to gauge their sensitivity to long-tail tokens and possible spam. Should the family-level results flip once low-volume tokens are dropped, the main finding would be fragile to the choice of tokens. Stable results, on the other hand, support reading the full-token graphs as a proxy for major economic transactions.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:sample-size-protocol]{Sample-definition sensitivity}

At each stage, alignment statistics are recomputed on subsamples of the expanded wallet pool drawn with the same deterministic seed. The main sample for every alignment claim remains the matched wallet cohort ($n=5{,}521$), on which its coefficients and confidence intervals are estimated. The pool subsamples differ from the cohort in size and also in composition. Some of their wallets appear in only one of the two graphs, so their score and proxy in the other graph are zero even after alignment, and such ties at zero inflate rank agreement mechanically. We report the per-stage coefficients as a sensitivity check on the sample, not as evidence of stability. The question is whether EndorseRank and AWP keep their relative ordering on the transfer and allowance families when the sample definition changes.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:reproducibility]{Reproducibility}

No empirical table in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} was transcribed by hand; an open, versioned evaluation pipeline generated every one of them. After the Phase~1 extracts are on disk, one evaluation driver recomputes all construct checks, all alignment statistics with their bootstrap confidence intervals and all runtime benchmarks, and writes the results to a single machine-readable summary. A separate stage then exports the tables for Chapter~\ref{ch:results} and Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval} from that file. The driver takes either cached ledger extracts or synthetic fixtures as input, and it has switches for the damping, top-token and sample-definition sweeps. One versioned configuration file stores the observation window, the damping value, the decay parameters, the event-topic IDs, the bootstrap resample count and seed, and the benchmark seeds. In fixture mode the pipeline needs no cloud credentials and runs entirely offline, so the implementation can be checked on a local machine.

The reproducible elements are these: deterministic wallet subsampling by SHA256 hashes; a fixed PageRank tolerance and iteration cap; one bootstrap seed and one resample-index matrix; the proxy formulas of Section~\ref{sec:proxy-formulas}; the benchmarking procedure of Section~\ref{sec:computational-benchmark}, with per-run timings kept in the logs; the coupling weight $\lambda$ and the holdout logic of Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol}, both fixed in the configuration file before the run began; and the extracted manifest files. From those same extracts the alignment and benchmark tables can be rebuilt without any new BigQuery queries. The machine-readable summaries behind all the tables, together with the active configuration, are committed to the Git repository under \texttt{results/}, directly beside the source code. The numbers in this thesis can thus be checked against those summaries without rerunning the pipeline. Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval} lists the GitHub repository URL, the exact commit hash that generated the tables in this thesis, the configuration file and the summary files.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:sampling-bias]{Sampling considerations and missingness}

The matched cohort is a deliberately constructed sample, not a simple random draw from all Arbitrum addresses. To be eligible, an address needs at least three closed GMX~V2 positions in the window; approval and transfer activity plays no part in the selection. The findings therefore apply to active GMX~V2 traders on Arbitrum One. By design, the cohort excludes wallets that did not trade on GMX~V2 in the window, among them dormant wallets and wallets that act only as long-term holders. Some cohort wallets still leave no footprint in one of the reputation graphs ($966$ in the allowance graph, $381$ in the transfer graph), and they receive a score of $0$ from the method concerned.

Data can be missing in a few ways. Amounts are read in raw base units, so no token needs its decimal precision and none is pruned for lacking it; an amount that cannot be parsed is read as zero and dropped with the zero-value records. EndorseRank is completely blind to approvals that emit no standard ERC-20 log event (for example, approvals on non-standard tokens). AWP, for its part, never ingests transfers made off-chain or inside custodial accounts. Both methods are thus biased toward on-chain, standard-token behavior. For an on-chain reputation study that is acceptable, though it matters once results are extrapolated to all the users of a particular protocol.

Adding addresses from the broader wallet universe ($N=99{,}080$) widens the set of sampled wallets for the runtime experiments, although the solver graph never grows beyond the cohort graph (Section~\ref{sub:expanded-pool}). The alignment claim is accordingly always computed on the matched cohort ($n=5{,}521$), while the node-count sensitivity analysis uses the pool.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:implementation-notes]{Implementation notes}

The PageRank code is written in Python, with a SciPy sparse transition operator and NumPy power iteration. Each configuration builds its edge lists once. The timing benchmarks leave out I/O and preprocessing and cover operator assembly and iteration only (Section~\ref{sec:computational-benchmark}). Optional fixture generation is the one place where randomness enters; given the parquet inputs and configuration seeds, evaluation on real data is deterministic. Small floating-point differences between platforms are to be expected, but they would be negligible against the three-decimal-place precision of the $\tau$ metric.

Figures are generated from the same evaluation artifacts as the tables, so the plotted values agree with the tabulated numbers.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:ethics]{Ethics}

Only publicly available blockchain data are used in this research, and it involves no human subjects. The institutional ethics review (Unisa SoC/CAES) was completed before any empirical data collection began. Appendix~\ref{sec:ethics-certificate} contains the ethics clearance certificate (reference [Unisa SoC/CAES ethics clearance ref: TBD], dated [TBD]).

\begin{itemize}
\item\phantomsection\label{sub:privacy-anonymity}
  Privacy and anonymity: An address on Arbitrum One is a pseudonym and does not by itself reveal the real-world identity behind it \cend{2}. Pseudonymity is not the same as anonymity, however, and in theory a determined adversary could tie an address to a person through exchange deposits, online profiles or heuristics. In this work we deliberately make no such links. Our data collection is limited to on-chain \texttt{Approval} and \texttt{Transfer} events, and we scraped no off-chain personally identifiable information (such as IP addresses, exchange KYC records, social media handles or deanonymization graphs). We did not cluster addresses into real-world entities, publish leaderboards of addresses likely to be personally identifiable, or try to join a wallet's transaction history with other databases to infer identity. The published results are aggregate measures only (correlation coefficients, runtimes, family means) and contain no individualized rankings of named participants. Where the main text quotes examples, they name broad categories of user (for example, `spenders with high allowances') and never specific addresses.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:data-minimization}
  Data minimization: The processed tables contain only the fields that graph construction and the checks require, namely the relevant addresses, token amounts and timestamps. Raw logs are kept so that the research pipeline can be audited. They will not be released for the purpose of deanonymizing users. Wherever possible, query filters were scoped to wallets, so as to avoid indiscriminate full-chain dumps outside the scope of the study design.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:data-usage-compliance}
  Data usage and compliance: Every query ran against public data hosted in the \texttt{bigquery-public-data.goog\_blockchain\_arbitrum\_one\_us} dataset and complied with the Google Cloud terms of service and rate limits. Dry-run byte estimates and per-query maximums prevented unnecessary scans. We sent no transactions and accessed no privileged or non-public indexing endpoints, and no smart contract was invoked in any way that might interfere with the normal operation of the network.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:fairness-transparency}
  Fairness and transparency: New participants, low-frequency actors and wallets that grant no public approvals may all be undervalued by graph-based reputation systems \cend{2}. For that reason we examined both EndorseRank and AWP for structural biases of this kind. In the allowance graph, for instance, an address that has received no incoming spend authority is at a disadvantage relative to addresses that have; transfer graphs, in turn, favor addresses embedded in value-flow networks. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses the limitations, among them cold-start effects and the Sybil susceptibility of approval graphs. Every code path, parameter choice and evaluation metric is documented in the open pipeline (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}), so that others can review the work and replicate it independently.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:responsible-disclosure}
  Responsible disclosure and impact: EndorseRank may inform risk assessment in DeFi, but it is not financial advice, credit advice or a consumer reporting product. Lending, trading and compliance decisions still need human oversight. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} acknowledges and discusses the potential for adversarial manipulation, for example Sybil attacks on $G_{\text{approve}}$ or wash transfers on $G_{\text{transfer}}$. The study reports aggregated, cohort-level findings and no wallet-level rankings intended for targeting individual users. Any future operational deployment would require additional governance, appeal mechanisms and fairness monitoring, all of which lie beyond the scope of this research.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:methodological-commitments}
  Summary of methodological commitments: Before results are interpreted, the design has already fixed the chain, window, cohort rules, graph formulas, proxy definitions, bootstrap and timing protocols, and statistical tests. Primary claims compare EndorseRank and AWP alone, on the matched wallet cohort and the spender holdout. The expanded pool and the sensitivity sweeps test efficiency and how far results depend on parameters and sample definition.
\end{itemize}
