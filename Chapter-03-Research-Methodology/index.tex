\chapter{Research Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} ended by identifying a gap in three parts. No reviewed method ranks wallets on allowance edges, the transfer-graph baseline is validated against proxies derived from its own input, and efficiency claims are seldom tied to edge counts under a fixed protocol. This chapter compares EndorseRank with Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) as social reputation scores on Arbitrum One and addresses each part of the gap with a measurable procedure. In the primary comparison, EndorseRank runs on the ERC-20 allowance graph and AWP on the time-decayed transfer graph \cend{3}; both are solved with classical PageRank \cend{15}.

Methodologically, the study follows construct (domain) alignment. Reputation scores are continuous rankings, and the checks rest on observable wallet-level statistics instead of binary default labels \cend{22}. In measurement terms, every ranking method is one indicator of a reputation construct that cannot be observed directly. Graph coherence is checked with contemporaneous transfer and allowance statistics. For the out-of-window check we use a temporal holdout of future new owner--spender approvals. The alignment statistics then ask whether, on the same wallets, the ordering a method induces co-varies with the ordering each check induces. This design is appropriate because the scientific claim is comparative (EndorseRank versus AWP) and is not a prediction of trading profit or credit default.

Every procedure runs on public blockchain logs inside a fixed observation window. Seeds are deterministic and the evaluation pipeline is open, so the reported tables can be regenerated from cached parquet.

Four principles guide each operational choice.
\begin{itemize}
\item Same-seed comparability: EndorseRank and AWP always score an identical wallet set, so no difference between them can stem from inconsistent sampling.
\item Pre-registration by configuration: the observation window, topic hashes, damping, decay parameters, bootstrap resamples and seed, and benchmark seeds are fixed in the configuration before results are interpreted.
\item Construction and timing: each cohort's graph is built only once, and the computational benchmarks time the PageRank solve alone.
\item Scope: Claims C1--C4 and hypotheses H1--H3 concern EndorseRank and AWP; claim C5 concerns the coupled operator and its ablation. RQ4 and RQ5 draw on the spender holdout cohort.
\end{itemize}

Except where stated otherwise, PageRank means the weighted variant of Page et al.\cend{15}, whose update at iteration $t$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pagerank}
\mathbf{r}^{(t+1)}
=
(1-d)\,\boldsymbol{\pi}
+
d\,P^{\top}\mathbf{r}^{(t)}
+
d\,\bigl(\mathbf{r}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{dang}}\bigr)\,\boldsymbol{\pi},
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the row-stochastic transition matrix formed from positive edge weights, $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is the uniform teleportation distribution over nodes, and $\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{dang}}$ stands for the set of dangling nodes (those whose out-strength is zero). With $w(u,v)$ denoting the weight of the directed edge from $u$ to $v$ and $s(u)=\sum_v w(u,v)$ the out-strength, the transition matrix is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transition-impl}
P_{u,v}
=
\begin{cases}
w(u,v)/s(u) & \text{if }s(u)>0,\\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
As is standard for sparse web and transaction graphs, dangling mass is fed back through the teleportation term and spread uniformly. Iteration stops once $\|\mathbf{r}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{r}^{(t)}\|_1 < \tau_{\mathrm{tol}}$ or after $I_{\max}$ iterations. The scores are then normalized to sum to one over all reachable nodes. For alignment, ranks are obtained by sorting the scores in descending order (rank $1$ is the highest score); ties are broken deterministically by wallet address, which acts as the secondary sort key.

The damping factor $d$ interpolates between pure propagation along the graph (as $d\to 1$) and uniform scores (as $d\to 0$). Its default of $d=0.85$ matches the classical web setting and the AWP baseline\cend{3}. The robustness sweeps vary $d$ with edge weights held fixed (Section~\ref{sec:scaling-robustness}). We set $\tau_{\mathrm{tol}}=10^{-8}$ and $I_{\max}=300$, stricter than typical interactive analytics would require, so that the measured runtimes track the structural properties of the graph and are not driven by early termination.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:data-sources]{Data sources and sampling}

This subsection specifies the empirical space, that is, the chain, time interval, public tables, events, and wallets that make up the main sample. No later step of graph or proxy construction brings in events from outside it.

\begin{itemize}
\item Primary chain: All events in the analysis are drawn from Arbitrum One (chain ID $42161$), an Ethereum Layer-2 rollup with heavy DeFi use and public transaction logs \cend{1}. Staying on one chain removes the problem of cross-chain aliases, where a single user running different addresses on several chains would show up as distinct nodes. It also means that every wallet in the sample faces the same gas fees and network latencies. Arbitrum One is the only source of empirical data for our comparison.

\item Observation window: The study covers six months, from 2025-12-01 00:00:00 UTC to 2026-05-31 23:59:59 UTC. The SQL queries take 2026-06-01 00:00:00 UTC as an exclusive end timestamp, which makes 2026-05-31 the inclusive upper bound. The window is long enough to fit the BigQuery data limits of a month-by-month extraction, yet short enough for the snapshot dates for max allowance to match the date ranges of the transaction history. AWP's time decay is anchored at the last moment of the window, $T_{\mathrm{obs}}=$ 2026-05-31 23:59:59 UTC. An event on the final day therefore has $\Delta t=0$, and one on the first day has $\Delta t\approx 181$ days.

\item BigQuery source: Logs are read from the public BigQuery table
\begin{center}
\texttt{bigquery-public-data.goog\_blockchain\_arbitrum\_one\_us.logs}.
\end{center}
One record corresponds to one event emitted by a contract and contains its block timestamp, address, event topics, and event data. Queries filter records by block timestamp, by contract address where relevant, and by event topic hashes. Because the dataset is public, no dedicated archival node is needed and the extraction is fully verifiable: anyone with a Google Cloud account can rerun the exact query against the same table. Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval} lists the queries with their estimated costs and other metadata.

\item Reputation events: EndorseRank uses ERC-20 \texttt{Approval} logs \cend{18}, a set that also covers approvals made through EIP-2612 \texttt{permit} transactions \cend{19}. AWP uses ERC-20 \texttt{Transfer} logs. For both, we keep only events in which a sampled wallet is the owner or spender (approval events) or the sender or receiver (transfer events). Filtering inside the query, instead of pulling every event on the chain, keeps the extracted data volume to a minimum and isolates the subgraph that bears on our cohort's interactions. The extraction runs in six monthly batches for two reasons: (a) each query is subject to a byte limit, and (b) if one month of extraction fails, the run must be able to resume from that point.

\item Matched cohort: Alignment is applied to a main sample made up of the wallets with non-zero connectivity in the reputation subgraph, that is, the subgraph formed by approval or transfer edges inside the observation window. This cohort is preprocessed once the BigQuery extraction phase has finished, which leaves a matched wallet cohort of $n=5{,}521$. Because EndorseRank and AWP are evaluated on the same seed set, any difference in alignment or runtime can be traced to the ranking logic and the graph structure; sampling differences cannot account for it.

\item Exclusions: Mempool transactions, off-chain order books, centralized exchange user accounts, and multi-chain bridges all lie outside the data we process. We also make no attempt to resolve multiple addresses controlled by a single operator into one entity. The public log table is our ground truth, and all decoding takes place in the open pipeline.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:collection-strategy]{Two-phase collection strategy}

Data collection and evaluation run as two separate phases. The first gathers the data, and the second performs all evaluation locally. Keeping them apart lets us rebuild the alignment tables and rerun the benchmarks and robustness experiments as often as needed without incurring cloud costs, and other researchers need the same ability to replicate our results.

\begin{itemize}
\item Phase 1 (reputation streams): We extract every ERC-20 \texttt{Approval} and \texttt{Transfer} event emitted by, or involving, a wallet in the matched cohort ($n=5{,}521$ wallets). A separate query covers each month, so if one query fails, the results already obtained for earlier months are unaffected. Every query also receives its own dry-run estimate, which tells us in advance how much it will be charged. Approvals are reduced to the most recent allowance per owner--spender--token triple. When a triple has several approvals in the window, only the one with the latest timestamp is kept; an observed allowance of $0$ drops the edge. Transfers are preprocessed into decimal-adjusted tables, with values scaled to their standard representation. The resulting tables contain sender, recipient, value, token and block timestamp, and they are the only inputs to graph construction for both EndorseRank and AWP.

\item Phase 2 (local evaluation): From the preprocessed Phase~1 parquet files we compute, entirely on a local machine, the reputation ranks, the transfer and allowance checks, the alignment metrics (Spearman correlation, Kendall $\tau$), the computational benchmarks (time and memory use), and the scaling and robustness extensions. To form the expanded pool of active wallets, we simply add addresses that appear in the reputation subgraphs (or in supplemental parquet files). The robustness experiments therefore need no second large chain scan to find these wallets. Once the Phase~1 extracts and the supplemental active-wallet parquet files are available, no further BigQuery scans are required.

\item Cost guardrails: Before any remote query starts, a dry run records the estimated number of bytes it will process. A query whose estimate exceeds our per-query budget of $150$\,GiB\footnote{This per-query byte limit is enforced by the study's own pipeline; BigQuery does not impose it.} is aborted. Scanning beyond the budget goes ahead only if the operator confirms it with an extra flag. For each query we log details such as the number of wallets scanned, the rows returned, the total bytes processed, and the query timestamp; these entries form part of our public acquisition log (see Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). These guardrails keep the study's use of public cloud resources responsible and allow the cost of its acquisition phase to be audited.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:event-decoding]{Event decoding}

A log emitted by a smart contract consists of topics and a data payload; it has no field names. Decoding turns each log row into a typed record, which graph construction and check generation then consume. An error in this step would silently corrupt both reputation graphs. The topic hashes and field mappings used by the decoder are listed below.

\begin{itemize}
\item Log anatomy: Each smart contract log records the address of the contract that emitted it, a sequence of topics, and an ABI-encoded data blob. Its first topic is always the keccak-256 hash of the event signature string; among the standard specifications, that hash identifies the event type uniquely. Indexed parameters are stored in the following topics as $32$-byte words (addresses are left-padded), and non-indexed parameters go into the data field in ABI order \cend{18}. BigQuery exposes these fields as table columns. SQL does the topic filtering, and the Python script decodes the numeric fields.

\item ERC-20 \texttt{Approval}: Its topic hash is
\begin{center}
\texttt{0x8c5be1e5ebec7d5bd14f71427d1e84f3dd0314c0f7b2291e5b200ac8c7c3b925},
\end{center}
the keccak-256 hash of the string \texttt{Approval(address,address,uint256)}. The owner and spender addresses, left-padded, occupy Topics~$1$ and~$2$; the allowance value is decoded from the data field and scaled by the token's decimals. Large allowance values (equal to \texttt{type(uint256).max}) are also kept after decimal adjustment. We include these unlimited allowances because on-chain they amount to a strong authorization. Under the ERC-2612 \texttt{permit} standard no new event type is emitted. A permit produces the same \texttt{Approval} event, so EndorseRank scores count the resulting allowance whether the authorization came from an on-chain \texttt{approve()} call or from an off-chain signature \cend{19}.

\item ERC-20 \texttt{Transfer}: Its topic hash is
\begin{center}
\texttt{0xddf252ad1be2c89b69c2b068fc378daa952ba7f163c4a11628f55a4df523b3ef},
\end{center}
obtained by applying keccak-256 to the string \texttt{Transfer(address,address,uint256)}. Sender and recipient are indexed parameters, and the transfer value is adjusted for decimals. Mint and burn logs, which use the zero address, are retained whenever one of the cohort wallets is involved, although in the filtered extract they are rare compared with the volume of wallet-to-wallet transfers. Self-transfers remain in the events table. They are left out of the Sybil-stability inbound-counterparty computations (Section~\ref{sec:proxy-formulas}), however, because a wallet sending tokens to itself reveals no counterparty of any kind.

\item Address normalization and units: All addresses are lower-cased before the join, so the checksummed and non-checksummed hex forms of an address match. Token amounts are stored as floating-point decimals adjusted for each token's number of decimal places, and weighted amounts are computed from these values. For ranking, however, the allowance and transfer edges use relative amounts within a cohort, and these amounts are not converted into absolute USD values.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:graph-construction]{Graph construction}

EndorseRank and AWP run the same PageRank solver, Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank}, and differ in how they define edges. In both, the wallet addresses are the nodes of a directed weighted graph. Each method keeps only the subgraph around the seed wallet set (edges with at least one endpoint in that set, which brings in the 1-hop neighbor addresses as well) and returns a score for every address in the seed wallet set; any address missing from the subgraph is assigned a score of $0$ before ranking. The restriction is needed both for performance and for ease of interpretation. A seed wallet may accrue score mass from outside addresses that approve or transfer to it, yet we report scores only for the seed wallet set, i.e.\ $\mathcal{W}$.

Edges in EndorseRank stand for endorsements, and edges in AWP stand for transfers. An allowance is a deliberate and revocable authorization for another party to spend tokens on the owner's behalf. A transfer is a movement of token value that has already been completed; it may belong to a payment, a routing path, market making or even wash trading, and none of these commits the sender to any endorsement \cend{4,18}. EndorseRank can thus be viewed as treating the latest allowance snapshots as a network of endorsements, much like the citation network of Page et al.\cend{15}. AWP, by contrast, counts each individual transfer as a citation and weights it by how long ago it happened, so that recent transfers count for more than older ones.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:endorserank-graph]{EndorseRank allowance graph}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ denote the set of latest allowances after preprocessing. For each (token, owner--spender) pair, only the most recent \texttt{Approval} event inside the observation window is kept, and a zero allowance deletes the edge. When a pair spans several tokens, its allowance weights are summed across them:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:approve-weight}
w_A(u,v)
=
\sum_{\mathrm{token}}
a^{\star}(u,v,\mathrm{token}),
\end{equation}
where $a^{\star}$ is the latest decimal-adjusted allowance. A directed edge $u\to v$ means that owner $u$ endorses spender $v$. Damping is set at $d=0.85$. No time decay is applied, because for the same owner--spender pair a newer approval overrides the older one, and this recency override replaces time-based weights \cend{18}.

The steps for building the graph and running PageRank are listed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:endorserank}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{EndorseRank graph construction and scoring}
\label{alg:endorserank}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Latest allowances $\mathcal{A}$, seed wallets $\mathcal{W}$, damping $d$, tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{tol}}$, max iterations $I_{\max}$
\Ensure Scores $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{ER}}$ on $\mathcal{W}$
\State $E \gets \emptyset$
\For{each $(u,v)$ in unique owner--spender pairs in $\mathcal{A}$}
  \State $w \gets \sum_{\mathrm{token}} a^{\star}(u,v,\mathrm{token})$
  \If{$w > 0$}
    \State add edge $(u\to v, w)$ to $E$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $E \gets$ edges in $E$ incident to at least one wallet in $\mathcal{W}$
\State $\mathbf{s} \gets \mathrm{WeightedPageRank}(E, d, \tau_{\mathrm{tol}}, I_{\max})$
\State \Return $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{ER}}[w] \gets \mathbf{s}[w]$ for $w\in\mathcal{W}$ (else $0$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:awp-graph]{AWP transfer graph}

AWP follows the method presented at IEEE RIVF 2023\cend{3}. In that method, a transfer of token value $v$ made $\Delta t$ days before the observation window closes at $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$ contributes the decayed weight
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:logistic-decay}
\sigma(\Delta t)
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp\bigl(k(\Delta t-t_0)\bigr)},
\qquad
k=0.01,\quad t_0=180\text{ days}.
\end{equation}
The edge weights are sums over transfers:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transfer-weight}
w_T(u,v)
=
\sum_{e:\,u\to v}
v_e\,\sigma(\Delta t_e).
\end{equation}
The damping factor is again $d=0.85$. Figure~\ref{fig:logistic-decay-methods} shows the shape of $\sigma(\Delta t)$ across the relevant ages. Recent transfers keep nearly all their weight, whereas transfers older than the midpoint $t_0$ are down-weighted smoothly.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{\figpath{logistic-decay.pdf}}
\caption{Logistic time-decay $\sigma(\Delta t)$ used by AWP ($k=0.01$, $t_0=180$ days). The horizontal axis shows event age in days before 2026-05-31 UTC, and the vertical axis shows the multiplicative weight applied to transfer value.}
\label{fig:logistic-decay-methods}
\end{figure}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:awp} states the construction and scoring steps formally.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{AWP graph construction and scoring}
\label{alg:awp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Transfers $\mathcal{T}$, seed wallets $\mathcal{W}$, observation end $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$, parameters $k,t_0,d,\tau_{\mathrm{tol}},I_{\max}$
\Ensure Scores $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{AWP}}$ on $\mathcal{W}$
\State $E \gets \emptyset$
\For{each transfer $e=(u,v,v_e,t_e)$ in $\mathcal{T}$}
  \State $\Delta t \gets (T_{\mathrm{obs}}-t_e)$ in days
  \State $w_e \gets v_e\cdot\sigma(\Delta t)$ with $\sigma$ as in Equation~\eqref{eq:logistic-decay}
  \State accumulate $w_e$ on edge $(u\to v)$
\EndFor
\State remove non-positive edges; retain edges incident to $\mathcal{W}$
\State $\mathbf{s} \gets \mathrm{WeightedPageRank}(E, d, \tau_{\mathrm{tol}}, I_{\max})$
\State \Return $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{AWP}}[w] \gets \mathbf{s}[w]$ for $w\in\mathcal{W}$ (else $0$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The graph $G_{\text{approve}}$ records lasting authorizations and $G_{\text{transfer}}$ records past value flows, so the two graphs differ in density and in sensitivity to time. Approvals are fairly rare, high-intent events. Transfers are common and often mechanical. On the matched cohort the approval subgraph is much sparser than the transfer subgraph (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}), and this sparsity is the structural basis of the computational-efficiency hypothesis (H3). Since the cost of one PageRank iteration scales with $|E|$, fewer edges mean faster solves and less memory for the adjacency structures in Equation~\eqref{eq:transition-impl}.

The implementation details below apply to both methods. Edge lists are stored in memory as (from node, to node, weight) triples. Before the solve, duplicate edges are merged by summing their weights, and non-positive weights are dropped. The solver builds a sparse compressed-row transition matrix, iterates Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank} and returns a dictionary that maps each node identifier to its score. Cohort scores are then aligned to $\mathcal{W}$, with zero filled in for missing entries, and turned into dense ranks for the correlations.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:coupled-graph]{Coupled operator over both layers (C-PR) and the seeded ablation (S-PR)}

EndorseRank and AWP each walk on a single edge type, and RQ5 asks whether one walk over both edge types ranks wallets better than either. To answer it, we treat the allowance graph and the transfer graph as two layers on the union node set $V=V_A\cup V_T$. Their layer transition matrices, $P_A$ and $P_T$, follow from Equation~\eqref{eq:transition-impl} applied to $w_A$ and $w_T$ respectively. For wallet $u$, the coupled transition row is a convex mixture of the two layer rows, renormalized over those layers where $u$ has out-edges:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coupled-transition}
P^{(\lambda)}_{u,\cdot}
=
\alpha_A(u)\,P_{A,u,\cdot}+\alpha_T(u)\,P_{T,u,\cdot},
\qquad
\alpha_A(u)=\frac{\lambda\,\mathbb{1}[s_A(u)>0]}{\lambda\,\mathbb{1}[s_A(u)>0]+(1-\lambda)\,\mathbb{1}[s_T(u)>0]},
\quad
\alpha_T(u)=1-\alpha_A(u),
\end{equation}
where $s_A(u)$ and $s_T(u)$ denote the out-strength of $u$ in each layer. If a wallet has out-edges in only one layer, it follows that layer with weight one, so no step is lost to a layer the wallet never uses. A wallet without out-edges in either layer is dangling; it is handled as in Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank}. Teleportation is uniform and the damping factor is $d=0.85$. The score vector $\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{CPR}}$ is the stationary vector of $P^{(\lambda)}$, computed with the iteration scheme, tolerance and cap used for the single-layer methods.

The operator contains both compared methods as special cases. With $\lambda=1$ every mixed row equals its allowance row, and with $\lambda=0$ it equals its transfer row. On a shared node set the two end points are therefore exactly EndorseRank and AWP; the pipeline's unit tests check this identity on a fixture. The empirical layers have different one-hop neighbors, so on real data the end points reproduce the single-layer rankings on the common wallets, though not the full score vectors. C-PR is not a third edge swap. It is a one-parameter family with the two compared methods at its ends, and RQ5 asks whether an interior point beats either end. The primary value $\lambda=0.5$ and the sensitivity values $\lambda\in\{0.25,0.75\}$ were fixed in the versioned configuration on 14~September~2026, before any coupled score had been computed.

In the S-PR ablation the transfer walk is kept, but the uniform teleportation vector $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank} is replaced by the normalized EndorseRank score, $\boldsymbol{\pi}=\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{ER}}/\|\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{ER}}\|_1$ on $V_T$ (a wallet without an EndorseRank score receives zero restart mass). The same vector redistributes dangling mass. The result is the TrustRank\cend{17} construction, but with allowance-derived seeds instead of hand-labeled ones. Allowances decide where the walk restarts; transfers decide how it spreads. A comparison of S-PR with C-PR separates what the allowance edge contributes as a propagation medium from what it contributes as a seed.

The edge set determines what the coupled operator costs. Because C-PR walks on $E_A\cup E_T$, one of its iterations costs as much as an AWP iteration plus an EndorseRank iteration, and its runtime is expected to be at or above AWP's. The speed result of Claim~C1 is therefore EndorseRank's alone. S-PR needs 1 EndorseRank solve and 1 transfer solve. Both operators are timed under the benchmark protocol of Section~\ref{sec:computational-benchmark}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:holdout-protocol]{Temporal holdout protocol}

Holdout scores use every event timestamped at or before $T_1=$ February~28, 2026, 23:59:59 UTC. Labels are counted on events between $T_2^{\mathrm{start}}=$ March~1, 2026 and $T_2^{\mathrm{end}}=$ May~31, 2026. The cohort consists of the $t_1$ inbound spenders ($n=1{,}335$), that is, the wallets with at least one positive allowance in the latest-positive snapshot at $T_1$. Every score in the holdout table (EndorseRank, AWP, C-PR at the three values of $\lambda$, S-PR and two raw-degree baselines) is computed from $t_1$ events alone.

We count two labels per wallet, one for each construct, so that in the future window each single-layer method is tested on its own construct and the hybrids on both:
\begin{enumerate}
\item New approval pairs: the count of owner--spender pairs, attributed to the spender, that appear in $T_2$ but were absent from the latest-positive $t_1$ snapshot. This label measures the endorsement construct.
\item New transfer senders: the count of distinct addresses that send the wallet a transfer in $T_2$ without having done so at or before $T_1$, self-transfers excluded. It measures the flow construct and is the transfer-side counterpart of the first label.
\end{enumerate}
For baselines we take each wallet's in-approve degree (the number of distinct owners with a positive allowance) and its transfer in-degree (the number of distinct senders), both at $T_1$. Each single-layer PageRank smooths one of these local statistics. Because their rows appear in the table, the increment of each PageRank over its own raw degree can be read off directly. That increment answers RQ4; the PageRank coefficient on its own does not.

Uncertainty is estimated with the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:statistical-tests}: a paired wallet bootstrap with $B=400$ resamples drawn through one shared index matrix, which makes every difference between two coefficients on the same label a paired difference. Eight contrasts were fixed before the run. They are EndorseRank minus in-approve degree on new approval pairs; AWP minus transfer in-degree on new transfer senders; EndorseRank minus AWP on new approval pairs, with AWP minus EndorseRank on new transfer senders; C-PR minus EndorseRank on new approval pairs, with C-PR minus AWP on new transfer senders; and the same two contrasts for S-PR.

RQ5 is decided by a pre-registered rule with two parts. The primary part uses the holdout: C-PR at $\lambda=0.5$ should do no worse than EndorseRank on new approval pairs (the interval of $\Delta\tau=\tau_{\mathrm{CPR}}-\tau_{\mathrm{ER}}$ contains zero or lies above it) and should be better than AWP on new transfer senders (the interval of $\tau_{\mathrm{CPR}}-\tau_{\mathrm{AWP}}$ lies above zero). The secondary part uses the same window and the matched cohort. Its condition is that C-PR falls in the interval of the better single-layer method on the transfer and allowance families, and that it exceeds both single-layer methods on the Sybil-stability family by intervals that exclude zero. If the primary rule fails, RQ5 receives a negative answer and the result is reported with its intervals. Once labels have been observed, $\lambda$ is not tuned.

The pipeline also computed other $t_2$ quantities: a revoke rate, an approval-then-outbound drain heuristic and Aave borrower liquidations. None of them is reported as supporting evidence. Revoke and drain had the wrong sign for an endorsement reading, and on this spender set the Aave events were either empty or measured the wrong construct.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:proxy-evaluation]{Construct-check design}

Only EndorseRank and AWP enter the primary evaluation. For each method $M$ and each check $P$ the pipeline computes the scores $\mathbf{s}_M$ and the check values $\mathbf{p}_P$, then reports Spearman's $\rho$ and Kendall's $\tau$ with 95\% bootstrap intervals (Section~\ref{sec:statistical-tests}). Before any correlation is taken, each check is oriented so that a higher value means higher expected reputation, as in the AWP validation protocol\cend{3}.

The same-window comparison is organized around two contemporaneous families:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Transfer (AWP baseline proxies\cend{3}): the wallet's inbound transfer activity.
\item Allowance: inbound approval activity, where the wallet is the spender.
\end{enumerate}
Both families are graph-adjacent. Their inputs are the same event classes that make up $G_{\text{transfer}}$ and $G_{\text{approve}}$, although each check is a local statistic and not a global PageRank score. A strong alignment of AWP with the transfer checks, or of EndorseRank with the allowance checks, shows coherence within a construct; it is not evidence of independent predictive power. Sybil-adjusted activity summaries appear only in the robustness tables and do not support any headline claim.

The temporal holdout of Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol} provides the out-of-window check. Packin and Lev-Aretz\cend{2} warn that decentralized credit scores can become opaque. Publishing explicit formulas (Section~\ref{sec:proxy-formulas}) and keeping same-window checks distinct from the time split reduce that opacity.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:proxy-formulas]{Operational definitions of proxies}

In what follows, $\mathcal{W}$ denotes the matched cohort. The notation is that of the check-computation module in the open evaluation pipeline.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:proxy-transfer]{Transfer family}

Write $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)$ for the inbound transfers to wallet $w$, whatever their sender, and define
\begin{align}
\mathrm{in\_degree}(w)
&=
\bigl|\{\,u : \exists\text{ transfer }u\to w\,\}\bigr|,
\label{eq:in-degree}\\
\mathrm{in\_value}(w)
&=
\sum_{e:\,\cdot\to w} v_e.
\label{eq:in-value}
\end{align}
Both are domain-intuition baselines defined in the AWP paper\cend{3}. Their premise is that economically important addresses tend to receive transfers from more distinct counterparties and in greater total value. Unlike PageRank, $\mathrm{in\_degree}$ and $\mathrm{in\_value}$ are local to each address. Neither takes the reputations of the senders into account. Comparing AWP, which is global and time-decayed, with these local baselines tests whether propagation and recency weighting add ordering information beyond the raw magnitude of the inflow.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:proxy-allowance]{Allowance family}

On the allowance side, let $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{in}}(w)$ be the set of latest allowances in which $w$ acts as spender (owner $\to w$), and define
\begin{align}
\mathrm{in\_approve\_degree}(w)
&=
\bigl|\{\,u : a^{\star}(u,w,\cdot)>0\,\}\bigr|,
\label{eq:in-approve-degree}\\
\mathrm{in\_approve\_value}(w)
&=
\sum_{u,\mathrm{token}} a^{\star}(u,w,\mathrm{token}).
\label{eq:in-approve-value}
\end{align}
These checks measure the endorsement that EndorseRank was designed to rank. High concordance between EndorseRank and this family is expected, and we interpret it as within-construct validity. As with the transfer baselines, the allowance checks are local; they count and sum incoming approvals without following multi-hop endorsement chains. Evidence from beyond the time window is reserved for the temporal holdout.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:proxy-sybil]{Sybil-adjusted stability family}

The inbound counters leave out self-transfers ($u=w$). Let $N_{\mathrm{tx}}(w)$ be the number of transfers that $w$ receives from other addresses and $N_{\mathrm{uniq}}(w)$ the number of distinct inbound counterparties. We take $t_{\min}(w)$ and $t_{\max}(w)$ to be the earliest and latest timestamps at which $w$ appears in the transfers table, as sender or as recipient. $M(w)$ is the number of distinct calendar months with activity; it is counted over inbound months and over full-span months, and the maximum of the two is used. Define
\begin{align}
\mathrm{inbound\_counterparty\_ratio}(w)
&=
\frac{N_{\mathrm{uniq}}(w)}{\max(N_{\mathrm{tx}}(w),1)},
\label{eq:counterparty-ratio}\\
\mathrm{transfer\_tenure\_days}(w)
&=
\frac{t_{\max}(w)-t_{\min}(w)}{86400}\quad\text{(seconds to days)},
\label{eq:tenure}\\
\mathrm{active\_months}(w)
&=
M(w).
\label{eq:active-months}
\end{align}
The counterparty ratio penalizes wallets that receive many transfers from few addresses, a simple Sybil/wash pattern. Tenure and active months track how persistent on-chain activity is over the observation window. These proxies are imperfect Sybil mitigations, because colluding adversaries can still maintain diverse counterparties. They do, however, operationalize the intuition that sustained, multi-counterparty activity says more about reputation than bursty self-dealing \cend{2}.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:statistical-tests]{Statistical tests}

Neither the reputation scores nor the validation proxies are binary default indicators; both are continuous and are handled as ranks. Following the AWP validation protocol\cend{3} and Cronbach and Meehl's approach to construct validity\cend{22}, concordance is measured with rank correlations, which are invariant under any monotonic re-scaling of either variable. Each coefficient is reported with a bootstrap confidence interval.

\begin{itemize}
\item Spearman's $\rho$: With $R_i$ and $S_i$ the dense ranks of score and proxy for wallet $i=1,\ldots,n$, Spearman's coefficient is the Pearson correlation computed on those ranks:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spearman-ops}
\rho
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_i-\bar R)(S_i-\bar S)}
{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_i-\bar R)^2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(S_i-\bar S)^2}}.
\end{equation}
Spearman's $\rho$ measures monotonic agreement. If higher reputation consistently goes with higher proxy values, $\rho$ is positive regardless of any nonlinearity in the raw scores.

\item Kendall's $\tau$: This coefficient is obtained by counting concordant and discordant pairs. Over all pairs $i<j$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kendall-ops}
\tau
=
\frac{2(C-D)}{n(n-1)},
\end{equation}
where $C$ counts the pairs that both rankings put in the same order and $D$ counts those they put in opposite order; ties are handled by the implementation's standard tie adjustment. Kendall's $\tau$ is the easier of the two to interpret, as a probability that two randomly chosen items are in the correct order. Under certain heavy-tailed distributions it is also less affected than Spearman by large rank shifts.

\item Why both: Spearman describes a global monotonic association, while Kendall puts more weight on the correct ordering of pairs. The AWP protocol \cend{3} requires both, and we report both so that our conclusions do not hinge on a single family of rank correlation. The score distributions for these wallets are heavy-tailed, with most wallets near zero and a handful of hubs accounting for a large share of the mass. Under such distributions Kendall remains well-defined, whereas a Pearson correlation on raw scores is dominated by the hubs.

\item Family aggregation: A family is summarized by the plain average over its proxies. The transfer-family mean $\tau$, for example, is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\tau_{\mathrm{in\_degree}}+\tau_{\mathrm{in\_value}})$. On a fixed cohort and fixed proxies, a comparison between methods is only a relative statement about those two methods and implies no universal property of reputation. Our conclusions are drawn from the confidence intervals, and no absolute $\tau$ cutoff is used as a decision criterion.

\item Uncertainty: Sampling uncertainty is estimated with a paired wallet bootstrap \cend{34}. We resample the matched cohort with replacement $B=400$ times (seed 42, hard-coded in the configuration) and reuse one index matrix for every method and proxy, so that the resampled statistics are paired across methods. For each proxy $\tau$, each family mean and each inter-method difference, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution form a 95\% percentile interval \cend{35}. Because the population Kendall $\tau$ is a U-statistic, at $n=5{,}521$ the percentile interval is first-order accurate. The number of resamples, however, was deliberately kept small. That choice makes the $O(B\,n\log n)$ cost of computing the tie-corrected $\tau$ across all methods and proxies tractable, but it limits the precision of the interval endpoints to about $\pm 0.005$.

\item Difference tests: Every comparison our argument depends on was fixed in advance as a paired contrast and is tested on the same resamples. Resample by resample, we compute $\Delta\tau=\tau_A-\tau_B$ and then report the 95\% percentile interval of that bootstrap distribution. A contrast counts as a difference when its interval excludes zero. Five of the pre-specified contrasts compare the two single-layer methods: (i) EndorseRank on its allowance family minus EndorseRank on the transfer family; (ii) AWP minus EndorseRank, both on the transfer family; (iii) EndorseRank minus AWP, both on the allowance family; (iv) AWP minus EndorseRank on the Sybil-stability family; and (v) EndorseRank on $\mathrm{in\_approve\_degree}$ minus EndorseRank on $\mathrm{in\_degree}$. The remaining eight set the hybrid operators against whichever single-layer method leads each family. Specifically, they are C-PR and S-PR each minus AWP on the transfer family, minus EndorseRank on the allowance family, and minus both on the Sybil-stability family; they include the secondary rule in Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol}. Because resampling is paired, the between-resample covariance in the two coefficient estimates cancels out, so the contrast interval will be narrower than the difference between the two separate confidence intervals.

\item Directionality and imputation: Each proxy is oriented so that a higher value means a better reputation. After re-alignment to $\mathcal{W}$, a wallet absent from a given proxy source is imputed as zero. With sparse transactions this is a conservative choice, and both scoring approaches apply it in the same way. No wallet appears in one dataset without also appearing in the other, and the wallets that enter alignment are always the full intersection of wallets in the relevant primary table.

\item What is excluded: The study attempts no causal inference (reputation does not cause future approvals in the experimental sense). It trains and evaluates no supervised learning models on separate training and testing sets, and it runs no hypothesis tests with accompanying $p$-values. We look instead at whether the confidence interval for the pre-specified contrast excludes the null value. The aim is to measure how far our proposed reputation metric aligns with the metrics established in prior work, and to compare how much computation each scoring procedure requires.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:computational-benchmark]{Computational benchmark (Claim~C1)}

Claim~C1 compares the computational performance of EndorseRank with that of AWP. The benchmark times the eigenvector computation separately from the one-time cost of building the underlying graph representation, because that cost is largely data loading and pre-processing overhead, which a production scoring service that recomputes reputation scores only periodically would likely amortize. The eigenvector computation, by contrast, is the main cost of running either algorithm.

\begin{itemize}
\item Timed component: The weighted adjacency matrix for each scoring procedure is built once, before the timing loop starts; extraction, decoding, allowance aggregation and the exponential decay of older transactions are all left out of the measurement. The timer covers the solver, which forms the sparse matrix from the edge list and then runs power iteration with damping factor $d=0.85$, convergence threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{tol}}=10^{-8}$ and an iteration cap of $I_{\max}=300$. Both of these steps are $O(|E|)$, and a production deployment would have to repeat both at every update of the scoring function. These parameters are the ones used to compute the alignment scores, which implies that the alignment scores and the execution times are based on the same underlying graph.

\item Measurement protocol: Every configuration is measured in a single session under one fixed protocol. A first, untimed run of the solver warms the internal caches and triggers any lazy initialization routines. Peak memory is tracked on this run only, because tracking it during the timed runs would add substantially to the elapsed time. Five timed runs follow. From them we report the mean and standard deviation of the run times and the average number of iterations to convergence, together with the peak memory seen in the warm-up run and the size of the input graph in nodes and edges. Within the session, results are memoized under a key formed from the edge multiset signature, damping, tolerance, iteration limit and repeat number. The same graph under the same settings occurs in the main benchmark, in the $d=0.85$ row of the damping sweep, in the in-cohort scaling stage with $n=5{,}521$, and in the expanded pool stage, whose induced subgraph is the cohort graph (Section~\ref{sub:expanded-pool}); in those cases the tables report the same values by design, and they are not independent measurements of the same quantity. I list edge counts in every runtime table, since edges are the main structural source of runtime variation in algorithms with $O(|E|)$ per-iteration cost \cend{15}. I also write the runtimes themselves to the machine-readable output (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}).

\item Cohorts: The main benchmark runs on the matched wallet cohort ($n=5{,}521$). For node-count sensitivity I draw deterministic subsamples from the expanded wallet pool (Section~\ref{sec:scaling-robustness}).

\item Fairness: To keep the comparison of AWP with EndorseRank fair, I run both with the same solver, tolerance, iteration limit, damping (apart from the damping sensitivity sweep) and seed wallet set. Differences can therefore be attributed to the density and weight structure of the two graphs (a snapshot of allowances vs.\ a time-decayed transfer history) and not to the implementation. My evaluation manifest records hardware and software details, so absolute runtimes can be read against the experimental machine. For comparing methods I prefer speedup ratios (AWP time divided by EndorseRank time), and each speedup is always reported alongside the corresponding edge ratio.

\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:scaling-robustness]{Scaling and robustness extensions}

I also examine how runtime responds to node count on a larger address set, and whether EndorseRank remains aligned with AWP when parameters and samples are perturbed. These analyses support H3 (scalability to larger $n$) and let me see whether my family-level conclusions extend beyond the sample I chose. Neither supplies an alternative primary sample. Unless stated otherwise, alignment claims in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} refer to the matched cohort, and node-count sensitivity refers to the expanded pool.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:expanded-pool]{Expanded wallet pool and node-count sensitivity of runtime}

I define the expanded wallet pool $\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{pool}}$ as the union of the matched cohort ($5{,}521$ addresses) and $93{,}559$ additional addresses sampled from monthly Arbitrum One ERC-20 activity in my observation window, taken from the supplemental active-wallet parquet that my sampling script produces. This gives $N=|\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{pool}}|=99{,}080$ addresses for the study. Intermediate-sized benchmarks are drawn from the pool by deterministic SHA256 subsampling with seed string \texttt{benchmark-tier2-v1}: wallets are sorted by
\begin{center}
\texttt{SHA256(seed || address)} with seed \texttt{benchmark-tier2-v1}
\end{center}
and the subsample is the first $n$ wallets in that order. The seed string is a constant fixed in my configuration. I report three stages, $n=10{,}000$, $n=50{,}000$ and the full pool $n=N=99{,}080$, all on the same approval and transfer parquet; in-cohort runs with $n\le 5{,}521$ are in Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}. Since the hashing is deterministic, the subsamples cannot drift between library versions the way pseudorandom draws do.

Two features of this design limit what the experiment can show. At every stage the graph is the subgraph of the cohort-window approval and transfer parquet induced by the sampled wallets. The edge count $|E|$ at a given stage thus depends on which wallets the hash order selects, and by construction it is not monotone in $n$. The other feature concerns the supplemental pool addresses, whose own approval and transfer logs were meant to be extracted in a secondary step. That extraction did not finish (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). As a result, the full-pool stage adds nodes but no edges beyond the cohort parquet, and it reproduces the cohort edge counts exactly. The pool stages therefore capture how runtime reacts when isolated or weakly connected nodes are added at fixed edge counts; they do not scale the edge count that drives per-iteration cost. Every scaling table reports per-stage $|V|$ and $|E|$ for both methods, and the scaling statement in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} goes no further than those columns allow.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:damping-protocol]{Damping sensitivity}

On the matched cohort we vary the PageRank damping over $d\in\{0.75,0.85,0.95\}$, with the graphs and proxies held fixed. The default, $d=0.85$, is the value used in classical web PageRank \cend{15} and in the AWP baseline \cend{3}. A smaller $d$ raises teleportation and shrinks scores. With a larger $d$, multi-hop paths receive more weight and convergence may slow down. The sweep asks whether alignment on the allowance and transfer families is sensitive to the teleportation probability.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:top-tokens]{Top-token subgraph}

For this check we extract a subgraph restricted to the top-$20$ ERC-20 tokens by combined allowance and transfer volume over the window. EndorseRank and AWP are recomputed on it to gauge their sensitivity to long-tail tokens and possible spam. Should the family-level results flip once low-volume tokens are dropped, the main finding would be fragile to the choice of tokens. Stable results, on the other hand, support reading the full-token graphs as a proxy for major economic transactions.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:sample-size-protocol]{Sample-definition sensitivity}

At each stage, alignment statistics are recomputed on subsamples of the expanded wallet pool drawn with the same deterministic seed. The main sample for every alignment claim remains the matched wallet cohort ($n=5{,}521$), on which its coefficients and confidence intervals are estimated. The pool subsamples differ from the cohort in size and also in composition. Some of their wallets appear in only one of the two graphs, so their score and proxy in the other graph are zero even after alignment, and such ties at zero inflate rank agreement mechanically. We report the per-stage coefficients as a sensitivity check on the sample, not as evidence of stability. The question is whether EndorseRank and AWP keep their relative ordering on the transfer and allowance families when the sample definition changes.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:reproducibility]{Reproducibility}

No empirical table in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} was transcribed by hand; an open, versioned evaluation pipeline generated every one of them. After the Phase~1 extracts are on disk, one evaluation driver recomputes all construct checks, all alignment statistics with their bootstrap confidence intervals and all runtime benchmarks, and writes the results to a single machine-readable summary. A separate stage then exports the tables for Chapter~\ref{ch:results} and Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval} from that file. The driver takes either cached ledger extracts or synthetic fixtures as input, and it has switches for the damping, top-token and sample-definition sweeps. One versioned configuration file stores the observation window, the damping value, the decay parameters, the event-topic IDs, the bootstrap resample count and seed, and the benchmark seeds. In fixture mode the pipeline needs no cloud credentials and runs entirely offline, so the implementation can be checked on a local machine.

The reproducible elements are these: deterministic wallet subsampling by SHA256 hashes; a fixed PageRank tolerance and iteration cap; one bootstrap seed and one resample-index matrix; the proxy formulas of Section~\ref{sec:proxy-formulas}; the benchmarking procedure of Section~\ref{sec:computational-benchmark}, with per-run timings kept in the logs; the coupling weight $\lambda$ and the holdout logic of Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol}, both fixed in the configuration file before the run began; and the extracted manifest files. From those same extracts the alignment and benchmark tables can be rebuilt without any new BigQuery queries. The machine-readable summaries behind all the tables, together with the active configuration, are committed to the Git repository under \texttt{results/}, directly beside the source code. The numbers in this paper can thus be checked against those summaries without rerunning the pipeline. Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval} lists the GitHub repository URL, the exact commit hash that generated the tables in this paper, the configuration file and the summary files.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:sampling-bias]{Sampling considerations and missingness}

The matched cohort is a deliberately constructed sample, not a simple random draw from all Arbitrum addresses. To be eligible, an address needs nonzero degree in the approval or transfer reputation subgraphs. The findings therefore apply only to wallets that leave some footprint in the endorsement graph or the transfer graph. By design, the cohort excludes dormant wallets, wallets that act only as long-term holders, and wallets that transact solely through non-ERC-20 channels.

Data can be missing in a few ways. A token with unknown decimal precision may be pruned or set to zero during preprocessing, and the pipeline logs each such choice. EndorseRank is completely blind to approvals that emit no standard ERC-20 log event (for example, approvals on non-standard tokens). AWP, for its part, never ingests transfers made off-chain or inside custodial accounts. Both methods are thus biased toward on-chain, standard-token behavior. For an on-chain reputation study that is acceptable, though it matters once results are extrapolated to all the users of a particular protocol.

Adding addresses from the broader wallet universe ($N=99{,}080$) widens node coverage for the runtime experiments. The alignment claim is accordingly always computed on the matched cohort ($n=5{,}521$), while the node-count sensitivity analysis uses the pool.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:implementation-notes]{Implementation notes}

The PageRank code is written in Python, with a SciPy sparse transition operator and NumPy power iteration. Each configuration builds its edge lists once. The timing benchmarks leave out I/O and preprocessing and cover operator assembly and iteration only (Section~\ref{sec:computational-benchmark}). Optional fixture generation is the one place where randomness enters; given the parquet inputs and configuration seeds, evaluation on real data is deterministic. Small floating-point differences between platforms are to be expected, but they would be negligible against the three-decimal-place precision of the $\tau$ metric.

Figures are generated from the same evaluation artifacts as the tables, so the plotted values agree with the tabulated numbers.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:ethics]{Ethics}

Only publicly available blockchain data are used in this research, and it involves no human subjects. The institutional ethics review (Unisa SoC/CAES) was completed before any empirical data collection began. Appendix~\ref{sec:ethics-certificate} contains the ethics clearance certificate (reference [Unisa SoC/CAES ethics clearance ref: TBD], dated [TBD]).

\begin{itemize}
\item\phantomsection\label{sub:privacy-anonymity}
  Privacy and anonymity: An address on Arbitrum One is a pseudonym and does not by itself reveal the real-world identity behind it \cend{2}. Pseudonymity is not the same as anonymity, however, and in theory a determined adversary could tie an address to a person through exchange deposits, online profiles or heuristics. In this work we deliberately make no such links. Our data collection is limited to on-chain \texttt{Approval} and \texttt{Transfer} events, and we scraped no off-chain personally identifiable information (such as IP addresses, exchange KYC records, social media handles or deanonymization graphs). We did not cluster addresses into real-world entities, publish leaderboards of addresses likely to be personally identifiable, or try to join a wallet's transaction history with other databases to infer identity. The published results are aggregate measures only (correlation coefficients, runtimes, family means) and contain no individualized rankings of named participants. Where the main text quotes examples, they name broad categories of user (for example, `spenders with high allowances') and never specific addresses.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:data-minimization}
  Data minimization: The processed tables contain only the fields that graph construction and the checks require, namely the relevant addresses, token amounts and timestamps. Raw logs are kept so that the research pipeline can be audited. They will not be released for the purpose of deanonymizing users. Wherever possible, query filters were scoped to wallets, so as to avoid indiscriminate full-chain dumps outside the scope of the study design.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:data-usage-compliance}
  Data usage and compliance: Every query ran against public data hosted in the \texttt{bigquery-public-data.goog\_blockchain\_arbitrum\_one\_us} dataset and complied with the Google Cloud terms of service and rate limits. Dry-run byte estimates and per-query maximums prevented unnecessary scans. We sent no transactions and accessed no privileged or non-public indexing endpoints, and no smart contract was invoked in any way that might interfere with the normal operation of the network.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:fairness-transparency}
  Fairness and transparency: New participants, low-frequency actors and wallets that grant no public approvals may all be undervalued by graph-based reputation systems \cend{2}. For that reason we examined both EndorseRank and AWP for structural biases of this kind. In the allowance graph, for instance, an address that has received no incoming spend authority is at a disadvantage relative to addresses that have; transfer graphs, in turn, favor addresses embedded in value-flow networks. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses the limitations, among them cold-start effects and the Sybil susceptibility of approval graphs. Every code path, parameter choice and evaluation metric is documented in the open pipeline (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}), so that others can review the work and replicate it independently.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:responsible-disclosure}
  Responsible disclosure and impact: EndorseRank may inform risk assessment in DeFi, but it is not financial advice, credit advice or a consumer reporting product. Lending, trading and compliance decisions still need human oversight. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} acknowledges and discusses the potential for adversarial manipulation, for example Sybil attacks on $G_{\text{approve}}$ or wash transfers on $G_{\text{transfer}}$. The study reports aggregated, cohort-level findings and no wallet-level rankings intended for targeting individual users. Any future operational deployment would require additional governance, appeal mechanisms and fairness monitoring, all of which lie beyond the scope of this research.


\item\phantomsection\label{sub:methodological-commitments}
  Summary of methodological commitments: Before results are interpreted, the design has already fixed the chain, window, cohort rules, graph formulas, proxy definitions, bootstrap and timing protocols, and statistical tests. Primary claims compare EndorseRank and AWP alone, on the matched wallet cohort and the spender holdout. The expanded pool and the sensitivity sweeps test efficiency and how far results depend on parameters and sample definition.
\end{itemize}

Next come the runtime benchmark with its node-count sensitivity, the sensitivity sweeps, the alignment tables with intervals and contrasts, the rank-divergence test, the coupled operator's position in the same window, and the two-label temporal holdout; the chapter closes by stating Claims~C1--C5.
