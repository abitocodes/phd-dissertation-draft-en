\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reported the measurements taken on Arbitrum One. Here each research question and each objective of Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} is held to the criterion stated there, and every interpretation points to the table of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} it rests on. Unless noted otherwise, same-window statements concern the matched cohort of contracts and holdout statements the spender cohorts.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:findings-rq]{Findings by research question}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq1]{RQ1: The two single-layer walks}

RQ1 asks whether EndorseRank and AWP, the walks over allowance edges alone and over transfer edges alone, order the same contracts in different ways, and how closely each score agrees with contemporaneous allowance and transfer checks. They order the contracts differently: the interval of their agreement excludes both zero and one (Table~\ref{tab:tie-sensitivity}). The divergence does not come from ties or from the rule for missing contracts: EndorseRank leaves few contract pairs tied, and keeping the contracts missing from a graph as isolated nodes leaves the agreement unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:rank-divergence}).

The divergence follows from how the two graphs are built. The endorsement graph records the latest approvals and has fewer than half of the edges of the transfer graph, which records every transfer \parencite{do2023awp}, and the two methods restart differently. PageRank places stationary mass according to the edges it is given \parencite{page1999pagerank,langville2006pagerank}, so a contract that receives many transfers from active senders but few approvals ranks high under AWP and modestly under EndorseRank, and the reverse holds for a contract that many owners approve without being a transfer hub. H1 is supported, largely by construction, since two walks over different graphs with different restarts were not expected to agree. In practice one score cannot stand in for the other: an operator who adopts EndorseRank for its speed gets a different ranking and not a faster approximation of AWP. The divergence is also the premise of C-PR: if the two walks ordered the contracts alike, one walk over both could add little to either.

In the same window each score follows the edges it walks on: the walks over transfers agree most with the transfer family and much less with the allowance family, the walks over allowances agree most with the allowance family, and every interval on a score's own family excludes zero (Table~\ref{tab:alignment-hybrid}).

Circularity bounds H2. Every proxy in a family is computed from the same events as the matching graph, so the agreement is an intended-construct check in the sense of \textcite{cronbach1955construct} and not an external test. Part of it is built in: AWP, whose edges weigh each transfer by its dollar value, follows the summed value of inbound transfers almost as closely as their number (Table~\ref{tab:alignment-transfer}). Ties do not bound H2 either: the isolated-node rule changes no coefficient (Table~\ref{tab:tie-sensitivity}). In the narrow sense of agreement with the checks of a score's own edges, H2 is supported, largely by construction. None of these comparisons was fixed in advance, and the paired differences between EndorseRank and AWP, which carry no claim, are in Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-er-awp}.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq2]{RQ2: Computational cost}

RQ2 asks what each score costs to compute on the same sample, and how much walking both edge sets adds. EndorseRank is the cheapest score in time, memory and iterations, and the two hybrids, which walk the transfer layer, take longer than AWP (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}), so walking both edge sets costs more than walking either. Each iteration costs time in $|E|$ and in $|V|$ \parencite{langville2006pagerank}, and AWP needs more iterations than EndorseRank; its time ratio lies well above the edge ratio at $d=0.85$ and moves with $d$ (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-results}), so the cost grows with the size of each graph and with the iterations its walk needs, which the benchmark does not separate.

The scaling tables qualify this result more than they extend it (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-scaling}). Each stage is a subsample of the cohort that keeps the edges incident to its contracts, so the stages never test a graph larger than the cohort graph (Section~\ref{sub:runtime-scaling}). The damping and value-slope checks leave the alignment pattern as it was (Tables~\ref{tab:robustness-damping} and~\ref{tab:robustness-slope}). H3 is supported on the matched cohort, again largely by construction, since both scores run the same solver; on the subsamples EndorseRank's lead in speed holds at every stage, but the time ratio follows the edge ratio at none, because the iterations each walk needs differ from stage to stage. H1--H3 are thus descriptive checks of the instruments; the tests whose outcome was open are RQ3--RQ5 and Section~\ref{sub:finding-neutral}.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq3]{RQ3: Temporal holdout and the raw degrees}

RQ3 has two clauses. The first asks whether scores frozen at the end of March 2026 predict new owner--spender approvals and new transfer senders on the spender cohort over the following three months. Every score predicts both labels, and the edge type decides how well (Table~\ref{tab:holdout-spenders}). On new approval pairs the in-approve degree leads, followed by the interior mixtures, EndorseRank and the allowance layer, while the scores built on transfers alone stay lower, partly because they score zero the one spender in six without a transfer edge, and these gained new approval pairs more often. On the other spenders AWP ranks this label above EndorseRank and below the in-approve degree in W0, without a paired contrast, level with C-PR in W0 and below it in W1 (Tables~\ref{tab:holdout-receiving} and~\ref{tab:cpr-contrasts}; restrictions chosen after the labels were known). On new senders the transfer in-degree leads, ahead of AWP, and the scores built on allowances alone rank lowest. W1 repeats this pattern among the same scores (Tables~\ref{tab:fresh-holdout} and~\ref{tab:fresh-posthoc}). The evidence concerns spenders; Section~\ref{sub:finding-neutral} turns to the traders.

The second clause asks whether any PageRank beats the raw degree it smooths, and none does (Tables~\ref{tab:holdout-diff} and~\ref{tab:fresh-posthoc}). AWP ranks later new senders below the transfer in-degree in both windows, with intervals below zero, and in W0 also on the spenders that had received a transfer before the freeze, so its zeros do not explain the gap (Table~\ref{tab:holdout-receiving}). EndorseRank, with the uniform restarts of the analysis plan, ranks new approval pairs below the in-approve degree in both windows, and so does its variant with AWP's activity restarts, by less (Section~\ref{sub:endorserank-restarts}); the transfer layer of C-PR, walked with uniform restarts, stays further below the transfer in-degree, without a paired contrast (Section~\ref{sec:holdout-results}, Table~\ref{tab:fresh-holdout}). On these labels the restart rule changes how far a walk falls short of its count, not whether it does. Known results lead one to expect the count to be hard to beat: in growing networks new links attach preferentially to nodes that already have many \parencite{barabasi1999scaling,newman2001clustering}, which makes degree a standard link-prediction baseline \parencite{libennowell2007link}, also on Ethereum transaction networks \parencite{lin2022tracking}, and where degree correlations are weak, as on the Web, PageRank is on average proportional to in-degree \parencite{fortunato2008indegree}. For most spenders EndorseRank is a reweighted approval count (Section~\ref{sub:theory-closed-form}). This does not undo the first clause, since the counts are statistics of the same edges. The coupled walk answers the second clause no better: C-PR ranks neither label above the count of the same edge type, lying below the in-approve degree on new approval pairs and below the transfer in-degree on new senders in both windows (Table~\ref{tab:cpr-contrasts}). All of these contrasts were computed after the labels were known.

The labels lie outside the window but belong to the same constructs as the scores, and neither contains any element of profit or repayment. RQ3 therefore shows the temporal predictive validity of each construct, with the raw degree as the baseline that a PageRank must beat, and leaves credit risk aside; the liquidation label of W1 (Section~\ref{sub:fresh-holdout}), on which Section~\ref{sub:finding-neutral} compares the edge types, comes closest to it.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq4]{RQ4: The coupled walk against its layers}

RQ4 and RQ5 concern the method of the thesis, and for C-PR, the coupling the plan fixed, both get a negative answer, each under its own rule, applied here, like R1--R3 in Section~\ref{sub:finding-neutral}, to the revised scores as a re-analysis; the registered analysis reached the same verdicts on both rules (Appendix~\ref{sec:map-registered}). RQ4 asks whether one walk over both layers ranks contracts better than either layer walked alone. Under rule A, fixed in the analysis plan, it does not. On W0 C-PR is no worse than the allowance layer on new approval pairs but falls behind the transfer layer on new senders (Table~\ref{tab:holdout-diff}), and in the same window the secondary rule fails on all three families (Section~\ref{sec:coupled-results}). The rule was fixed before any coupled score existed, so $\lambda$ could not have been tuned to it, although the revised C-PR's dollar weights, allowance cap and score windows were set after the registered results were known. It limits an obvious next step: the plan's convex mixture of the two edge types at the transition level gained on new approval pairs over the allowance layer and lost on new senders against the transfer layer.

The ablation and the layer weights explain this answer. S-PR keeps the transfer walk but restarts it from the allowance walk; it ranks the flow label as well as the transfer walk or slightly better and loses most of the endorsement signal, since a teleportation vector derived from endorsements changes little when transfer edges decide where the walk goes, and since S-PR scores only the transfer-layer nodes, part of that loss is coverage. In C-PR the allowance edges carry part of the walk, so the allowance signal survives on the spender holdout: every interior mixture is above the allowance layer on new approval pairs and below the transfer layer on new senders (Tables~\ref{tab:holdout-spenders} and~\ref{tab:holdout-diff}). Its layers carry dollar amounts without a bound, apart from the cap on allowances, and restart uniformly, whereas AWP bounds the weight of each transfer and restarts at active senders; AWP ranks new senders better than C-PR in both windows, and walked alone C-PR's transfer layer already ranks them below AWP (Tables~\ref{tab:holdout-spenders} and~\ref{tab:fresh-holdout}), so part of that gap comes from the weights and restarts, not from the coupling. Other couplings are not evaluated in this thesis (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq5]{RQ5: Allowance edges added to a transfer walk}

RQ5 asks whether the walk improves on a walk over the transfers alone. An exploratory comparison on W0 said that it did on new approval pairs, but it was made after the labels were known. Rule B, registered before the study extracted the July--September logs, decides the question, and its answer is no: C-PR again beat the transfer layer on new approval pairs, but non-inferiority was shown neither on new senders nor on the traders' liquidation-free close rate, and the rule needed all three conditions (Section~\ref{sub:fresh-results}). In the first sensitivity analysis, with AWP as the comparator, F1 holds and F2 and F3 fail: C-PR ranks new approval pairs higher and new senders lower, and the close rate does not separate the two (Table~\ref{tab:fresh-contrasts}). The rule was registered before the study extracted its labels and fails on two of its three conditions. W1 establishes something narrower: adding allowance edges to C-PR's transfer walk ranks new approval pairs better on new data, partly through the spenders the walk scores zero, at a loss on new senders larger than the margin of the registration; whether C-PR beats its transfer layer on the other spenders was not computed.

Beside the rules, the contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:cpr-contrasts} show where that information comes from and what it costs. C-PR ranks both spender labels above EndorseRank in both windows, as it does above its own allowance layer, so walking the transfers as well improves on a walk over allowances alone on every spender label, on later inflow most. Against AWP, the gain on later approvals comes largely from the spenders that the transfer walk scores zero because they have no transfer edge; among the spenders it can rank, C-PR and AWP are level on later approvals in W0 and C-PR leads in W1, while AWP orders later senders better in both windows. What the allowance layer adds to a transfer walk out of window is therefore mainly coverage, an order among the contracts that the transfer walk leaves tied, with a small gain on later approvals among the others in W1. EndorseRank with activity restarts, an allowance walk that restarts at approving owners, is level with C-PR on later approvals in W0 and ranks them better in W1; C-PR leads it on later senders in both windows and within the window on the transfer and Sybil-stability families (Section~\ref{sub:cpr-contrasts}). No PageRank of the study, the coupled one included, ranks later arrivals above the raw count of the same edge type. These contrasts were computed after the labels were known and decide nothing.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-neutral]{Allowance against transfer on outcomes built from neither edge type}

Unlike the spender labels of RQ3--RQ5, which favor the scores built on their own edge type, the GMX trader outcomes are built from neither edge type and were measured after the scores were frozen. The comparison of the two edge types on them (Section~\ref{sub:neutral-label-protocol}) adds no research question and is reported as Claim~C6 (Section~\ref{sub:neutral-label-results}).

This comparison bears on counterparty risk, the use that motivated the thesis (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}). For a protocol that extends leverage or credit to a contract account, a counterparty that is often force-liquidated is a riskier one, and forced liquidation is the nearest outcome to default in these data. On later liquidation avoidance in W1, the primary window, the difference P between the approval count and the transfer count at the freeze has a Bonferroni 97.5\% interval above zero (Table~\ref{tab:neutral-label-contrasts}), as does the interval stratified by the number of closes, so R1 is met: in the registered wording, there is a count-level lead of allowance over transfer on later liquidation avoidance. In W0, the secondary window, P was positive but not resolved. The transfer count also ranks this outcome (Table~\ref{tab:fresh-holdout}), and the transfer scores rank as well the outcomes that grow with activity, the number of profitable closes and the realized gain (Table~\ref{tab:fresh-traders}): they record how much a contract trades.

Four limits bound this reading. First, R2 is not met: the like-for-like PageRank pair, EndorseRank with AWP's restarts against AWP, is not resolved in either window, and EndorseRank again falls below the approval count it smooths (Table~\ref{tab:neutral-label-contrasts}). Second, the comparison rests on a few dozen approval-receiving traders per window, all of whom avoided liquidation, against about three in four of the others (Table~\ref{tab:neutral-label-recipients}), and every interval is wide. Third, every trader is a contract, so account type cannot explain the pattern, but the role of a contract may: nearly all recipients were approved by one contract (Section~\ref{sub:neutral-label-protocol}), so they form one cluster, and they close more positions, which fits a family of managed or pooled trading contracts. Fourth, R3 applies in W1: the same signal is lower on the share of closes that were profitable, so the construct it ranks is forced-liquidation avoidance, not trading success; its lower non-loss share is partly built in, because the pipeline counts a liquidated close as not a loss.

Eight findings outside the objectives also shape the conclusions. Five limit the claims: the value slopes chosen in the revision were checked only in the same window (Section~\ref{sub:threat-construct}); the subsample stages never ran on a graph larger than the cohort graph (Section~\ref{sub:finding-rq2}); on the trader cohorts no score ranks the share of profitable closes with an interval above zero (Section~\ref{sub:trader-results}); EndorseRank's point estimates on both spender labels lie slightly above those of the allowance layer of C-PR, without a paired contrast, so how approvals are weighted may matter (Section~\ref{sub:threat-construct}); and C-PR's lead over AWP on new approval pairs comes largely from the spenders that AWP scores zero (Section~\ref{sub:cpr-contrasts}). \dissertationSubheading[sec:theoretical-implications]{Theoretical implications}

The results bear on three areas of theory: propagation semantics on financial graphs, the construct validity of on-chain scores, and the economics of trust under pseudonymity.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:theory-propagation]{Propagation semantics beyond transfers}

PageRank was first formulated for hyperlinks, read as a citation network \parencite{page1999pagerank}. \textcite{do2023hodgerank} carried this reading over to Ethereum transactions, and AWP \parencite{do2023awp} refined it with value and age weights and restarts at active senders. EndorseRank applies it to ERC-20 allowances, where an owner who lets a spender act for them issues a directed endorsement of spending authority \parencite{vogelsteller2015erc20}. The RQ1 divergence shows that changing what the edges mean, and where the walk restarts, changes the ranking even on the same nodes, so graph-based reputation is a family of methods, each valid relative to the relationships its edges encode. Peer-to-peer and web trust systems reached the same conclusion from the other side: in EigenTrust \parencite{kamvar2003eigentrust} and TrustRank \parencite{gyongyi2004trustrank} the seed set and the edge definitions largely decide the outcome. EndorseRank's edge is native to a token standard, where theirs belonged to a message-passing overlay or a hyperlink graph. C-PR takes the next step and lets one walk follow both relations of the same addresses, so that a contract's score can draw on the payments it receives and the spending rights it is granted at once.

The holdouts add a qualification that the propagation metaphor hides. Changing the edge type changed what the score could anticipate: on new approval pairs the scores built on allowance edges led, partly through the spenders that walks over transfers score zero, and on new senders the walks over transfers led, in both windows. No walk added to the count of its own edges out of window: AWP, EndorseRank with either restart rule and the layers of C-PR all ranked their labels below the raw degree (Section~\ref{sub:finding-rq3}). On these data the choice of edge decides which spenders a score can order, and the count of that edge orders them best. When the two layers share one walk, the allowance signal survives where allowance edges carry the walk and is mostly lost where they only seed its restarts (Section~\ref{sub:finding-rq4}), and the coupled walk ranks every spender label above the walk over allowances alone while staying below the walk over transfers on later senders (Section~\ref{sub:finding-rq5}).

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:theory-closed-form]{Depth-one spenders: what a walk adds to a count}

The allowance graphs keep only the approvals of the five tokens that a cohort contract grants or receives (Section~\ref{sec:graph-construction}), so most nodes are pure owners, with out-edges only, or pure spenders, with in-edges only. A cohort contract can be both, so the graph also has paths of more than one step. For a spender whose owners receive no approvals, a walk with uniform restarts has a closed form.

\emph{Proposition.} Let PageRank with transition matrix $P$ be computed by Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank} with damping $d$ and uniform restarts, so that dangling mass is also spread uniformly. Then every node without in-edges has the same score $c$, and every node $v$ whose in-neighbors all lack in-edges has
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:closed-form}
r_v = c\,\bigl(1 + d\,\delta(v)\bigr), \qquad \delta(v)=\sum_{u} P_{uv},
\end{equation}
the sum of the transition probabilities into $v$. In EndorseRank $P_{uv}=w(u,v)/o(u)$, so $\delta(v)$ is the owner-normalized in-strength of $v$: each owner's endorsement is divided over all the spenders it approves. In C-PR $P_{uv}$ is the coupled transition of Equation~\eqref{eq:coupled-transition}, and an in-neighbor without in-edges is one that has none in either layer.

\emph{Derivation.} At the fixed point, $r_v = (1-d+d\,r_D)/|V| + d\sum_u r_u\,P_{uv}$, where $r_D$ is the mass held by the dangling nodes. A node without in-edges receives only the first term, so every such node has the score $c=(1-d+d\,r_D)/|V|$, the constant $c$ of Section~\ref{sub:sybil-bound}. If every in-neighbor $u$ of $v$ is such a node, substituting $r_u=c$ gives Equation~\eqref{eq:closed-form}.

On EndorseRank's graph at $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$, 5{,}232 of the 6{,}224 cohort spenders have depth one, and EndorseRank as computed in this thesis lies within 1\% of Equation~\eqref{eq:closed-form} for every one of them; of the deeper spenders, which have an owner that itself receives approvals, 21.9\% do. Over all spenders the share is 87.5\%, and over the W0 spenders at $t_1$ it is 70.8\%. Over the cohort spenders EndorseRank's Kendall $\tau$ with $\delta$ is $0.908$, a point estimate (scripts \texttt{run\_same\_window.py} and \texttt{run\_holdout.py}). These shares were not computed for the union graph of C-PR, where an owner that also received a transfer has in-edges and its spenders are therefore not of depth one.

Two consequences follow. First, for a depth-one spender EndorseRank only reweights the approval count, counting an owner that approves many spenders for less at each; besides the time and value weights, that normalization is all that separates it from the count, which ranks new approval pairs better (Section~\ref{sub:finding-rq3}). For these spenders the negative answer to the second clause of RQ3 for EndorseRank thus has an analytic basis: there was no propagated information for the walk to add. With activity restarts, dangling mass follows the restarts, so for a depth-one spender the variant is a one-step sum over its owners weighted by their restart mass: it differs from the count only in how owners are weighted, not through longer paths, and it too ranks new approval pairs below the count. Second, the graph does have deeper paths, and for most deeper spenders the score departs from the closed form, so multi-hop propagation is present in this graph, but over all spenders EndorseRank did not beat the raw count out of window, and the deeper spenders were not tested apart. The ego network cuts longer paths (Section~\ref{sub:threat-external}), so testing propagation further would need a deeper sample, such as the two-hop neighborhood of the cohort or a chain-wide allowance graph, or restarts seeded at vetted contracts.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:theory-construct]{Multi-construct domain alignment}

The checks were designed to separate transfer centrality from allowance delegation, and each score aligns with the construct of the edges it walks on, the mixtures falling between their layers. Classical construct-validity theory would predict this pattern for instruments that measure different constructs \parencite{cronbach1955construct}, and the integrated view of \textcite{messick1995validity} goes further, counting the consequences of using a score as part of its validity. The same theory says what such a pattern cannot settle: when two instruments measure different constructs, the one that comes out ahead on a check is the one built for that check, so ordering the instruments by their coefficients does not show which is the better measure of reputation. Outcomes built from neither edge type are the exception, since neither instrument was built for them, which is why the thesis compares the edge types there (Section~\ref{sub:finding-neutral}); even there, which edge type leads depends on the outcome.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:theory-pseudonymity]{Reputation under cheap pseudonyms}

Identities are cheap to create on public ledgers \parencite{nakamoto2008bitcoin}, and under Ethereum's account model a new address needs no registration step at all \parencite{wood2025yellowpaper}. Any reputation system that rewards inbound transfers or allowances is therefore exposed to Sybil strategies, in which an attacker creates edges among addresses it controls \parencite{douceur2002sybil}. Section~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model} formalizes the cost of attacking an allowance graph, where a closed farm of addresses that approve one another needs, unlike transfer wash trading, no token balance and pays little beyond gas.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:practical-implications]{Practical implications and deployment scenarios}

Protocols and infrastructure providers have to choose between transfer-hub detection, authorization-aware reputation and a walk that combines the two. The measured results support choosing the score by scenario over recommending a single one.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-hybrid]{The coupled walk and combined use}

Whether to combine the two signals is a question about deployment and about method, and the evidence answers the two differently. Folding the two edge types into one walk did not pay against the published transfer walk on these data: C-PR costs more than AWP, more than the EndorseRank and AWP solves together (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}), trails it on new senders in both windows and, among the contracts AWP can rank, gains on new approval pairs only in W1 and only slightly (Sections~\ref{sub:finding-rq5} and~\ref{sub:cpr-contrasts}), so an operator free to choose should keep the two scores apart, running EndorseRank or the approval count for authorization-aware monitoring and AWP for transfer-hub audits, at the cost of both solves. For an operator tied to a single transfer walk weighted by dollar amounts, adding allowance edges bought authorization information that held on new data, chiefly by ranking contracts that the transfer walk leaves at zero, at a loss on inflow larger than $0.01$, so rule B is not met.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-transfer]{Transfer-hub and flow monitoring}

A protocol that describes current flow (transfer-hub search by market makers and liquidity providers, routing analysis, or transfer-based compliance heuristics) can read it from in-degree and in-value directly. AWP \parencite{do2023awp} agrees with present inflow (Table~\ref{tab:alignment-hybrid}) and out of window ranked later new senders above every other PageRank but below the raw transfer in-degree in both windows (Tables~\ref{tab:holdout-spenders} and~\ref{tab:fresh-posthoc}), so on these data a protocol that wants to anticipate flow has no reason to use it over the count.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-allowance]{Authorization-aware and low-latency refresh}

Where explicit spending authorization matters most (token vaults, allowance-gated integrations, or risk engines that treat approvals as acts of trust), protocols have reason to use allowance edges. The cheapest form is the spender's in-approve degree, which needs no solver and predicted new approvals better than EndorseRank, and than every other PageRank of the study, in both windows (Tables~\ref{tab:holdout-diff} and~\ref{tab:fresh-posthoc}). EndorseRank, its propagated version, agrees most with the allowance checks (Table~\ref{tab:alignment-hybrid}) and solves faster than AWP (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}), but for most spenders it reflects the endorsing owners only through how many spenders each approves (Section~\ref{sub:theory-closed-form}), a property not shown to improve any label, and under uniform teleportation it is open to cheap inflation by closed farms (Section~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model}). The variant with activity restarts came closer to the count after the labels were known but stayed below it in both windows, so on these data no propagated allowance score improves on the count.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-industry]{Industry adoption scenarios}

The scenarios below say for whom each score is useful and for whom it is not.

\begin{itemize}
\item DEX operators. A protocol that sets fee tiers or monitoring priority by user needs a score for its users, and its users are mostly externally owned accounts, which this thesis does not score (Section~\ref{sec:data-sources}). The scores can instead rank the routers, vaults and pools that the DEX integrates with.
\item Lending and margin protocols. Lending markets want to put more of their capital to use without cutting collateral \parencite{bartoletti2021lending}, and the stress of 2020 showed the cost of updating risk parameters late \parencite{gudgeon2020crisis}. When the counterparty is a contract, their screening question is whether it will be force-liquidated. On that outcome a lead of the approval count over the transfer count was resolved in the primary window but not in W0, the PageRank pair is unresolved, and the signal, lower on trading success, rests on a few dozen contract traders per window (Section~\ref{sub:finding-neutral}). A screen built on it would need a registered test on a larger sample. The scores are not tools for predicting default.
\item Wallet and security tooling. Users and integrators want to know whether a spender contract is widely authorized. A badge that shows how many owners have approved a spender, or its EndorseRank, makes that delegation explicit \parencite{vogelsteller2015erc20,lundfall2020permit}; it conveys the position of a graph node and is neither a security audit nor a guarantee against malicious contracts.
\item Liquidity providers and market makers. To control inventory and spreads, these participants need to find transfer hubs and see how flow is structured. Transfer counts and AWP are the suitable tools for that goal \parencite{do2023awp}, and their benefit is lower screening cost, not higher profit and loss.
\item Airdrop and governance programs. Sybil clusters routinely abuse token distributions and governance eligibility \parencite{douceur2002sybil,luo2025airdrop}. EndorseRank as defined here offers little protection below the top of its ranking: a closed farm collects about three times its population share and can concentrate it on one target, so about 25 addresses lift a contract into the cohort's top tenth, although reaching the top would take about 47{,}000 (Section~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model}); the bound behind these figures holds for C-PR as well. Programs can require diverse incoming endorsements from owners with independent histories, discount mass that circulates inside closed clusters, or send teleportation and dangling mass only to a vetted seed set; none of these stops an attacker who buys endorsements from honest owners.
\item Analytics and compliance providers. Exchanges, funds and compliance teams need counterparty rankings that outsiders can recompute. C-PR, EndorseRank and AWP can be published for defined windows and parameters and audited, unlike proprietary machine learning models \parencite{packin2024credit}, provided that their Sybil exposure is disclosed and they are not presented as profit predictions.
\end{itemize}

In none of these scenarios does reputation replace collateral.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-multiples]{Standardized scores as shared infrastructure}

A score shared by several parties is useful only if each of them can recompute it and see how it might be manipulated. C-PR, EndorseRank and AWP can be recomputed from public logs and a public daily price series with the open pipeline (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}), and Sections~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model} and~\ref{sec:regulatory-ethics} document how they can be manipulated.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:societal-impact]{Societal and practical impact}

In the DeFi market, vetting a counterparty is expensive, and the capacity to do it is unevenly spread. Wealthy actors may be able to pay for proprietary risk tools, while retail participants work from anecdotal evidence or skip vetting altogether. A score built from the permissions users have already granted, and cheap to recompute (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}), lowers the barrier to vetting the contracts that ask for those permissions. Such a score shows how many owners would be exposed if a contract misused its allowances, which transfer records do not show; an approval granted earlier is one route by which tokens are drained (Section~\ref{sec:approval-security}). It is not shown to anticipate drains: the drain heuristic rose with every score, the transfer-based ones included (Section~\ref{sec:holdout-results}).

The results support fitting a score to each decision over recommending a single one, which also lowers the risk of using a flow score to decide authorization. The degree contrasts cited above were computed after the labels were known.

Where the decentralized credit scoring systems discussed by \textcite{packin2024credit} are opaque, a score built from public events and public prices with versioned parameters supplies the explanatory information that regulators suggest should be required when DeFi protocols are analyzed \parencite{zetzsche2020defi}. It also brings a duty, described in Section~\ref{sec:regulatory-ethics}, to ensure that an ordinal, graph-based statistic is not mistaken for an identity, a credit history, or a security audit.

Any reputation metric that affects how value is allocated is open to exploitation; the next section models what it costs to manipulate a walk over allowance edges, EndorseRank and C-PR among them.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:sybil-cost-model]{Sybil attack cost model on allowance graphs}

A Sybil attack exploits cheap pseudonyms to create a false impression of trustworthiness \parencite{douceur2002sybil}. On a transfer graph, a malicious actor can inflate in-degree and in-flow artificially by moving assets among accounts it controls, paying for this in transaction fees and possible inventory loss \parencite{cong2023washtrading}. Allowance graphs expose a different attack surface, since each edge records an authorization, not an actual transfer. In this section we formulate a simple cost model for inflating a walk over allowance edges with fake allowances, computed on EndorseRank's graph; its bound holds for C-PR as well (Section~\ref{sub:sybil-bound}). It builds on the optimal link farms of \textcite{gyongyi2005alliances} and on the result of \textcite{cheng2005sybilproof} that symmetric functions such as PageRank are not Sybil-proof (Section~\ref{sec:reputation-systems}), and adds the cost of an allowance edge and the size of the gain on this study's graph.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:sybil-setup]{Attack setup}

Let the attacker control a set of addresses $\mathcal{S}$, and write $\mathcal{H}=V\setminus\mathcal{S}$ for the honest addresses in the endorsement graph $G_{\text{approve}}=(V,E,w)$. An edge $(u,v)$ of weight $w_{uv}>0$ records that $u$ has authorized $v$ to spend its tokens. The weight is that of Equation~\eqref{eq:approve-weight}. With damping parameter $d\in(0,1)$, EndorseRank finds the PageRank vector $\mathbf{r}$ on $G_{\text{approve}}$ as the solution of \parencite{page1999pagerank}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pagerank-sybil}
\mathbf{r} = d\, P^{\top} \mathbf{r} + \frac{1-d}{|V|}\mathbf{1},
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the transition matrix of Equation~\eqref{eq:transition}. A node with out-edges splits its row in proportion to its outgoing allowance weights. A dangling node, one without out-edges, has every entry of its row equal to $1/|V|$, so its mass is spread uniformly over all nodes \parencite{langville2006pagerank}. The fixed point is the same as that of the iteration in Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank}, which the implementation runs and which feeds dangling mass back through the uniform teleportation vector. The attacker's objective is to maximize the mean EndorseRank of a target set $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{S}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
J(\mathcal{G}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}} r_i,
\end{equation}
subject to a budget constraint on the cost of the attack, which is defined next.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:sybil-cost]{Cost components}

The attacker creates a set of edges $E_{\mathcal{S}}$, all with sources in $\mathcal{S}$, and may also buy a set $E_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{S}}$ of edges from honest owners into $\mathcal{S}$. Costs are counted over $q$ score windows, the number of score refreshes for which the targets are meant to hold their rank. Gas is the only cost that every edge incurs.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Gas cost. Each \texttt{approve} or \texttt{permit} call consumes gas; write $c_{\mathrm{gas}}$ for the expected fee of one allowance update. EndorseRank reads the \texttt{Approval} events of the twelve months before each anchor, so an approval enters only the graphs whose window holds it; inside a closed farm its time weight does not matter, since row normalization removes it (item 2). A closed farm therefore has to emit its approvals again at least once every twelve months, and over score refreshes that span $y$ such periods $C_{\mathrm{gas}} = y\,|E_{\mathcal{S}}|\,c_{\mathrm{gas}}$.

\item Exposure cost. An approval locks no tokens, and the owner need not hold any balance of the token when it approves. The amount costs nothing either: an unlimited allowance takes the full value weight $V$ without any balance behind it, and after row normalization a single out-edge gives $P_{uv}=1$ whatever its weight. The list of five tokens does not raise this cost, since approving a listed token needs no balance either. An edge inside $\mathcal{S}$ therefore exposes nothing and costs nothing beyond gas. Exposure arises only when an attacker address that holds tokens approves a spender $v\notin\mathcal{S}$, for instance to look like an ordinary user of known contracts, since $v$ can then move those tokens. Such an edge also lets score mass leave $\mathcal{S}$. With $\ell_{uv}$ the expected cost to the attacker of one such exposure over one window, $C_{\mathrm{exp}} = q\sum_{(u,v)\in E_{\mathcal{S}},\,v\notin\mathcal{S}} \ell_{uv}$, which is zero for a closed farm.

\item Revocation and detection cost. Legitimate users revoke old allowances from time to time, and a farm of fresh addresses that approve only one another might be identifiable. Let $c_{\mathrm{rev}}$ be the expected cost per window of detection and of the revocations the attacker issues to avoid it; over $q$ windows, $C_{\mathrm{rev}} = q\,c_{\mathrm{rev}}$.

\item Bought honest edges. The attacker can also pay honest owners to approve its addresses, for instance through an app that asks users for permission. A bought edge $(h,a)$, with $h\in\mathcal{H}$ and $a\in\mathcal{S}$, stays in the graph for twelve months after $h$ last approves it, and while it is in force, and it loses share of $h$'s row as it ages, so keeping it effective costs, per window,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal}
c_{\mathrm{in}}(h,a) = c_{\mathrm{gas}} + p_h + \rho_h,
\end{equation}
where $p_h$ is the payment to the honest owner and $\rho_h$ is a risk premium the owner asks against the allowance being spent by the attacker.
\end{enumerate}

Over the $q$ windows, the total cost of the attack is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total-cost}
C = C_{\mathrm{gas}} + C_{\mathrm{exp}} + C_{\mathrm{rev}} + q\sum_{(h,a)\in E_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{S}}} c_{\mathrm{in}}(h,a).
\end{equation}
For a closed farm, $C_{\mathrm{exp}}=0$ and $E_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{S}}$ is empty, so $C=y\,|E_{\mathcal{S}}|\,c_{\mathrm{gas}}+q\,c_{\mathrm{rev}}$.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:sybil-bound]{Influence and a cost-effectiveness bound}

By the usual sensitivity results for PageRank, an edge moves $\mathbf{r}$ in proportion to the stationary mass at its source node and to its normalized outgoing weight \parencite{langville2006pagerank}. Consider first a closed farm: every node of $\mathcal{S}$ has at least one out-edge, all of these edges end in $\mathcal{S}$, and no honest owner approves an address in $\mathcal{S}$. Let $D$ be the set of dangling nodes, all of which then lie in $\mathcal{H}$, and let $r_D=\sum_{j\in D} r_j$ be their stationary mass. By Equation~\eqref{eq:pagerank-sybil}, a node $i\in\mathcal{S}$ receives $d\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}} r_j P_{ji}$ from the farm, $(1-d)/|V|$ from teleportation and $d\,r_D/|V|$ from the dangling rows. The rows of the farm's nodes sum to one inside $\mathcal{S}$, so summing over $\mathcal{S}$ gives $\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}} r_i = d\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}} r_i + |\mathcal{S}|\,(1-d+d\,r_D)/|V|$, that is,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:closed-cluster}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}} r_i = \frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{|V|}\cdot\frac{1-d+d\,r_D}{1-d}.
\end{equation}
The first factor is the farm's population share, and the second says how many times that share the farm collects. The second factor equals one only when $r_D=0$, that is, on a graph without dangling nodes; otherwise the dangling rows pass part of the honest mass to the farm. Since the dangling nodes lie outside $\mathcal{S}$, $r_D\le 1-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}} r_i$, and substituting this into Equation~\eqref{eq:closed-cluster} gives a bound that holds on any graph:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cluster-mass}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}} r_i \le \frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{(1-d)\,|V| + d\,|\mathcal{S}|}.
\end{equation}
For a farm that is small against $|V|$, the bound is close to $1/(1-d)$ times the population share. On the EndorseRank graph of the matched cohort at $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$, with $2{,}513{,}982$ nodes, the pipeline's own PageRank code gives $r_D=0.365$, $0.394$ and $0.419$ at $d=0.75$, $0.85$ and $0.95$, so a closed farm on this graph collects 2.10, 3.23 and 8.96 times its population share. A direct check with the same code agrees: a ring of 20 addresses, each approving the next, added to the graph collects 3.23 times its population share at $d=0.85$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:cluster-mass} limits the farm's total mass, not the score of one target, and the attacker can concentrate that mass. Take a star in which $f$ farm addresses each approve the target $t$ and $t$ approves each of them back. Every farm address $a$ has $t$ as its only out-neighbor, so $P_{at}=1$, and the row of $t$ sums to one over the farm. Write $c=(1-d+d\,r_D)/|V|$ for the mass that each node receives from teleportation and the dangling rows. The balance equations $r_a=c+d\,r_t P_{ta}$ and $r_t=c+d\sum_a r_a$ then give
\begin{equation*}
r_t = \frac{c\,(1+df)}{1-d^2}.
\end{equation*}
At $d=0.85$, $c=1.93\times10^{-7}$, which is the common score of every address that receives no allowance. With $f=10$ (eleven addresses and twenty approvals), the target reaches $r_t=6.60\times10^{-6}$ on the graph with the star added, above the median EndorseRank of the cohort contracts but only $2.4\times10^{-4}$ of the highest ($0.0278$). The score grows linearly in $f$: each further farm address adds $d\,c/(1-d^2)$ to $r_t$ and costs two approvals. Solved for $f$, the formula needs about 25 farm addresses to reach the cohort's 90th percentile, about 2{,}292 its 99th and about 47{,}073 its highest score. Looking ordinary therefore takes a few dozen approvals and reaching the top about 94{,}000, two per farm address, since the highest-ranked cohort contract holds about 3\% of the graph's stationary mass. Because the graph keeps only the approvals of the twelve months before each freeze, the farm has to emit these approvals again at least once a year to keep the target's rank (Section~\ref{sub:sybil-cost}). This study did not measure $c_{\mathrm{gas}}$, so these counts are not turned into a price.

Honest in-edges are therefore not needed to reach the upper part of the ranking, but bought ones add to the gain. When the farm has no out-edges to honest addresses, an edge $(h,a)$ bought from an honest owner $h$ adds, to first order,
\begin{equation*}
\Delta\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}} r_i \approx \frac{d\,r_h\,P_{ha}}{1-d}
\end{equation*}
to the farm's mass, where $P_{ha}$ is the share of $h$'s row that the new edge takes. A fresh large or unlimited approval bought from $h$, with $V(z)$ close to one, takes about $\sigma(0)/(o(h)+\sigma(0))$ of $h$'s row; for the median owner in the graph that share is $0.683$. The edge an attacker would buy is therefore a large or unlimited approval from an owner whose own approvals are old, small or few, preferably one with a high $r_h$. Such an approval puts the owner's balance of the token at the attacker's disposal, so the risk premium $\rho_h$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:marginal} grows with what the owner holds and is close to zero only for an owner that holds none of the token. In the capped allowance layer of C-PR such an approval takes a large share of a row only if it is large against the owner's other allowances (Section~\ref{sub:coupled-graph}). The attack's cost-effectiveness is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:efficiency}
\eta = \frac{\Delta J(\mathcal{G})}{C},
\end{equation}
with $\Delta J(\mathcal{G})$ the gain in the objective of Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} that the attack edges produce, held over the $q$ windows that $C$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:total-cost} pays for.

Equations~\eqref{eq:closed-cluster}--\eqref{eq:efficiency} show that EndorseRank, as defined here, is open to cheap inflation by closed farms. Under uniform teleportation and uniform redistribution of dangling mass, a closed cluster is not held to its population share. The coupled walk is open to the same farm. The derivation of Equation~\eqref{eq:cluster-mass} uses only the uniform teleportation and the uniform spread of dangling mass, which C-PR shares; a farm address whose only out-edges are approvals follows them with weight one whatever $\lambda$ is (Equation~\eqref{eq:coupled-transition}), and a single out-edge takes the whole row whatever its amount, so the cap $U$ does not protect C-PR either. The farm factors and star sizes above were computed on the allowance graph only; on the union graph of C-PR the dangling mass, and with it $c$, differ and were not computed. The control that does bound closed clusters changes the walk itself: if teleportation and dangling redistribution go only to a vetted seed set, as in TrustRank \parencite{gyongyi2004trustrank}, a cluster outside that set gains mass only through edges from honest owners, which the attacker has to buy at the price of Equation~\eqref{eq:marginal}. AWP's restart rule gives the transfer side no protection either: a farm whose addresses send small amounts to one another raises their activeness and so draws restart mass to itself, and EndorseRank with activity restarts inherits the same exposure. The Sybil-adjusted stability proxies (Section~\ref{sub:proxy-sybil}) flag repeated transfers from a few addresses but not a farm of fresh addresses, so they cannot replace an adversarial assessment, which we leave to future work.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:regulatory-ethics]{Regulatory and ethical considerations}

On-chain reputation metrics raise questions of transparency as well as equity. Earlier legal work has shown that decentralized finance markets remain bound by disclosure and consumer protection rules even though settlement happens on-chain \parencite{zetzsche2020defi}. \textcite{packin2024credit} add a warning of their own: decentralized credit scoring may perpetuate black-box harms when models cannot be interpreted or contested, or when they are linked to identity databases without due process. C-PR, EndorseRank and AWP are all computed deterministically from on-chain events and a public daily price series with open-source algorithms, so anyone can recalculate them using the open-source software (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). That openness partly answers the black-box criticism. It leaves the distributional concern untouched, because contracts with little activity, new ones among them, will score lower whatever their code.

Ethically, these scores should never be used as security ratings, as creditworthiness measures in the traditional sense, or as evidence that the owners who approve a contract are distinct individuals \parencite{douceur2002sybil}. They are rank-based indicators of a contract's position in the defined graph topology. If a deployment grants financial services on the strength of score thresholds, it should clearly document the graph definitions behind the scores, the timeframe, and the limitations, above all the exposure to Sybil attacks. A regulatory framework centered on interpretability would prefer this kind of ranking to proprietary machine learning models like those \textcite{kotb2023credit} reviews, as long as providers make no exaggerated claims about how well they can predict loan repayment rates \parencite{packin2024credit}.

Privacy on an open blockchain is limited by the ledger's very nature \parencite{nakamoto2008bitcoin}, and computing the reputation scores draws on nothing beyond public data. Further attempts to uncover the identity behind each address are discouraged, and the scores should not be treated as permanent, fingerprint-like identifiers. This study scores contract addresses alone and makes no attempt to link any address to a real-world individual (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:threats-validity]{Threats to validity}

The findings reported here should be read against the limitations below, which are grouped under construct, internal, external and adversarial validity.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:threat-construct]{Construct validity}

The threats below concern whether the scores and checks measure what they are read as measuring.

\begin{itemize}
\item These ratings are not credit ratings: no default labels from uncollateralized lending enter the analysis.
\item Same-window checks rest on local allowance and transfer statistics.
\item Holdout construct. A future new approval is still an endorsement event and a future new sender a flow event: both lie outside the score window, yet neither is an independent credit or trading construct. Being counts of arrivals, they also favor any score that tracks the current count of counterparties, and in both windows the raw counts rank them best (Section~\ref{sub:finding-rq3}).
\item Unit of analysis. The walks over allowances score the receiving side of an approval, and every scored address here is a contract (Section~\ref{sub:cohorts}). Claims about the reputation of traders rest on the W0 trader run and the comparison of Section~\ref{sub:finding-neutral}, both on small samples.
\item Own-construct agreement is partly mechanical. EndorseRank smooths spender in-approve degree over the whole graph, and AWP does the same for incoming transfers weighted by their value, so each score is expected by construction to agree with its own family. The within-score contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:tau-diff} were computed for this reason; with few ties they compare like with like, but they remain checks of the instruments.
\item Value weights. EndorseRank and AWP pass each US-dollar amount through $V(z)$ with a slope set so that the median amount weighs one half (Section~\ref{sec:amount-revision}), so the weights record the value of the approvals and transfers an edge carries as well as their number and age. The slopes are choices of the revision, checked only in the same window (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-slope}). The layers of C-PR add dollar amounts without the bound of $V$, capping each allowance at $U$ (Section~\ref{sub:coupled-graph}). On both spender holdouts EndorseRank ranks both labels slightly better than the allowance layer walked alone (Tables~\ref{tab:holdout-spenders} and~\ref{tab:fresh-posthoc}), without a paired contrast, so how amounts are weighted may matter for this construct, and neither weighting is shown to be the right one. Such weighting choices propagate into $\mathbf{r}$ and into the rank correlations \parencite{langville2006pagerank}.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:threat-internal]{Internal validity}

The threats below concern whether the comparisons are free of choices that could have produced their results.

\begin{itemize}
\item Shared score windows. Every method and proxy is computed over the twelve months before its freeze, 1~July~2025 to 30~June~2026 in the same window and W1 and 1~April~2025 to 31~March~2026 in W0. Market regimes peculiar to those months (volatility, incentive programs, protocol changes) may push alignment up or down. An allowance last set before the window began is not an edge even while it is in force, so a contract whose owners approved it long ago and have not approved it again loses those edges. The checks on damping and value slopes measure sensitivity to these parameters, but none of them varies the calendar window.
\item Cohort selection. The matched cohort is the 6,483 contracts on Arbitrum One that received non-zero approvals of one of the five tokens from at least three owners in the twelve months before $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$, and results may not carry over to contracts with fewer approvals or with approvals of other tokens only. Nor do the graphs see every cohort contract (Section~\ref{sub:cohorts}), and the sample-definition sensitivity shows how strongly the coefficients react when the evaluation population changes (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-sample-size}).
\item Baseline fidelity. AWP follows the published method in its edge weights and its restarts (Section~\ref{sub:awp-graph}). The paper gives no parameter values, so $k$, $t_0$ and $b$ are this study's choices (see value weights above). The paper scored Ethereum transactions; here AWP scores transfers of five ERC-20 tokens on Arbitrum One, valued in US dollars, on the cohort's ego network, which limits the activeness of a sender outside the cohort to its transfers to cohort contracts.
\item Order of analysis. The cohorts, scores, labels, statistics and rule A were fixed in the analysis plan of 9~October~2026, before any log of the study was extracted. Rule B with its margin, the two sensitivity analyses and the comparison on trader outcomes were registered later that day, after the W0 results were known and before the study extracted the July--September logs (Section~\ref{sub:fresh-holdout}). Every other comparison, the degree contrasts among them, was computed after the relevant labels were known and is reported with intervals; none of them decides a question.
\item Comparators of the rules. Rules A and B compare C-PR with its own layers walked alone, the only contrast that changes the coupling and nothing else. C-PR also differs from EndorseRank and AWP in its amount weights and its uniform restarts, so its contrasts with them (Table~\ref{tab:cpr-contrasts}) mix the coupling with those choices; the registered sensitivity analysis with AWP as the comparator is the only rule-bound contrast with a published walk.
\item Dependence and leakage. Contracts that share owners or a protocol are resampled as independent, and the post hoc contrasts are not adjusted for multiplicity (Section~\ref{sec:statistical-tests}). The cohort of each freeze is drawn from the approvals of its own score window, so no label window chooses the contracts around which its data are drawn (Section~\ref{sec:holdout-protocol}); the five tokens, however, were chosen by their rows in the score window of $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$, which holds the label window of W0.
\item Post hoc definition of the main analysis. The amounts, the token list and the score windows of the revised analysis were defined after the registered results of all three windows were known (Section~\ref{sec:amount-revision}). The revised analysis, the main analysis of this thesis, is therefore post hoc, and its verdicts under rules A, B and R1--R3 are a re-analysis, not registered tests. Its slopes and cap come from quantiles of the W0 score window, which ends at $t_1$, and the registered analysis reached the same verdicts on rules A and B and on R2 and R3; R1 holds on the revised scores and did not in the registered analysis (Appendix~\ref{sec:map-registered}). Some contrasts differ between the two, among them F3 and the sign of AWP minus the transfer in-degree on new senders in both windows, which lay above zero in the registered analysis (Table~\ref{tab:registered-vs-revised}), and the signs of Q in W0 and of S-PR against the transfer walk on new senders in W0.
\item Timing environment. All runtimes are wall-clock times from one machine (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). The standard deviations in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime} cover only repeated solves of one configuration, and in the damping sweep each graph--damping pair is timed once. Variation between machines is not measured, so the comparison is stated in ratios instead of seconds.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:threat-external]{External validity}

The threats below concern how far the results carry beyond the data studied.

\begin{itemize}
\item Single chain and window. The evidence comes from Arbitrum One alone, from contracts that at least three owners approved on one of five tokens, over the twelve months of scored activity before each freeze and two three-month label windows. Extending the findings to other rollups, Layer-1 chains or lending-centered venues would need cross-protocol validation, since DeFi venues differ in protocol design \parencite{werner2022sok} and in market structure \parencite{schar2021defi}.
\item Sample scaling versus alignment scaling. The subsample stages bear only on runtime on subgraphs of the matched-cohort graph (Section~\ref{sub:runtime-scaling}); they do not show that the $\tau$ profile holds at other population sizes.
\item Ego-network depth. Each graph keeps only the edges incident to the cohort contracts, so the findings about propagation do not carry over to graphs with longer endorsement paths (Section~\ref{sub:theory-closed-form}).
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:threat-adversarial]{Adversarial validity}

The threats below concern what an attacker could do to the scores.

\begin{itemize}
\item Sybil and wash trading. Rankings may be manipulated through adversarial allowance or transfer patterns \parencite{douceur2002sybil}; the Sybil-adjusted proxies only partly address this, and the cost model of Section~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model} is analytical, not an empirical red-team evaluation.
\item Strategic approval behavior. Once a score built on allowances is live, rational actors may grant allowances strategically to farm rank. An observational $\tau$ estimated before deployment may overstate how well the ranking resists such behavior.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:limitations]{Limitations}

On this sample, the strengths of C-PR are a ranking of both spender labels above EndorseRank in both windows, agreement with both families that lies between its two layers, and an order among the contracts that the transfer walk leaves tied. Its weaknesses are the failure of both rules, a ranking of later senders below AWP and the transfer in-degree, a ranking of later approvals below the in-approve degree in both windows and, in W1, below EndorseRank with activity restarts, a liquidation-free close rate ranked below the in-approve degree, a Sybil-stability coefficient below AWP's and the highest cost of the three walks.

The coupled operator was tried at three fixed values of $\lambda$, with uniform teleportation and a per-node convex mixture at the transition level, on two layers weighted by dollar amounts, with each allowance capped at $U$. Layers weighted as in EndorseRank and AWP, node-dependent or learned weights, mixtures of the stationary vectors instead of the transition rows, and other Markov chains are not evaluated in this thesis, so the answers to RQ4 and RQ5 apply only to that family of methods on this dataset. Deployment remains bound by the considerations of Section~\ref{sec:regulatory-ethics}.

EndorseRank, the allowance reference, agrees with the allowance checks, ranks differently from AWP and costs little because its graph is sparse. It carries little transfer information, ranks later approvals below the raw in-approve degree in both windows (computed after the labels were known) and, like C-PR, is exposed to closed farms. None of the scores says anything about contracts that few owners approve or about externally owned accounts, which are not scored (Section~\ref{sec:data-sources}).

The data add four limits. The graphs hold five tokens only (Table~\ref{tab:usd-tokens}), so a contract that owners approve only on other tokens is outside the cohort. Every price comes from one public service, the daily series of DefiLlama \parencite{defillamanddocs}, so an error in it on some date would pass into every amount of that day (Appendix~\ref{sec:data-tokens}). The account type was read once for each address, at blocks of 9~October~2026 after both label windows (Table~\ref{tab:account-types}), so a contract that destroyed itself before EIP-6780 \parencite{ballet2023eip6780} is left out as an externally owned account (Section~\ref{sub:cohorts}). And the trader cohorts are small (Section~\ref{sub:cohorts}), so every trader result has wide intervals.

None of the scores draws on off-chain evidence about who controls an address; their only off-chain inputs are the token list and the daily price series. Unlike attestation tooling such as Human Passport \parencite{humanpassportnddocs}, they give no guarantee that two highly ranked addresses are controlled by different actors, so they should be read as orderings of an already admitted population and not as a Sybil defense. Counting only the approvals of owners that pass an attestation gate before computing C-PR or EndorseRank is a plausible deployment, but the gate's own weaknesses would carry over to it \parencite{bordeianu2026sybil}, and evaluating the combination needs labeled Sybil data that this study did not collect.

These limitations narrow what can be concluded. For this cohort of contracts and time frame, C-PR is a walk that carries authorization into a transfer-based reputation score at a loss on inflow, and EndorseRank a cheap, allowance-specific score for spender contracts; neither is a credit scoring method, nor a Sybil-resistant identity mechanism, nor a substitute for protocol-level collateralization \parencite{gudgeon2020crisis}. The allowance edge they rest on is worth adding to on-chain reputation, and the approval count is the cheapest way to use it. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} restates what has been shown, what that knowledge is worth and what it would take to extend these bounds.
