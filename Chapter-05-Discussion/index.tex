\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reported what was measured on Arbitrum One. This chapter says what those measurements mean. Each research question and each objective of Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} is judged against the criterion stated there (Sections~\ref{sec:findings-rq}--\ref{sec:objectives-achievement}). The same tables then support the theoretical reading (Section~\ref{sec:theoretical-implications}), the deployment reading (Section~\ref{sec:practical-implications}), the case for a cheap, auditable, authorisation-aware score (Section~\ref{sec:societal-impact}) and a cost model for a Sybil attack on an allowance graph (Section~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model}). Regulatory and ethical points, threats to validity and limitations close the chapter (Sections~\ref{sec:regulatory-ethics}--\ref{sec:limitations}). Same-window statements refer to the matched cohort and holdout statements to the spender cohort unless noted.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:findings-rq]{Findings by research question}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq1]{RQ1: Reputation structure and rank divergence}

RQ1 asks whether EndorseRank and AWP produce meaningfully different wallet orderings on an identical sample. The inter-method Kendall $\tau$ and its interval (Table~\ref{tab:alignment-family-ci}, last row) sit well inside the open interval between unrelated and identical orderings: correlation is present, because both methods draw on overlapping wallet activity in one window, but a large share of pairwise orderings disagree (Figure~\ref{fig:rank-scatter}).

The divergence is structurally expected. The endorsement graph encodes current spending authorisation with about one ninth as many edges as the transfer graph, which encodes time-decayed economic flow\cend{3}. PageRank propagates influence along whatever edges it is given under the same damping convention \cend{15}, and the sensitivity of the stationary vector to the edge set is well characterised \cend{23}; different edge sets therefore yield different stationary mass. A wallet that receives large transfers without granting allowances ranks highly under AWP and modestly under EndorseRank, and conversely for a wallet that authorises spenders without being a transfer hub. H1 is supported. The practical corollary is that the two scores are not substitutes: adopting EndorseRank for efficiency means accepting a different ranking geometry, not a faster approximation of AWP.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq2]{RQ2: Construct-specific alignment}

RQ2 asks which method aligns more strongly with contemporaneous allowance checks and with contemporaneous transfer checks. Tables~\ref{tab:alignment-family-ci} and~\ref{tab:tau-diff} show construct-specific winners. AWP leads on the transfer family and EndorseRank on the allowance family, and both between-method paired differences exclude zero in opposite directions.

Two qualifications matter for how H2 is read. First, each method's own-construct agreement is partly mechanical, because the proxies of a family are computed from the same event stream that builds the corresponding graph, which makes RQ2 a construct-validity observation in the sense of Cronbach and Meehl \cend{22} rather than external validation. Second, the within-method contrasts are null: EndorseRank's allowance alignment is not statistically higher than its own transfer alignment, and its agreement with in-approve degree is not statistically higher than its agreement with transfer in-degree (Table~\ref{tab:tau-diff}, contrasts~i and~vi). The evidence therefore supports the between-method statement (transfer centrality does not recover authorisation, and endorsement centrality does not recover transfer volume as well as AWP) but not a claim that EndorseRank is ``more about'' allowances than about transfers within its own ranking. H2 is supported in that construct-specific, between-method form.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq3]{RQ3: Computational efficiency}

RQ3 asks whether EndorseRank is computationally more efficient than AWP on the same matched sample. Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime} answers affirmatively on wall-clock time, memory and iterations, and reports the edge counts next to the runtimes so that the ratio can be attributed: the endorsement graph is about one ninth the size of the transfer graph, and per-iteration cost is linear in $|E|$ \cend{23}. The advantage follows from representing authorisation as a latest-allowance snapshot rather than as an accumulated, logistically decayed transfer history\cend{3}; nothing in the solver differs between the two methods.

The node-count sensitivity of Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-scaling} qualifies the claim rather than extending it. The runtime ratio stays within a narrow band across staged node counts, but the pool stages add addresses without adding extracted edges, so they do not test how either method scales when the edge population itself grows. Damping and top-token perturbations (Tables~\ref{tab:robustness-damping} and~\ref{tab:robustness-tokens}) leave the alignment pattern unchanged; the runtime ratio does move with damping. H3 is supported for the matched cohort and for the bounded node-count experiment that was actually run.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq4]{RQ4: Temporal holdout of future new approvals}

RQ4 has two clauses. The first asks whether scores frozen at the end of February predict new owner--spender approvals in the following three months on inbound spenders more strongly than AWP. Table~\ref{tab:holdout-spenders} shows that EndorseRank does and AWP does not; EndorseRank's interval excludes zero, and the paired difference to AWP (Table~\ref{tab:holdout-diff}) is the largest contrast in the thesis. Because the labels post-date every scored event, this is not a same-window construct check, and it is the strongest evidence that endorsement centrality carries information about spenders that transfer centrality does not. The same ordering holds, more weakly, on the flow label: EndorseRank predicts new transfer senders better than AWP does on this cohort.

The second clause asks whether EndorseRank is more informative than the same-window allowance degree, and the answer is no. The spender's in-approve degree at $t_1$ predicts new approval pairs with $\tau=0.711$, EndorseRank with $0.479$, and the paired increment is $-0.233$ with an interval that lies well below zero. AWP stands in the same relation to the transfer in-degree on new senders. The reading is simple. A count of new counterparties over three months grows with the count of existing counterparties, and the local degree carries that signal without the dilution that owner-side centrality and allowance value introduce when the count is propagated. The out-of-window content of EndorseRank is therefore inherited from the degree it smooths, not added to it. This does not remove the first clause: EndorseRank still separates from AWP by construct out of window. It does remove any claim that propagation adds predictive value over the degree for this label, and it applies with equal force to the transfer-graph baseline.

RQ4 differs from the research proposal, which planned a comparison against a trading-success reference. That comparison was withdrawn because the outcome-native reference was built from the same GMX events as the proxies it would have been validated against (Section~\ref{sub:gf-pr}); the holdout replaces it with labels that are out of window but still of the same constructs: new approvals and new senders, with no profit or repayment component. What RQ4 shows is temporal predictive validity of the endorsement construct relative to the transfer construct, bounded above by the raw degree; it says nothing about credit risk.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:finding-rq5]{RQ5: Coupling the two edge types}

RQ5 asks whether a single walk over both layers ranks wallets better than either single-layer method. It does not, under the rule fixed before the run. On the holdout, C-PR at $\lambda=0.5$ falls between EndorseRank and AWP on both labels, the paired difference to EndorseRank on new approval pairs lies below zero, and the paired difference to AWP on new senders includes zero (Table~\ref{tab:holdout-diff}). The sensitivity values order monotonically: more allowance weight raises $\tau$ on both labels, so the transfer layer contributes nothing out of window for spenders that the allowance layer does not already contribute. In the same window (Section~\ref{sec:coupled-results}) C-PR tracks AWP to within a few thousandths on the transfer and Sybil-stability families and is indistinguishable from zero on the allowance family, so the secondary rule fails on its allowance part; the seeded ablation is below the method it borrows from on every family. The mixture is dominated by the denser layer, and $\lambda$ acts only at the minority of nodes that have out-edges in both.

The ablation locates the reason. S-PR, which keeps the transfer walk and restarts it from the EndorseRank distribution, reproduces AWP on the flow label and loses almost the whole endorsement signal on the approval label. Once transfer edges govern propagation, an endorsement-derived teleportation vector changes little. The allowance edge earns its result by being walked on, not by being used as a seed, and mixing it with transfer edges at the transition level dilutes it in proportion to the transfer weight. That is a statement about these two layers on this chain; a learned or node-dependent $\lambda$, or a coupling that keeps the layers separate and combines their stationary vectors afterwards, was not tested and is left to future work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

The negative answer is reported as a contribution rather than hidden, for two reasons. The rule was pre-registered, so the result cannot have been shaped to the data. And it bounds a natural next step for anyone who reads the crossed pattern of Table~\ref{tab:alignment-family-ci} as an invitation to combine: on this evidence a convex mixture of the two edge types at the transition level does not produce a score that beats the better single-layer method on either construct.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:theoretical-implications]{Theoretical implications}

The findings bear on three strands of theory: hyperlink-style propagation on financial graphs, construct validity for on-chain scores, and the economics of trust under pseudonymity.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:theory-propagation]{Propagation semantics beyond transfers}

PageRank's original formulation treats inbound hyperlinks as citations \cend{15}. Do and Do\cend{4} carried that metaphor to Ethereum transactions, and Do, Do and Nguyen\cend{3} refined it into AWP by weighting transfer edges with value and a logistic decay of age. EndorseRank carries the metaphor to ERC-20 allowances: an owner who authorises a spender issues a directed endorsement of spending authority \cend{18}. The divergence measured under RQ1 shows that changing the edge semantics changes the ranking geometry even when the node set is held fixed. Graph-based reputation is therefore a family of methods whose validity depends on which relationships are encoded as edges \cend{23}. Trust-propagation systems in peer-to-peer and web settings made the same point from the other side: in EigenTrust \cend{16} and TrustRank \cend{17}, seed sets and edge definitions dominate outcomes. EndorseRank contributes an edge definition native to a token standard rather than to a message-passing overlay or a hyperlink graph.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:theory-construct]{Multi-construct domain alignment}

The check design separates transfer centrality from allowance delegation and tests each method on both. The resulting pattern, each method converging with its own construct and diverging from the other's, is what classical construct-validity reasoning predicts for indicators that measure different things \cend{22}, and Messick's \cend{33} unified view adds that the consequences of using a score are part of its validity. Rank correlations \cend{20,21} operationalise the reasoning for ordinal reputation scores without imposing classification thresholds, and the paired bootstrap \cend{34} turns the pattern into tested differences rather than informal comparison of point estimates. The null within-method contrasts show that convergent evidence on a method's own construct is not the same as discriminant evidence within the method.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:theory-pseudonymity]{Reputation under cheap pseudonyms}

Public ledgers permit cheap identity creation \cend{5}, and Ethereum's account model makes address creation free of any registration step \cend{36}. Reputation systems that reward inbound transfers or allowances are therefore exposed to Sybil strategies in which an attacker manufactures edges among controlled addresses \cend{11}. The empirical results do not eliminate that threat; they show that allowance and transfer graphs respond differently to the activity present in the observational sample. Section~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model} formalises attack cost on allowance graphs and argues that non-zero allowances to external spenders impose opportunity and revocation costs that transfer wash trading does not.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:practical-implications]{Practical implications and deployment scenarios}

Protocols and infrastructure providers face a design choice between transfer-hub detection and authorisation-aware reputation. The empirical trade-off supports scenario-dependent adoption rather than a single recommended score.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-transfer]{Transfer-hub and flow monitoring}

Protocols that prioritise detection of high-flow wallets, whether for market-maker identification, routing analysis or transfer-based compliance heuristics, have reason to prefer AWP despite its cost. Its transfer alignment (Table~\ref{tab:alignment-transfer}) makes it the better match when the operational target is economic flow centrality\cend{3}. Its runtime on the matched cohort (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}) is acceptable for batch analytics and less attractive for near-real-time refresh.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-allowance]{Authorisation-aware and low-latency refresh}

Protocols that emphasise explicit spending authorisation (token vaults, allowance-gated integrations, risk engines that treat approvals as trust acts) have reason to prefer EndorseRank. Its allowance alignment (Table~\ref{tab:alignment-allowance}), its holdout result (Table~\ref{tab:holdout-spenders}) and its sparse graph and short solve time (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}) support frequent recomputation. Operators accept weaker transfer alignment in exchange for authorisation semantics, out-of-window evidence and speed. One caution follows from Table~\ref{tab:holdout-diff}: an operator whose only question is how many new approvals a spender will attract is better served by the spender's current in-approve degree than by any PageRank, and should use the propagated score for what propagation adds, which is resistance to a single owner's weight and a ranking that accounts for who the endorsing owners are.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-hybrid]{Evaluation design and combined use}

Combining the two signals is a deployment question and a method question, and the evidence answers them differently. Running EndorseRank for continuous authorisation-aware monitoring and AWP for periodic transfer-hub audits is feasible and costs the sum of the two solves. Folding the two edge types into one walk is not supported: the coupled operator costs as much as AWP (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}) and ranks no better than the appropriate single-layer method on either construct (Section~\ref{sub:finding-rq5}). An operator who wants both constructs should compute both scores and keep them separate. The open pipeline enables cross-protocol replication on any ERC-20 chain with allowance logs (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). Integration with live risk oracles remains future work (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}), but EndorseRank's efficiency profile is compatible with refresh cadences that a transfer-graph method may not sustain at equal cost.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-industry]{Industry adoption scenarios}

Both methods supply a cheap, standardised and independently recomputable ordinal signal. Value capture is hypothesised to run through fees, utilisation, monitoring cost and reduced reward leakage, not through reducing collateral. The scenarios below map actors to decision problems consistent with the results.

\begin{itemize}
\item DEX operators. Protocols that earn from trading fees must decide which wallets to prioritise for monitoring, fee tiers or feature access while refreshing reputation frequently. EndorseRank fits this loop because its score tracks authorisation activity and can be recomputed in seconds without transfer-history aggregation. Any revenue effect would run through fee volume and lower monitoring cost; fee and monitoring policies are the justified levers.
\item Lending protocols. Lending markets seek higher capital utilisation without abandoning collateralisation \cend{41}; the 2020 stress episodes show what happens when risk parameters lag \cend{9}. Reputation can enter as an auxiliary signal for interest-rate or fee tiers and review cadence on already-collateralised positions, not as a substitute for collateral. EndorseRank is the natural input when the protocol already relies on ERC-20 approvals for deposits and borrows (Section~\ref{sec:allowance-endorsement}). The scores are not default predictors; any collateral-differentiation experiment remains future work.
\item Wallet and security tooling. Users and integrators must decide whether a spender contract is widely authorised. Displaying a spender's EndorseRank, or the distribution of inbound endorsement mass, as a trust badge operationalises allowance semantics as explicit delegation \cend{18,19}. The badge reports graph position under published edge definitions; it is not a security audit or a guarantee against malicious contracts.
\item Liquidity providers and market makers. These actors need transfer hubs and flow structure for inventory and spread management. AWP is the better method for that target\cend{3}; the operational channel is lower screening cost, not improved profit and loss.
\item Airdrop and governance programmes. Token distributions and governance eligibility are routinely gamed by Sybil clusters \cend{11,32}. Programmes can require inbound endorsement diversity or apply the closed-cluster mass bound of Section~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model} (Equation~\ref{eq:cluster-mass}) as a filter before rewards are paid. Such filters mitigate closed-cluster inflation; they do not stop open-cluster attacks that recruit honest inbound endorsements.
\item Analytics and compliance providers. Exchanges, funds and compliance teams need auditable counterparty rankings that third parties can recompute. The open pipeline supports publishing EndorseRank and AWP under disclosed windows and parameters; auditability is the differentiator relative to opaque machine-learning scores \cend{2}. Providers should disclose Sybil exposure and the fact that the scores are not profit forecasts alongside any surfaced rank.
\end{itemize}

In none of these scenarios is reputation a substitute for collateral; the standardised, auditable, low-latency ordinal signal is a way to lower the cost of counterparty differentiation.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:deploy-multiples]{Standardised scores as shared infrastructure}

The practical value of a reputation score, like that of any standardised financial ratio, is that it is public, comparable and cheap to recompute, so that many parties can reason from the same number while knowing how it can be gamed. Forecasting power is secondary. EndorseRank and AWP can play that infrastructural role for pseudonymous wallets: they are computed from public logs by an open pipeline (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}), cheap enough to refresh, and independently verifiable. Neither is a profit forecast, and both are useful only if their construction is public and their failure modes are named (Sections~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model} and~\ref{sec:regulatory-ethics}).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:societal-impact]{Societal and practical impact}

Counterparty screening in DeFi is expensive and unevenly available. Well-resourced actors run proprietary risk engines over full transfer histories; retail users rely on informal signals or none. A score that can be refreshed at a fraction of the transfer-graph cost (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime}) and that reads directly off the approvals users already grant lowers the resource barrier to systematic screening. Because approvals are also the mechanism through which most token-draining exploits operate (Section~\ref{sec:approval-security}), a spender-centred endorsement score speaks to a risk that users face directly and that transfer volume does not capture.

For protocol operators, the results support a shift from a single ``reputation'' to construct-specific instruments chosen for the decision at hand. That shift reduces the risk of mis-specified gating (using a flow score to decide an authorisation question, or the reverse) and, because both instruments here are computed from public data by a published pipeline, it allows gating rules to be audited by the users they affect. The efficiency result makes continuous monitoring economically feasible for smaller protocols that could not sustain periodic transfer-graph refresh at equal cost.

For regulators and the public interest, the contribution is an existence proof that a useful on-chain reputation signal can be fully transparent: the input events are public, the parameters are versioned, and each table can be regenerated from them. That differs from the black-box decentralised credit scores whose due-process risks Packin and Lev-Aretz\cend{2} describe, and it matches the explainability that regulatory analyses of DeFi call for \cend{63}. The same transparency imposes an obligation, developed in Section~\ref{sec:regulatory-ethics}, not to let an ordinal graph statistic be mistaken for identity, creditworthiness or proof of personhood.

The evidence is from one chain and one six-month window, and it concerns the endorsement construct, not credit outcomes. Social benefit therefore depends on deployments that respect the construct boundary. Any reputation signal that gates value also invites strategic behaviour. The cost model of the next section shows where that behaviour is expensive, without showing that it is impossible.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:sybil-cost-model]{Sybil attack cost model on allowance graphs}

Sybil attacks exploit cheap pseudonyms to manufacture reputation \cend{11}. On transfer graphs, an attacker can wash-trade value among controlled wallets, inflating in-degree and in-value at the cost of fees and inventory risk \cend{29}. On allowance graphs the attack surface differs, because edges represent authorisation rather than completed transfers. This section formalises a minimal cost model for inflating EndorseRank through fabricated allowances.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:sybil-setup]{Attack setup}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the set of addresses controlled by an attacker and $\mathcal{H}$ the set of honest wallets in the endorsement graph $G_{\text{approve}}=(V,E,w)$. An edge $(u,v)$ with weight $w_{uv}>0$ denotes that $u$ currently authorises $v$ to spend decimal-adjusted token units totalling $w_{uv}$. EndorseRank computes the PageRank vector $\mathbf{r}$ on $G_{\text{approve}}$ with damping $d\in(0,1)$ \cend{15}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pagerank-sybil}
\mathbf{r} = d\, P^{\top} \mathbf{r} + \frac{1-d}{|V|}\mathbf{1},
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the row-stochastic matrix obtained by normalising outgoing allowance weights, with the standard handling of dangling nodes \cend{23}.

The attacker seeks to maximise the mean EndorseRank of a target subset $T\subseteq\mathcal{A}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
J(T) = \frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{i\in T} r_i,
\end{equation}
subject to a budget on attack cost defined below.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:sybil-cost]{Cost components}

Fabricating an allowance edge $(u,v)$ with weight $w_{uv}$ incurs at least three cost components.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Transaction cost. Submitting \texttt{approve} or \texttt{permit} consumes gas. Let $c_{\mathrm{gas}}$ be the expected fee per allowance update. For $m$ updates,
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{gas}} = m\, c_{\mathrm{gas}}.
\end{equation}

\item Capital lock and opportunity cost. A non-zero allowance to an external spender $v\notin\mathcal{A}$ exposes tokens owned by $u$ to transfer by $v$. Even if $v\in\mathcal{A}$, maintaining large allowances to contracts or routers may be required to mimic honest behaviour. Let $\ell(w_{uv})$ be the opportunity cost of capital associated with weight $w_{uv}$ over the reputation refresh horizon (for example, forgone yield). Then
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{cap}} = \sum_{(u,v)\in E_{\mathcal{A}}} \ell(w_{uv}),
\end{equation}
where $E_{\mathcal{A}}$ denotes attacker-created edges.

\item Revocation and detection cost. Honest users revoke stale allowances; attackers who never revoke may be distinguishable. Let $c_{\mathrm{rev}}$ be the expected cost of periodic revocation and re-approval needed to maintain a target edge set over $k$ refresh epochs:
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{rev}} = k\, c_{\mathrm{rev}}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

Total attack cost is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total-cost}
C = C_{\mathrm{gas}} + C_{\mathrm{cap}} + C_{\mathrm{rev}}.
\end{equation}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:sybil-bound]{Influence and a cost-effectiveness bound}

Under standard PageRank sensitivity results, the influence of an edge on $\mathbf{r}$ is mediated by the stationary mass at the source and the normalised outgoing weight \cend{23}. A coarse upper bound on the gain in $J(T)$ from adding edges among $\mathcal{A}$ alone (a closed Sybil cluster with no honest inbound endorsements) is limited by the teleportation mass:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:closed-cluster}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}} r_i \leq |\mathcal{A}|\cdot\frac{1-d}{|V|} + d\cdot \sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}} r_i,
\end{equation}
which rearranges to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cluster-mass}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}} r_i \leq \frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{|V|}.
\end{equation}
A closed attacker cluster therefore cannot capture more than its population share of total rank mass. To exceed that share, the attacker needs inbound endorsements from $\mathcal{H}$, which requires either compromising honest wallets or inducing honest owners to approve attacker-controlled spenders; the cost of either action is typically dominated by $C_{\mathrm{cap}}$ and social-engineering effort rather than by gas alone.

When the attacker purchases inbound allowances from honest owners (for example, by operating a service that requests approvals), the marginal cost per unit of honest-sourced weight $w_{h a}$ can be written
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal}
c_{\mathrm{in}}(w_{h a}) = c_{\mathrm{gas}} + \ell_{\mathrm{honest}}(w_{h a}) + \pi(w_{h a}),
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{\mathrm{honest}}$ is compensation paid to the honest owner and $\pi$ is a risk premium for potential loss from malicious spending. Cost-effectiveness of the attack is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:efficiency}
\eta = \frac{\Delta J(T)}{C},
\end{equation}
with $\Delta J(T)$ the increase in mean target rank attributable to the attack edges.

Equations~\eqref{eq:closed-cluster}--\eqref{eq:efficiency} imply that EndorseRank is not Sybil-proof, but that closed-cluster inflation is population-share limited and that cross-cluster inflation requires costly honest-sourced allowances. Transfer wash trading faces different costs (fees and inventory) but does not require exposing spending authority to external addresses. The Sybil-adjusted stability proxies of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} partially address observational manipulation; they do not replace adversarial evaluation, which remains future work.

\dissertationSubheading[sec:regulatory-ethics]{Regulatory and ethical considerations}

On-chain reputation scores raise questions of both transparency and fairness. Legal analyses of DeFi emphasise that decentralised markets remain subject to disclosure and consumer-protection concerns even when settlement is on-chain \cend{63}. Packin and Lev-Aretz\cend{2} caution that decentralised credit scoring can reproduce black-box harms if models are opaque, unappealable or coupled to identity systems without due process. EndorseRank and AWP are fully determined by public ledger events and published algorithms; any party can recompute the scores from the open pipeline (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-eval}). That auditability is a partial response to black-box concerns, but it does not resolve distributional effects: wallets with limited approval or transfer history receive low scores regardless of off-chain creditworthiness.

Ethically, scores must not be presented as identity, as creditworthiness in the consumer-lending sense or as proof of unique personhood \cend{11}. They are ordinal indicators of on-chain graph position under specified edge semantics. Deployments that gate access to financial services should disclose the graph definition, observation window and known failure modes, including Sybil exposure. Regulatory regimes that require explainability favour graph propagation over opaque machine-learning scores of the kind surveyed by Kotb \cend{14}, provided operators resist overclaiming predictive power for loan default \cend{2}.

Privacy is structurally limited on public ledgers \cend{5}: reputation computation uses data already broadcast. The ethical obligation therefore concerns what is done with the analysis: deanonymisation beyond address-level aggregation must be avoided, and scores must not be reused as persistent biometric-like identifiers. This research analyses addresses only; no off-chain identity linkage is performed (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

\dissertationSubheading[sec:threats-validity]{Threats to validity}

Interpretation of the empirical claims should account for the following threats, organised by construct, internal, external and adversarial validity.

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:threat-construct]{Construct validity}

\begin{itemize}
\item Checks are not credit labels. No under-collateralised lending default labels are used. Same-window checks are allowance and transfer local statistics; the holdout labels are future new approvals and future new transfer senders.
\item Holdout construct. Future new approvals are still endorsement events and future new senders are still flow events. They are out of the score window, but they are not an independent credit or trading construct, and both are counts of arrivals, which favours any score that tracks the current count of counterparties (Section~\ref{sub:finding-rq4}).
\item Own-construct agreement is partly mechanical. EndorseRank is a global smoothing of spender in-approve degree, and AWP of decayed transfer in-strength; each method's agreement with its own family is expected by construction, which is why the within-method contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:tau-diff} are reported and why the null result on them is treated as informative.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:threat-internal]{Internal validity}

\begin{itemize}
\item Shared observation window. All methods and proxies are computed over 1~December~2025 to 31~May~2026. Market regimes specific to that window (volatility, incentive programmes, protocol changes) may inflate or deflate alignment. Robustness checks on damping and top-token subgraphs reduce sensitivity to hyperparameters and token concentration but do not vary the calendar window.
\item Cohort selection. The matched cohort requires non-zero connectivity in both reputation subgraphs. Results may not extend to addresses absent from allowance and transfer subgraphs; the sample-definition sensitivity of Table~\ref{tab:robustness-sample-size} shows how strongly the coefficients respond when that requirement is relaxed.
\item Edge-weight measurement. Token-decimal normalisation, unit conversion and permit-versus-approve coverage can affect $w_{uv}$. Measurement error in edge weights propagates to $\mathbf{r}$ and to rank correlations \cend{23}.
\item Timing environment. Runtimes are wall-clock measurements on one machine; the standard deviations in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-runtime} bound within-session noise but not between-machine variation, which is why ratios rather than seconds are the comparative summary.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:threat-external]{External validity}

\begin{itemize}
\item Single chain and window. Evidence is limited to Arbitrum One over six months. Generalisation to other rollups, Layer-1 chains or lending-centred venues requires cross-protocol validation; DeFi venues differ in protocol design \cend{8} and in market structure \cend{1}.
\item Pool scaling versus alignment scaling. The expanded pool supports a node-count statement about runtime only (Section~\ref{sub:runtime-scaling}); same-window alignment remains anchored to the matched cohort, and the holdout to spenders. Efficiency gains should not be assumed to preserve the same $\tau$ profile at arbitrary population sizes.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubsubheading[sub:threat-adversarial]{Adversarial validity}

\begin{itemize}
\item Sybil and wash trading. Adversarial allowance or transfer patterns may manipulate rankings \cend{11}. Sybil-adjusted proxies partially address but do not eliminate this risk. The cost model of Section~\ref{sec:sybil-cost-model} is analytical; it is not an empirical red-team evaluation.
\item Strategic approval behaviour. Once EndorseRank is deployed, rational actors may grant allowances strategically to farm rank. Observational $\tau$ estimated before deployment may overstate robustness to strategic behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\dissertationSubheading[sec:limitations]{Limitations}

Results reflect one six-month window on Arbitrum One for a matched wallet cohort and a spender holdout cohort whose sizes and graph dimensions are given in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. EndorseRank's demonstrated strengths are allowance-construct alignment, rank distinctness from AWP, computational efficiency attributable to sparsity, and holdout alignment with future new approvers where AWP shows none; its demonstrated weaknesses are weaker transfer alignment and weaker longitudinal-stability alignment than AWP, an allowance lead over its own transfer alignment that did not reach significance, and a holdout coefficient below that of the raw in-approve degree it smooths.

The coupled operator was tested at three fixed values of $\lambda$ with uniform teleportation and a per-node convex mixture at the transition level. Node-dependent or learned weights, mixtures of the stationary vectors rather than of the transition rows, and other chains were not tested, so the negative answer to RQ5 is a statement about that family on this data. Token-decimal normalisation, unit conversion and permit-versus-approve coverage may affect edge weights. The expanded-pool experiment tests node count, not edge growth, because the second-tier extraction did not complete. Extended baselines (alternative PageRank variants and lending-oriented graphs) are archived for future comparison (Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix-archive}) and excluded from the primary comparison. Unsecured lending default labels are out of scope. Regulatory and ethical deployment constraints (Section~\ref{sec:regulatory-ethics}) limit how scores should be used even when alignment is favourable.

These limitations bound the claims: EndorseRank is a credible, efficient, allowance-specific social reputation method on the studied cohort and window; it is not a universal credit score, a Sybil-proof identity system or a replacement for protocol-level collateralisation \cend{9}. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} restates what was established, what it adds to knowledge, and the work that would be needed to relax these bounds.
