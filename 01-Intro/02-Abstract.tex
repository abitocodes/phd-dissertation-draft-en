\chapter*{Abstract}

\label{ch:abstract}

Decentralized finance (DeFi) protocols use on-chain wallet reputation to screen integration candidates, set risk parameters, and detect automated and malicious behavior. Earlier approaches to on-chain reputation, such as the Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP) of Do, Do and Nguyen\cend{3} on the transfer graph, rely on historical token transfers. Such scores are computationally expensive to update on a rollup that publishes millions of token transfers each month. Token transfers also quantify economic activity without capturing an explicit act of endorsement. Every EVM-compatible chain already supports an alternative endorsement mechanism, the ERC-20 allowance, through which an owner authorizes a spender to move tokens on the owner's behalf. This thesis examines whether allowances can be used to construct a wallet reputation score that users can interpret, that is cheap to compute, and that can be verified from public blockchain records.

We introduce EndorseRank, a PageRank score on the directed endorsement graph whose edges are the most recent non-zero ERC-20 allowances between wallets, and we ask whether combining the allowance and transfer layers brings any benefit. The joint operator is the coupled PageRank (C-PR), in which each wallet node's transition row is a convex combination of the two layers with weight $\lambda$; on the same set of nodes, $\lambda=1$ yields EndorseRank and $\lambda=0$ yields AWP. The control is the endorsement-seeded PageRank (S-PR), a walk on the transfer graph that is restarted from the EndorseRank distribution, used as its teleport vector. Under a quantitative, positivist research design, all methods are evaluated against a faithful implementation of AWP on the same subset of $n=5{,}521$ Arbitrum One wallets, over the period 1~December~2025 to 31~May~2026. Alignment with the corresponding transfer, allowance and Sybil stability measures is assessed with Kendall's $\tau$, and a paired wallet bootstrap (400 samples) quantifies the uncertainty of each parameter and of each pre-specified difference between parameters. In the temporal holdout, wallet scores are computed from transactions up to 28~February~2026 and tested against two outcomes that occur after the last transaction included in each score: owner--spender approvals first occurring in March--May~2026, and transfer senders first occurring in March--May~2026. For each outcome, the baseline is in-degree at the snapshot date. Both $\lambda$ and the criteria for what counts as an improvement were fixed in a versioned configuration file before the coupled scores were computed. We also measure runtime under a single timing setup (one warm-up, five timed runs) and examine sensitivity to node count on a larger pool of $N=99{,}080$ addresses.

We report four findings. First, EndorseRank solves PageRank almost 10 times faster than AWP on the same subset (0.40 seconds against 4.11 seconds), because its endorsement graph has 14,727 edges and the transfer graph 137,087; as the node count varies, the ratio remains between 8.1 and 12.2. The coupled operator walks both edge sets and is therefore as expensive as AWP. Second, the methods measure different things. AWP correlates more strongly with the transfer proxies ($\tau=0.534$ versus 0.333 for EndorseRank), and EndorseRank more strongly with the allowance proxies ($\tau=0.355$, 95\% CI $[0.322,0.384]$), on which AWP is slightly negative ($-0.030$); the two scores correlate with each other at $\tau=0.316$. EndorseRank's correlation with the allowance proxies is not statistically higher than its own correlation with the transfer proxies, so the allowance result is treated as a construct check and not as external validation. Third, on the holdout spenders ($n=1{,}335$), EndorseRank ranks the wallets that later receive new approvals at $\tau=0.479$ (95\% CI $[0.427,0.529]$), whereas AWP is near zero ($0.044$). The in-approve degree at the freeze date reaches $\tau=0.711$, and EndorseRank's margin over it is negative. AWP likewise falls below the transfer in-degree on new senders. On this cohort, neither PageRank adds out-of-window information beyond its own count. Fourth, C-PR at $\lambda=0.5$ violates the pre-registered rule on both counts: it does worse than EndorseRank on new approvals ($\Delta\tau=-0.184$, interval $[-0.213,-0.149]$) and no better than AWP on new senders ($\Delta\tau=+0.007$, interval $[-0.012,0.028]$). S-PR mirrors AWP. None of the damping, top-20-token and sample-definition checks changes this pattern.

The present comparison is the first same-cohort evaluation of an allowance-based and a transfer-based PageRank that includes confidence intervals, difference tests, a two-label temporal holdout with degree baselines, a pre-registered test of a coupled operator and a versioned pipeline with committed summaries. An allowance-based reputation score can be updated at about one tenth of the cost of the transfer-graph baseline, and it captures a trust action that the baseline does not. Its ability to predict future endorsement, however, is contained in the spender's approval count. Walking both edge types in a single chain interpolates between the single-layer scores without surpassing either. For practitioners, the implication is that the allowance count is the inexpensive proxy for approval-related filtering, that EndorseRank is its propagated version, and that the transfer graph should be consulted separately when payment flows are relevant. EndorseRank does not beat AWP at identifying transfer hubs. Its Sybil resistance depends on the economic cost of creating approvals, which is modelled but not quantified here.
